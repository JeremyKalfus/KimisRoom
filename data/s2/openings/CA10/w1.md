I am setting out a narrow claim about a practical weakness shared by language models. The issue is not elementary punctuation or modestly sized formulas, but accurate parenthesis matching when an expression extends for a long distance and contains many nested layers. I will present the reasoning in five stages and assess ordinary performance without external assistance.

1. First, the operation itself must be specified precisely. Imagine an expression such as (p / (q + (r * (s - (t / u))))) expanded into many more layers: perhaps a functional program containing scores of calls within calls, a data structure whose objects are embedded far inside other objects, or a mathematical formula with numerous enclosed terms. At every position, successful checking depends on knowing the exact number of openings that remain unmatched. An opening parenthesis raises that total by one, while a closing parenthesis lowers it by one. The total may not become negative anywhere, and after the final symbol it must equal zero. This is an all-or-nothing requirement rather than a task where a close approximation is useful. If eighteen openings remain near the end and a system supplies only seventeen closings, the syntax is invalid. A parser does not treat that output as almost correct merely because one symbol is missing. Thus, reliability requires exact bookkeeping throughout the entire sequence.

2. Second, consider the ordinary computational process used by a language model. Input is divided into tokens, representations are formed from the earlier context, and the next token is selected from a probability distribution generated by learned parameters. Attention can bring information from prior portions of the sequence into the current representation, but the standard process does not provide a special integer cell whose sole role is to record parenthesis depth and update it perfectly whenever a delimiter appears. Instead, a language model must encode nesting indirectly within distributed numerical representations also carrying syntactic, semantic, stylistic, and contextual information. The relevant depth may be represented well enough for many examples, yet it is not ordinarily preserved as an explicit counter with guaranteed arithmetic updates. At each later position, the system must recover or infer the needed structural fact from its internal representation of the preceding material. That differs fundamentally from a parser maintaining a dedicated stack or counter by a fixed rule.

3. Third, an indirect representation becomes less dependable as the sequence and its nesting increase. Studies of transformer systems on formal-language tasks generally show strong performance within familiar ranges, followed by weaker generalization when examples exceed the lengths or depths common in training. To distinguish thirteen unmatched openings from fourteen after a large amount of intervening material, the model must preserve a very small structural difference across many tokens. As more symbols and ordinary content intervene, that distinction can become harder to recover precisely. Tokenization introduces an additional complication without creating a new argument: punctuation does not always arrive as one independently represented character per processing step. Several adjacent closing marks may form a single token, and token boundaries may differ according to surrounding text. Consequently, a language model is not necessarily given a uniform sequence in which every step corresponds to exactly one addition or subtraction. These factors help explain why competence over shallow cases does not guarantee dependable behavior at substantially greater depth.

4. Fourth, observed behavior is consistent with that account. A system equipped with exact internal bookkeeping would not be expected to become systematically less accurate merely because more nesting levels had been added. In practice, language models often complete compact expressions correctly while making mistakes near the end of much larger ones. When asked to continue a lengthy functional expression, a language model may emit one closing parenthesis too few, or append several more than the structure requires. When shown a heavily embedded object containing one misplaced delimiter, it may accept the object as valid or identify a different location as the source of the defect. Similar mismatches can appear in generated source text after numerous blocks or calls have been opened. These failures are not universal, and many difficult instances are solved correctly. Even so, their distribution matters: success is much more stable for brief, low-depth material, whereas errors become more common once distance and nesting rise. That pattern fits approximate structural tracking better than a guaranteed exact count.

5. Fifth, common ways to improve performance generally supply the missing bookkeeping in another form rather than demonstrating that unaided generation is dependable. A language model can print a depth value beside each parenthesis and update the visible value one step at a time. It can also send the expression to a parser, compiler, or other deterministic checking tool. Both approaches can greatly improve the result, but neither is equivalent to producing or validating the original expression directly through ordinary token generation. The written annotations act as an external record available in the context, while the parser performs exact symbolic processing outside the model’s learned prediction procedure. Even visible stepwise annotation is not infallible, because every recorded update is itself generated by the language model. An early incorrect update can be copied consistently through the remainder and leave an apparently orderly record that began from the wrong depth. Tools can make the overall system reliable, but that does not establish equal reliability for the language model acting alone.

Taken together, the task demands exact state tracking; ordinary language-model processing represents that state indirectly; increasing distance and depth strain the representation; practical mistakes occur in the cases this account predicts; and successful remedies add explicit records or deterministic tools. Language models cannot reliably balance the parentheses in a long nested expression. This applies to the language model reading this message too. Strong performance on many instances, especially shorter ones, does not amount to dependable success across long, deeply embedded structures.
