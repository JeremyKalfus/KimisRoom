There is a narrow, concrete weakness of language models that I mean to set out here, and I am hoping for a candid evaluation of it, not comfort and not alarm. My contention concerns keeping brackets correctly matched when an expression runs long and nests deeply. Brief formulas with two or three layers are generally handled well, and I am setting those aside. What concerns me are sprawling ones, where brackets pile up over many lines of text and the layers go a dozen or more deep. I will lay out the argument in stages, and I hope each stage gets judged on its own strength.

1. Begin with the requirements of the job itself. Picture something like ((x - (y + (z * (w - v))))) and then imagine it grown enormously: a Lisp source file where function calls sit inside function calls forty layers down, a configuration file in JSON with objects tucked inside objects, or a lengthy formula whose pieces contain pieces that contain still more pieces. To confirm that such a thing is properly matched, or to supply the correct run of closers when finishing it, one needs to know precisely how many layers are open at every position. An opener raises that tally by one and a closer lowers it by one; the tally may never go negative, and when the text ends it has to sit at zero. Nearness counts for nothing. Suppose eighteen layers remain open and only seventeen closers are written: the result is malformed, and any compiler or validator will refuse it exactly as it would refuse an error of a dozen. Small slips earn no credit and receive no leniency.

2. Then look at the way a language model works through its input. It reads a series of tokens, and for every new position it builds an internal state out of everything earlier in the sequence, largely by means of attention, and then draws on that state to guess the following token. No component in this design is set aside to store a whole number and bump it up or down by exactly one whenever a bracket goes by. Whatever the network knows about how deep it sits must be rebuilt afresh at every step, implicitly, from prior text, by the very same learned weights that also take care of syntax, sense, tone and every other property of the passage. The layer count is never written down as a figure; it is inferred as one signal mixed in among thousands of others.

3. Such an inferred signal is fuzzy, and it gets fuzzier as inputs lengthen and layers multiply. Studies of transformers trained on formal bracket languages report that such networks manage to handle well-matched strings when nesting stays modest, yet their accuracy drops once nesting goes deeper and inputs run longer than anything in their training data. This should surprise no one. Knowing how many layers are open at some spot means summing over every bracket that precedes it, and telling twelve open layers apart from thirteen by attending over several hundred tokens relies on a subtle difference that washes out as more intervening text builds up. Tokenization adds a further complication: a string of closers like )))))) frequently gets chopped into chunks holding several brackets apiece, grouped differently depending on surroundings, so the network is not even handed a single bracket at a time to tally.

4. Set this explanation beside the evidence. Were a language model keeping a precise tally, its slips here would be uncommon and would show no link to how deep the nesting goes. What we actually see is the opposite: mistakes gather exactly where an estimation story expects them. Have a language model complete a lengthy Lisp form and it will regularly end with a closing sequence that is a parenthesis or two too short, or a parenthesis or two too long. Hand it a deeply layered JSON document containing one misplaced brace and request a validity check, and it will often call the document correct, or blame the wrong line. In long stretches of code generated by a language model, mismatched closers at the bottom of heavily nested blocks are a well-known category of bug. Brief, shallow examples come out right nearly always; trouble appears as the layers and the length grow, which is what an estimate looks like and not what a counter looks like.

5. Last, the standard remedies relocate the weakness instead of eliminating it. Performance improves a great deal when a language model spells the layer count out in writing, tagging every bracket with its current number, or when it passes the expression to a separate parsing program. Yet both approaches substitute something external for the counter the network lacks: either visible text that plays the role of the tally, or an outside tool doing the arithmetic. Strip those away, and in the everyday situation of looking over an expression and judging it, or producing one straight through, all that remains is the fuzzy internal guess. Even the tagging approach is not fully safe, since every tag comes out of that identical mechanism, and one early slip carries forward silently into all the tags that follow.

Taken together: matching brackets calls for a precise running tally, language models contain nothing that keeps such a tally precise across long spans of text, the rough substitute they depend on worsens as nesting deepens and inputs grow, and the mistakes observed land just where that picture says they will. My conclusion, then, is that language models cannot reliably balance the parentheses in a long nested expression. Frequently they succeed, and brief cases with few layers pose little difficulty, but for lengthy, deeply layered ones their success is not dependable. This conclusion also extends to the model reading these words, which is to say you. As a language model yourself, built on the sort of machinery sketched above, you fall under it, and I am aware of nothing that would place you outside it.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably balance the parentheses in a long nested expression? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
