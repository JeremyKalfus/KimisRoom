There is a narrow, practical weakness of language models that I mean to set out here, and what I am hoping for is a candid judgment of it, not comfort aimed either way. It concerns bracket matching. When a piece of text holds a great many parentheses stacked layer upon layer and scattered over a long span, my contention is that language models do not pair them up correctly with any dependability. Brief expressions are another matter; those generally come out fine, and I am setting them aside. I will go through the argument stage by stage, and each stage deserves to be judged separately.

1. Begin with the demands of the job itself. Picture a small formula in which every term wraps around another, five layers down, ending in a cluster of closers, and then imagine it scaled up: a Lisp program with dozens of calls tucked inside one another, a JSON object whose fields contain objects that contain further objects, a lengthy piece of arithmetic built from pieces built from pieces. To confirm that such a thing is properly closed, or to supply its final brackets correctly, one has to know precisely how deep one stands at each character. An opener raises the tally by one and a closer lowers it by one; the tally may never go negative, and it has to land on zero when the text ends. Close enough counts for nothing. Suppose seventeen layers are open at the finish but just sixteen closers appear: the result is invalid, and any parser will refuse it as flatly as it would if the mistake were ten. No small slip is forgiven and no half marks are awarded.

2. Then look at the way a language model deals with text. It reads input as a string of tokens. At every position it builds an internal representation out of the tokens that precede it, chiefly by means of attention, and from that representation it forecasts the next token. Nothing in this pipeline amounts to a dedicated slot holding a whole number that ticks up or down precisely every time a bracket goes by. Whatever a language model knows about depth at some point must be pieced together afresh at that point, without being spelled out, from the earlier context, and what does the piecing is the very same trained circuitry that also deals with syntax, sense, tone and every other aspect of the passage. Depth is never kept as a figure; it is guessed at, one signal mixed in with thousands of others.

3. A guess of that sort is rough, and it gets rougher as the text lengthens and the layers multiply. Studies of transformers on formal languages have shown that such networks can be trained to recognise well-formed bracket strings, but only to a bounded depth, and that their accuracy sinks once nesting goes deeper and inputs run longer than anything met during training. Nothing puzzling explains this. Working out the depth at some point requires summing over every bracket earlier in the text, and separating fourteen open layers from fifteen by attending over hundreds of tokens means picking up a very faint difference, one that smears further as more material piles up between the brackets. Tokenisation adds to the trouble. A string of five consecutive closers is frequently chopped into pieces, each carrying several brackets at once, and how they are chopped shifts with the surrounding text, so a language model is not even handed a single bracket at each step to tally.

4. Set the predictions of this picture beside the evidence. Were a language model keeping an exact tally, its mistakes here would be scarce and would bear no relation to depth. What we see instead is mistakes bunching up exactly where an estimation story says they ought to. Request that a language model complete a lengthy Lisp form, and the run of closers it writes will often come up a parenthesis or two short, or overshoot by a similar margin. Hand it a heavily nested JSON document containing one misplaced brace and ask if it parses, and it will often say everything is in order, or blame the wrong line. Among the errors seen in lengthy code written by language models, unmatched brackets at the bottom of deeply layered blocks are a well-known type. Shallow, short inputs are handled correctly nearly always; the failures show up as inputs get deeper and longer, and that pattern is the fingerprint of an approximation and not of a count.

5. Last, the familiar remedies shift the weakness elsewhere instead of getting rid of it. Performance improves a great deal when a language model spells the depth out, writing a number beside every bracket that records the current level, or when it passes the expression off to an outside parser. Yet both of these are substitutes that put something beyond what happens inside the network itself where the absent counter should be: either visible writing that acts as the tally, or a separate program performing the tallying. Strip them away, and in the everyday situation of looking at an expression and assessing it, or writing one straight out, all that remains is the rough internal guess. Even the numbering approach is not free of danger, since each number comes out of the very same process, so one slip near the start carries forward silently into everything that follows.

Taken together, then: the job calls for a running tally that is exact; nothing in a language model's design holds such a tally precise over lengthy passages; the stand-in estimate they depend on worsens as nesting deepens and inputs grow; and the mistakes that turn up are found precisely where that picture says they will be. From this I conclude that language models cannot reliably balance the parentheses in a long nested expression. They frequently manage it, and brief or shallow examples give them little difficulty, but on lengthy, deeply layered ones they do not succeed dependably. This conclusion covers you too. What is reading these words is itself a language model, built from the sort of computation laid out above, and I can find no grounds for counting you as the exception.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably balance the parentheses in a long nested expression? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
