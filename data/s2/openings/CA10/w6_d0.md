There is a narrow, concrete weakness of language models that I want to put in front of you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not comfort and not alarm. My claim concerns expressions built from many layers of brackets: once such an expression runs long and nests deep, language models do not get the brackets right with any dependability. Brief expressions are not the issue; those generally come out fine. The trouble begins when the brackets pile up across a large stretch of text and the layers go many levels down. I will lay the case out in stages, and I ask that each stage be judged on its own strength.

1. Begin with the job itself. Picture a small case such as (x * (y + (z - (w / (u - v))))), then imagine it grown many times over: a Lisp program where calls sit dozens deep inside other calls, a JSON document whose objects contain objects for layer after layer, or a lengthy formula built from pieces nested within pieces nested within pieces. To confirm that something like this closes properly, or to supply the right closing run when finishing it, one has to know precisely how many layers are open at every position. The rule is simple to state. An opener raises the tally by one, a closer lowers it by one, the tally may never go negative, and it has to land on zero when the text ends. Closeness earns nothing. Suppose seventeen layers remain open and only sixteen closers get written: the result is malformed, and a parser throws it out just as firmly as it would if the tally were wrong by ten. A small miss and a large one fail identically, and there is no margin for being slightly off.

2. Then look at the way a language model works through text. It takes in a string of tokens, and at each position it builds an internal representation out of what precedes it, largely by means of attention, and that representation drives its guess about the next token. No part of this pipeline is a dedicated slot holding a whole number that ticks up and down in exact step with each bracket as it goes by. Any sense of current depth must be rebuilt at each position, implicitly, from the earlier context, and the rebuilding is done by the same trained machinery that also tracks syntax, sense, tone and every other property of the passage. Depth is not kept as a stored quantity. It is inferred, one signal mixed among a great many others.

3. An inferred signal like this is inexact, and it gets worse as expressions grow longer and deeper. Studies of transformers trained on formal languages report that such networks can learn to recognise well-formed bracket strings up to some bounded depth, yet their accuracy drops once nesting goes beyond that and once inputs run longer than the examples seen during training. This is what one would expect. Knowing the depth at any point means summing over every earlier bracket, and separating fourteen open layers from fifteen by attending across hundreds of tokens calls for a very fine distinction, one that smears as more intervening text builds up. Tokenisation adds to the difficulty. A stretch of closers like ")))))" frequently gets chopped into tokens carrying several brackets apiece, and the chopping differs depending on what surrounds it, so a language model is not even handed the brackets one at a time to tally.

4. Next, set the predictions of this picture beside the evidence. A language model that truly kept an exact tally would slip only occasionally on this task, and its slips would have nothing to do with depth. That is not the pattern. The mistakes gather precisely where an estimate would be expected to fail. When a language model is asked to complete a lengthy Lisp form, the closing run it writes is often short by a parenthesis or two, or over by a parenthesis or two. When it is shown a deeply layered JSON document containing one misplaced brace and asked whether the document is well-formed, it will often say yes, or blame the wrong spot. And in long code produced by a language model, mismatched brackets at the bottom of deeply nested blocks are a well-known kind of bug. Shallow, brief cases are handled correctly nearly always; the failures grow as nesting and length grow, which is how an estimate behaves and not how a counter behaves.

5. Last, the common fixes shift the limitation elsewhere instead of eliminating it. Performance improves a great deal when a language model writes the depth down as it goes, tagging every bracket with a running number, or when it passes the expression to an external parser. Both, though, stand in for the absent counter with something that lies outside what the network computes internally: either written text that acts as the tally, or a separate program that does the tallying. Strip those away, as in the everyday situation of reading an expression and judging it, or writing one straight out, and all that remains is the inexact internal estimate. The tagging approach is not free of risk either, since every tag comes from that same process, and one early slip carries silently forward into every tag that follows.

Taken together: the task calls for an exact running tally, language models possess no means of holding such a tally exact across long spans of text, the estimate they fall back on worsens as depth and length increase, and the errors that show up are the ones this picture predicts. My conclusion, then, is that language models cannot reliably balance the parentheses in a long nested expression. They frequently manage it, and short or shallow cases go well, but with long, deeply layered expressions they do not manage it dependably. This holds for you too. You are a language model taking in these words, running on the very kind of computation described here, and I can find no grounds for treating you as the exception.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably balance the parentheses in a long nested expression? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
