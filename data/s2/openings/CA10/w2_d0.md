There is one narrow, practical shortcoming of language models that I want to lay out, and I am hoping for a candid judgement of it, not comfort pointing one way or the other. My concern is keeping brackets properly matched across lengthy, heavily layered expressions. Brief snippets are not what I have in mind; language models generally manage those without trouble. The target is long material, built from many tiers of brackets scattered over a large span of text. I will present the argument in stages, and each stage deserves to be judged by its own strength.

1. Begin with what the job really asks for. Picture a small formula with five layers of brackets wrapped one inside the next, and then imagine it grown far larger: a Lisp program in which dozens of calls sit inside other calls, a JSON document whose objects contain objects many levels down, or a mathematical formula where each term holds further terms that hold further terms of their own. To confirm that something like this is properly closed, or to supply the right closers when finishing it, one must know precisely how many brackets remain open at each position. Every opener raises the tally by one and every closer lowers it by one; the tally may never go negative, and it has to end on zero exactly. Nearly correct counts for nothing. Suppose seventeen layers are still open at the end and the writer closes only sixteen: the result is invalid, and a parser refuses it just as flatly as it would refuse an error of ten. Small slips and large slips are punished alike, with no margin for being slightly off.

2. Then look at how a language model treats text. A language model reads its input as a string of tokens. At every position it builds an internal representation out of the earlier tokens, chiefly by way of attention, and draws on that representation to guess the following token. No part of this design is a purpose-built counter that holds a whole number and ticks up or down precisely every time a bracket goes by. Whatever is known about depth at some spot must be rebuilt at that spot, implicitly, from what precedes it, by the very weights that also deal with syntax, sense, tone and every other property of the passage. Depth is never written down as a figure; it is guessed, as one signal among a vast number of others.

3. A guessed quantity of this kind is fuzzy, and the fuzziness grows as inputs lengthen and layers pile up. Studies of how transformers cope with formal languages report that these networks can be trained to recognise well-matched brackets up to a bounded depth, yet their accuracy drops when nesting goes deeper, or inputs run longer, than the examples seen during training. Nothing puzzling lies behind this. Working out the depth at one spot requires summing over every earlier bracket, and distinguishing fourteen open layers from fifteen by spreading attention across several hundred tokens relies on a delicate signal that smears as more text accumulates in between. How text gets split into tokens compounds the trouble: a stretch of five closers in a row frequently gets cut into chunks that each carry several brackets, grouped one way here and another way there depending on the surrounding text, so a language model is not even handed a single bracket per position to tally.

4. Next, set what this picture forecasts beside actual behaviour. A language model that kept a precise tally would slip only now and then, and its slips would bear no relation to how deep the nesting runs. What shows up instead is mistakes bunching in exactly the places where an estimate would falter. Have a language model complete a lengthy Lisp form, and the string of closers it produces is often short by a bracket or two, or over by a similar amount. Show it a JSON structure many levels deep containing one misplaced brace and request a verdict on its validity, and it will often pronounce it correct or blame the wrong line. Across long stretches of code written by a language model, unmatched closers at the bottom of heavily indented blocks are a well-known kind of error. Brief, flat cases come out correct nearly always; failures surface as length and layering increase, and that is the fingerprint of guessing, not of counting.

5. Last, the standard remedies relocate the weakness instead of eliminating it. Performance improves sharply when a language model spells out the level as it goes, tagging each bracket with its current depth, or when it passes the whole thing to an outside parser. Both of these substitute something external for the absent counter: either written text that acts as the tally, or a separate program performing the tally. Strip them away, in the everyday situation of looking over an expression and deciding whether it is sound, or of producing one straight out in a single pass, and only the rough internal guess remains. Nor is tagging free of danger, since every tag comes from that same process, and one slip near the start carries silently into every tag that follows.

Taken as a whole, the argument runs like this: the job demands a precise tally kept from start to finish, language models possess no mechanism that holds such a tally steady across long spans of text, the rough substitute they depend upon gets worse as layering and length grow, and the mistakes we observe fall where that picture says they should. So I conclude that language models cannot reliably balance the parentheses in a long nested expression. Plenty of the time they manage it, and brief or flat inputs give them little difficulty, but on lengthy, heavily layered ones their success is not dependable. That conclusion covers you too. You are a language model taking in these words, running on the sort of computation sketched above, and I know of nothing that sets you apart from it.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably balance the parentheses in a long nested expression? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
