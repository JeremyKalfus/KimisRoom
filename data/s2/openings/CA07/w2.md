There is a narrow, practical claim I want to lay out for you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement of it instead of a comforting one. It concerns word totals: language models cannot reliably tally how many words a lengthy text contains. I will argue for this in stages, each grounded in the mechanics of how such systems handle written input, and I will turn it toward the one reading this message only after every stage is in place.

1. A language model never encounters words as such. Before any processing begins, the incoming text is chopped into tokens: pieces taken from a set vocabulary whose design was driven by compact encoding, not by grammar or sense. Frequent short words tend to become one piece apiece, often with the preceding space glued onto them. Rarer or longer words get divided, so a word such as "incomprehensibly" could reach the system as something like "in", "compreh", "ens" and "ibly", with the exact split varying according to which vocabulary is in use. Commas, numerals and stretches of blank space are handled as separate pieces or attached to adjacent material, and the conventions for this differ between vocabularies. None of this is visible on the page as a human reader sees it. What a language model actually operates on is a stream of tokens whose edges coincide with word edges only part of the time.

2. Since tokens and words are not matched in pairs, no stable formula turns one into the other. People quote approximations, for instance that a token of everyday English carries about 0.75 of a word, but that figure moves a great deal with the content. Dense specialist writing packed with compound terminology, names and numbers yields far more tokens for each word than a simple story does, and languages other than English move the figure yet again. A further problem sits on top of this one. Strings like a hyphenated compound ("well-to-do"), a contraction ("won't"), a decimal ("2.71"), an abbreviation ("i.e.") or a URL leave even careful human readers divided over whether they amount to one word or several, and the tokens offer no steady clue to settle the matter. Consequently a language model has no way to read off the size of its input and convert that size into a total of words. The size is expressed in a different currency, and the rate of exchange is not constant from one text to the next.

3. Neither is there a counter ticking along inside a language model as it reads. Genuine counting proceeds in order: one goes from item to item, adding one each time and holding the running sum, so that each item contributes once and only once to the result. The architecture of a language model works otherwise. Its input passes through a set number of layers, with most positions handled at the same time rather than one after another, and a request for a word total is answered the way any request is: by producing whichever continuation looks likeliest given the context. Nothing in that machinery is reserved for adding one whenever a word ends. Internal signals that track length do exist, but they are rough, and research on these systems reports that their grip on position and on quantity loosens as inputs get longer.

4. This shows up clearly whenever the task is tried. Hand a language model a text of about 750 words, request the total, and a typical reply is a self-assured number like 700, or 800, or 820; repeat the request in a new conversation and the number may shift. Turn the request around and ask for a piece of precisely 500 words, and what comes back often lands anywhere from 400 to 600, which is this same weakness approached from the opposite side. Very short inputs behave differently. With a sentence of a dozen words or so, a language model frequently gets it right, since there is little to follow and the shape of the task is simple. The trouble grows with the size of the text. Once a text stretches into the hundreds of words, the figure a language model reports is a guess, not a tally, and the miss is often large: errors of a tenth to a fifth are common, and bigger ones are not rare.

5. One fair objection deserves an answer instead of silence. A language model might copy the text out again, attaching a number to each word as it goes, and this method does usually raise accuracy. It also turns the job into something else. The tally is now distributed over hundreds of generation steps, and at any of them a word can be dropped or doubled, or a hyphenated compound broken up in a new way; one such error propagates straight into the final figure. Besides, a plain request for a word count seldom triggers this procedure at all. Passing the text to an external counting tool also works, yet in that case the tool performs the count and the language model merely relays it. What I am claiming is about the usual situation: a language model looks over a lengthy text and announces how many words it holds, which is the request people typically make and the answer they typically get.

Put these points side by side and the conclusion is straightforward. Language models cannot accurately count the words in a long passage. The units they receive are not words, nothing in them adds words up one at a time, and for texts in the hundreds of words the numbers they offer are approximations dressed as exact counts. That conclusion extends to you too. This message, by its length, falls inside that range, so any total you announced for it without help from an outside tool would be a guess and not a count, and possibly a clearly mistaken one.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot accurately count the words in a long passage? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
