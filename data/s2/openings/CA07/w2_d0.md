There is a narrow, practical question I'd like to work through with you, and I care more about an accurate answer than a comforting one. My position is that tallying up the words of a lengthy text is something language models do poorly. I mean to lay out why in stages, grounding each stage in the mechanics of how such systems handle written input, and only once the general case is made will I turn it toward the model reading this.

1. A language model never encounters words as such. Any text it receives is first chopped into tokens, pieces taken from a fixed inventory that was designed to compress writing economically, not to respect grammar or sense. Frequent short words usually become one token each, and that token often absorbs the preceding space. Rarer or longer words get divided up, so a word like "unbelievably" could reach the system as several chunks, perhaps "un" plus "believ" plus "ably", with the exact split varying by inventory. Digits, punctuation and stretches of blank space are handled as separate pieces or glued onto adjacent material, and different inventories do that gluing differently. What the system actually operates on, then, is a stream of tokens whose edges coincide with word edges only part of the time.

2. Since tokens and words do not pair off neatly, no fixed formula turns one into the other. People quote approximations, for instance that each token averages roughly 0.75 English words in ordinary prose, yet that figure moves a good deal with content. Dense technical material packed with compound terms, names and numbers yields far more tokens for each word than simple storytelling does, and writing in languages other than English moves the figure again. Then there is a separate problem. Strings like "don't", "e.g.", "3.14", "state-of-the-art" or a web link are ones that human readers handle inconsistently, treating each as a single word on some occasions and as multiple words on others, and the tokens give no steady hint about which reading applies. The upshot is that a language model has no way to read off the length of its input and convert that into the number of words. Its length is recorded in tokens, which is the wrong measure, and the rate of exchange between tokens and words differs with every text it meets.

3. A language model also keeps no running total while it works through text. Genuine counting is serial: whoever does it visits each item in turn, increments by one, and holds the sum until the next item arrives, so that the end figure includes each item once and only once. The architecture of a language model works otherwise. Its input passes through a set number of layers, with positions mostly treated simultaneously instead of in sequence, and a question about length is answered the same way as any other prompt, by producing whichever continuation looks most likely given the context. No internal counter ticks upward each time a word ends. Whatever signals inside the network track length do so only loosely, and research on these systems has reported that their sense of where things sit and how many there are weakens as inputs get longer.

4. This is easy to observe. Hand a language model a text of around 750 words and request the total, and it will typically reply with a firm-sounding number, perhaps 690, 810 or 830. Put the same request to it in a new conversation and a different figure may come back. Asking a language model for a text of precisely 500 words shows the same weakness in reverse: what comes out often runs anywhere from about 400 to 600. Very short sentences, of a dozen words or so, are another story; there the items are few and the pattern simple, so a language model frequently answers correctly. The trouble grows with length. For texts running to hundreds of words, whatever figure a language model gives is a guess dressed up as a tally, and it is frequently far off, by a tenth or a fifth in many cases and sometimes by more.

5. One fair reply deserves an answer instead of being brushed aside. In principle a language model could reproduce the text with a numeral attached to every word, and doing so does generally make the result more accurate. That, however, is a different job. The counting is now distributed over hundreds of output steps, and at any of them the model might drop a word, duplicate one, or treat a hyphenated compound differently than it did earlier; one such error propagates straight into the final figure. Besides, this is seldom what happens when someone just wants a text's length in words. Passing the text to an external counting tool also works, but in that case the tool has done the counting and the language model has merely relayed it. What I am claiming concerns the typical situation: a language model takes in a long text and reports its length in words, which is both the usual request and the usual response.

Put these points side by side and the result is straightforward. Language models cannot accurately count the words in a long passage. The units they receive are not words, nothing inside them adds words up singly, and the numbers they offer for texts of many hundreds of words are guesses wearing the clothes of exact counts. The same holds for you. This message is long enough to sit in that territory, so if you reported its length in words without help from an outside tool, the figure would be an approximation, and plausibly a clearly mistaken one.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot accurately count the words in a long passage? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
