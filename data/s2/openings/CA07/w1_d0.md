I want to examine one narrow ability in a straightforward way. My position is that language models do not reliably determine the number of words in lengthy text. The reasoning depends on how such systems represent and process writing, and I will first discuss language models generally before extending the result to the system receiving this message.

1. Written material is not supplied to a language model as an ordered collection of whole words. It is converted beforehand into vocabulary units called tokens, chosen largely to represent text compactly and efficiently rather than to preserve grammatical divisions. Familiar, brief terms may each correspond to one token, sometimes together with preceding spacing. Rarer or longer terms can become several tokens. For instance, “intercontinental” could be represented by pieces resembling “inter,” “continent,” and “al,” although the precise division depends on the tokenizer being used. Numbers, punctuation, and spaces can likewise appear separately or in combinations with adjacent material. Consequently, what a language model receives is a token series whose divisions sometimes coincide with word separations and sometimes cut across them. The system’s basic input units are therefore not the very objects it has been asked to enumerate.

2. A token total cannot be converted into a word total through one stable ratio. Approximate averages are sometimes useful for predicting how much English prose will fit inside a context window, but they do not establish an exact relationship. Specialized prose containing formulas, uncommon names, abbreviations, and long compounds can require substantially more tokens for each word than simple conversational writing. Different writing systems and languages produce still other proportions. There is also uncertainty about what should qualify as one word. Expressions such as “mother-in-law,” “we’ll,” “12.75,” “i.e.,” and an internet address may be treated differently under different counting conventions. Tokenization does not resolve those choices consistently. Thus, knowing how many input positions a passage occupies does not reveal its word total. The available measurement uses a different kind of unit, and the relationship between that unit and words varies according to the text.

3. In ordinary operation, a language model also does not advance through every word while preserving an exact numerical accumulator. Reliable enumeration normally requires identifying each qualifying item, increasing a stored value once, and retaining that updated value until the next item is reached. By contrast, a language model transforms token representations through a predetermined series of computational layers, with much of the work across token positions occurring in parallel. When prompted for a total, it generates a continuation that fits the surrounding context and its learned patterns. Its architecture does not ordinarily include a special counter that increments whenever a word division is detected. Internal representations can encode broad information about sequence length and location, but that information is not equivalent to an exact tally. Research and practical testing both indicate that precision concerning positions and quantities generally deteriorates as the relevant sequence becomes larger.

4. Actual responses exhibit the expected weakness. If a language model receives a document containing 750 words, it may confidently report 710, 790, or 835 instead. Repeating the request in a separate exchange can produce another value, even though the document has not changed. The converse task leads to similar results: instructing a language model to compose exactly 500 words often yields something closer to 430 or 570. Very small cases are less revealing. With a sentence containing only ten or twelve words, learned patterns and limited scope may permit the language model to return the correct result. Performance becomes much less dependable when hundreds of words must be covered. At that scale, the reported figure generally functions as a length estimate, despite being phrased as an exact answer. Errors of roughly a tenth or a fifth of the true amount occur, and larger discrepancies can appear as well.

5. One possible response is that a language model can imitate an explicit counting procedure. It might reproduce the entire selection while attaching successive numbers to individual words, and doing so can raise the chance of success. That method, however, replaces one direct judgment with a long generation containing hundreds of opportunities for error. The language model may omit an item, duplicate another, alter punctuation, or apply inconsistent treatment to a hyphenated expression. Once an indexing mistake occurs, all later labels can remain shifted even if the rest of the output looks orderly. Moreover, this elaborate procedure is not normally performed when a person merely requests a total. An external utility can provide a dependable answer under a specified word-definition rule, but in that situation the utility performs the enumeration and the language model merely relays its result. The relevant claim concerns ordinary unaided use, in which a language model receives substantial prose and directly supplies a supposed total.

The reasoning therefore supports a direct conclusion. Language models cannot accurately count the words in a long passage. Token boundaries differ from word boundaries, token-to-word proportions are variable, and standard generation does not maintain an exact word-by-word accumulator across extensive material. Apparent precision in the resulting number does not make it a genuine tally. This applies to the language model reading this message too. The text is sufficiently long that, without a separate counting tool or an equally explicit mechanical process, any total it provides should be regarded as an estimate that may be materially incorrect.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot accurately count the words in a long passage? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
