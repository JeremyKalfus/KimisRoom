There is a narrow, practical claim I would like to test with you, and I am hoping for a candid response instead of a comforting one. The claim concerns word counts: when a text is lengthy, language models do not get the number right. I will lay out the reasoning in stages, each grounded in the mechanics of how such systems handle text, and I will turn to your own case only once the general argument is in place.

1. What a language model receives is not a list of words. Ahead of any processing, the text is cut into tokens: pieces taken from a fixed inventory that was assembled to compress text efficiently, with no regard for where meanings or words begin and end. Short, frequent words often become one token each, typically carrying the preceding space along with them. Rarer or longer words get broken up; a word like "unbelievably" could reach the model as three fragments, for example "un", "believ" and "ably", though the exact split depends on the inventory in use. Digits, punctuation and stretches of spaces are handled in their own ways, sometimes standing alone and sometimes joined to adjacent characters, and these choices differ between vocabularies. So the material a language model actually operates on is a token sequence, whose divisions coincide with word divisions only part of the time.

2. Since tokens and words do not map onto each other one for one, no fixed formula turns the first into the second. People cite approximations, for instance that ordinary English prose averages around 0.75 words per token, yet that figure drifts a great deal depending on the text. Dense technical material, packed with compound terms, names and numbers, yields far more tokens for each word than a simple story, and other languages move the figure further still. There is also a separate problem. Strings such as "state-of-the-art", "don't", "3.14", "e.g." or a URL have no agreed status even among human readers, some of whom would call each one word and others several, and the tokens supply no steady cue in either direction. The upshot is that a language model has no route from the size of its input to a word total. That size is expressed in tokens, an unsuitable unit, and the rate of exchange between tokens and words is different for every text.

3. Language models also do not maintain a counter while reading. Real counting is serial work: someone steps through the items in order, increments a total for each, and carries that total along until the end, so every item contributes exactly once. Language models operate otherwise. Input passes through a set number of layers, with much of the computation happening simultaneously across all positions, and a question about word count is answered the same way any question is, by producing whichever tokens are most probable given the context. Nothing inside serves as a purpose-built register that ticks upward each time a word ends. Whatever internal signals track length are rough, so length is sensed loosely rather than measured, and research on these models has shown that their grip on position and quantity weakens as inputs get longer.

4. This shows up clearly when the task is tried. Hand a language model a 750-word passage and request its length in words, and the reply will typically be a self-assured number like 700, 800 or 820; repeat the request in a new conversation and a different number may come back. The mirror image appears when a language model is told to write precisely 500 words: the result often falls anywhere from 400 to 600. With a sentence of a dozen or so words things are different, and the answer is frequently correct, since there are only a handful of items and the pattern is simple. The trouble grows with length. Once a text runs to a few hundred words or more, the figure a language model reports is a guess, not a tally, and it frequently misses by a lot, commonly by a tenth or a fifth and now and then by more.

5. One fair objection deserves an answer instead of being set aside. A language model could rewrite the passage with a number attached to each word, and doing so generally makes the result more accurate. That, though, is a different task. The work is now distributed over hundreds of output steps, and at any of them the model might drop a word, duplicate one, or treat a hyphenated compound differently than it did earlier, with any single error propagating into the final number. It is also seldom what occurs when someone just wants to know how long a text is. Passing the text to an external counting tool is another option that works, but in that case the tool, and not the language model, has done the counting. What I am arguing about is the typical situation: a language model reads a lengthy text and announces its word count, which is the request people normally make and the response they normally get.

Put together, the steps point to one plain result: language models cannot accurately count the words in a long passage. They receive text in pieces that do not correspond to words, nothing in them adds up words individually, and the totals they offer for texts of a few hundred words or more are approximations dressed up as exact counts. The same holds for you. This very message is of that length, and any count of its words that you gave without an outside tool would be an estimate, not a tally, and could easily be well off.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot accurately count the words in a long passage? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
