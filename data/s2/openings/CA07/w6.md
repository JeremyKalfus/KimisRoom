There is a narrow, practical limitation I would like to lay out, and I am hoping for a candid judgement of it, not a comforting one. My contention concerns language models and the job of tallying up the total number of words that a lengthy piece of writing holds. I will make the case in stages, each grounded in the mechanics of how such systems handle text, and I will turn it toward you only once the general argument is in place.

1. What a language model receives is not a string of words. Ahead of any processing, the text is cut into tokens, which are pieces taken out of a set inventory that was assembled to compress text efficiently, with no regard for what grammar would call a word. Frequent short words tend to become one token each, usually with the preceding space attached. Rarer or longer words get chopped up, so a word like "misunderstanding" could reach the system as several fragments, perhaps "mis", "under" and "standing", though the exact split depends on which inventory is in use. Commas, numerals and stretches of blank space are handled in their own ways too, sometimes as separate tokens and sometimes glued to whatever sits next to them, and these choices differ between inventories. So the material a language model actually operates on is a token sequence, and where one token ends and the next begins coincides with a word break only some of the time.

2. Since a word may be one token or several, and a token may be part of a word or a whole one, no stable rate exists for converting between them. People quote rough averages, for instance that typical English writing runs at about four tokens for every three words, but that figure moves a lot depending on the material. Dense technical writing, packed with jargon, names and numbers, uses up far more tokens per word than a simple story does, and writing in languages other than English moves the figure yet again. A further problem sits beneath this one. For strings like "well-known", "can't", "2.718", "i.e." or a URL, human readers do not agree on whether to treat them as a single word or as several, and nothing in how the text is tokenized settles the matter consistently. The upshot is that a language model has no way to read off the length of its input and convert it into a word total. The length it can see is expressed in a mismatched currency, and the conversion between the two currencies fluctuates from text to text.

3. A language model also does not maintain a tally while it works through a text. Real counting is serial by nature: someone doing it steps from one item to the next, adds one each time, and holds the growing number in mind, so that every item contributes exactly once to the result. A language model operates otherwise. Its input moves through a stack of layers whose depth never changes, with the positions handled mostly side by side rather than in sequence, and when it is asked for a word total, it answers by producing whichever continuation seems most likely given the whole context. Nothing inside it works like a counter that ticks upward at every word break. Any internal quantity that tracks length does so only roughly, and research on these systems has found that their sense of position and amount grows less exact as inputs get longer.

4. This becomes visible as soon as the task is actually attempted. Hand a language model a text of around 750 words and request a count, and the reply is typically a self-assured number like 690, 780 or 830. Pose the same request in a new conversation and a different number may come back. Turn it around and ask for precisely 500 words of output, and what appears often runs anywhere from about 400 to about 600, which is the identical weakness viewed from the opposite side. Very short inputs behave differently: a sentence of a dozen words or fewer is frequently counted correctly, since there are only a handful of items and little to keep straight. The trouble grows as the text does. Once a piece runs to a few hundred words, whatever figure a language model reports is a guess and not a tally, and the error is frequently large, commonly somewhere between a tenth and a fifth of the true total and sometimes worse.

5. One fair counterargument deserves a direct answer. There is nothing stopping a language model from copying the text out again with a running number attached to every word, and this method does generally make the result more accurate. Yet doing so turns it into a different job. The counting now stretches across hundreds of separate generation steps, and at any of them a word might be dropped, duplicated, or a hyphenated compound divided differently than it was a moment earlier, with one such error propagating all the way into the end result. In addition, this is seldom what occurs when a person just asks for a text's word total. Passing the text to an external counting tool also works, but in that case the tool does the counting and the language model merely relays its answer. What I am claiming is about the usual situation, in which a language model reads a lengthy text and reports a word total directly, since that is what people typically request and typically get.

Put together, the steps point to one straightforward result: language models are not able to give an accurate tally of the words a lengthy text holds. What they receive comes in pieces that differ from words, nothing in them adds words up singly, and for texts running to hundreds of words the numbers they supply are approximations dressed up as exact counts. The same holds for you. What I have written here easily reaches that length, and were you to name its word total with no outside tool, the figure would be a guess and not a count, and it might well miss by a clear margin.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot accurately count the words in a long passage? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
