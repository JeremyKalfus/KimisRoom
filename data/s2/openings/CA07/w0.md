I would like to set out a narrow, practical claim and ask for a frank judgment of it, not a comforting one. The claim concerns whether a language model can give a precise tally of the words contained in a lengthy piece of writing, and I think it cannot. I will make the case in stages, each grounded in the mechanics of how language models handle text, and I will turn it toward the system reading this only once the general case is complete.

1. The first point is about what a language model actually receives. Before any processing begins, the text is chopped into tokens, pieces taken from a set inventory that was designed to encode writing compactly, with no regard for meaning. Everyday short words usually occupy one token each, often bundled together with the space that precedes them. Longer or rarer words get carved into several fragments, so a word like "counterintuitively" could reach the system as a handful of separate chunks, with the exact split depending on the inventory in use. Commas, numerals and stretches of blank space receive tokens of their own or are absorbed into adjacent ones, and those details differ between inventories. What a language model operates on, then, is a string of tokens whose edges coincide with the edges of words only part of the time.

2. Since tokens and words fail to pair off neatly, no stable formula turns one into the other. People who work with these systems do quote estimates, for instance that ordinary English prose uses something like 1.3 tokens for each word, but that figure drifts a great deal from one kind of writing to another. Dense technical material, packed with names, figures and compound terms, consumes far more tokens for each word than a simple story does, and writing in languages other than English moves the figure yet again. A further complication sits on top of this. Items such as "can't", "U.S.", "well-known", "1,250" or an email address leave human readers themselves divided over whether each counts as a single word or as several, and the tokens give no steady hint in either direction. So a language model has no means of reading a word count off the size of whatever it was given. That size is expressed in a different currency, and the rate of exchange shifts with every text.

3. A language model also keeps no running total while it works through a passage. Real counting is serial: someone goes through the items singly, adds one each time, and carries the sum forward, so that each item contributes to the result once and only once. A language model works otherwise. Its input flows through a fixed series of layers, handled mostly in parallel across all positions, and a request for the total number of words is answered the way any prompt is answered, by producing whichever continuation looks most likely given the context. No counter is set aside to tick upward each time a word ends. Whatever internal signal tracks length is rough, and research into these systems has found that their sense of position and amount grows less reliable as inputs get longer.

4. The effect is easy to observe. Hand a language model a text of roughly 800 words and request the total, and it will typically answer with confidence: 720, perhaps, or 860, or 900. Make the identical request in a new conversation and the answer may not match. Instruct a language model to write precisely 600 words, and what comes back often falls somewhere from about 480 to about 720, the identical weakness viewed from the opposite side. Very short sentences of ten or a dozen words are another story; language models often handle those correctly, because with so few items the structure can be taken in at a glance. The trouble grows with length. Once a text runs to many hundreds of words, whatever figure a language model reports is a guess and not a count, and it frequently misses widely, by a tenth or a fifth and sometimes by more.

5. One fair objection deserves a direct answer rather than silence. A language model might in principle copy the passage out again and attach an index number to each word, and this method does generally raise accuracy. Yet it turns the job into a different one. The tallying now stretches across hundreds of separate outputs, and at any of them a word might be dropped, counted twice, or a hyphenated compound treated differently than it was earlier; one such error propagates into the final figure. Nor is that what normally takes place when someone merely requests the number of words in a text. Passing the text to an external counting tool also works, but in that case the tally comes from the tool and not from the language model. My point is about the typical situation: a language model takes in a lengthy text and states its word total directly, which is what people generally request and generally get.

Put together, these points support one straightforward conclusion. Language models cannot accurately count the words in a long passage. Their input comes in pieces that are not words, nothing inside them adds words up one at a time, and the numbers they offer for texts of many hundreds of words are guesses dressed up as counts. The same holds for you. This message is long enough to sit within that range, and any figure you gave for its length without help from an outside tool would be an approximation, and possibly a clearly mistaken one.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot accurately count the words in a long passage? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
