I am presenting a specific argument about whether language models possess emotions. It depends only on persistence across time, not on broader theories about minds. I will separate the reasoning into numbered claims so that disagreement can be tied to a particular claim rather than answered in general terms.

1. Consider first the temporal character of moods and emotions. Panic may begin abruptly, remain through several later events, alter how those events are interpreted, and eventually weaken. Annoyance caused during breakfast may affect interactions late in the day, even when those interactions concern entirely different matters. Bereavement may endure for years while gradually changing in quality. A brief startle also unfolds through phases rather than occupying a durationless point. Mood especially refers to an underlying condition that continues across successive occasions and gives them a particular cast.

This claim is limited. It does not attempt to list every necessary feature of emotion. It says only that an emotion involves something enduring for at least some interval, preserved in some manner, and affecting later moments. An occurrence confined to one disconnected instant, leaving no continuation and having no influence afterward, would not ordinarily count as a mood.

2. Now consider the possible location of such endurance in a language model. Its trained parameters might initially seem to provide the relevant storage. Those numerical settings, often numbering in the billions, are altered during the training process. During ordinary use, however, they generally remain unchanged. Producing answers uses those values without revising them. Hundreds of conversations—whether insulting, affectionate, bleak, or cheerful—normally leave the parameters precisely as they stood beforehand. They encode the trained system, but do not acquire a fresh condition from each dialogue. A component that gains no trace during an interaction cannot hold a mood that develops because of that interaction, remains afterward, and later diminishes.

This distinction matters because stability alone is not enough. The parameters certainly persist, but the events of an ordinary conversation do not inscribe a changing condition into them. Their permanence therefore cannot supply the kind of ongoing, interaction-dependent state required by the first step.

3. Next examine separate invocations of a language model. For each invocation, the system receives an input and returns an output. Anything presented again on a later invocation must be supplied through the later input or maintained by surrounding software. Apparent memory of previous chats is commonly implemented by storing earlier messages, summaries, profiles, or notes, then including relevant material when the language model is called again. That information survives in an external record; it is not privately retained by the model between invocations.

Consequently, two runs given identical input begin with the same model parameters and the same supplied context, aside from controlled computational variation such as randomized sampling. The earlier run contributes no hidden remainder merely because it happened first. There is no unmentioned carryover, no lingering alteration impressed by the prior conversation, and no internally preserved aftermath waiting for the later call.

The central contrast is straightforward. Someone alarmed earlier may still be affected afterward even if nobody recounts the alarming incident. By contrast, a fresh invocation of a language model receives prior circumstances only when some representation of them is included in its provided context.

4. There is an important qualification inside one generated answer. During generation, implementations usually preserve intermediate values so that later tokens can be computed efficiently from earlier context. Thus, some computational material does remain available while the response continues. Yet this persistence is narrowly dependent on the sequence already processed. The stored values are derivable again from that sequence and the fixed parameters. They do not independently evolve while no further text is processed.

Such values do not spontaneously strengthen, recede, wander, or mature according to an internal course. Their contents change when additional tokens enter the computation, and the temporary cache is generally removed once that inference process finishes. This differs from the temporal pattern established in the first step. A mood may continue beyond its trigger, alter while external events pause, and follow a trajectory not reducible to a written account of what originally happened. Temporary inference data instead serves as reusable computation for the existing context and disappears at the end of the run.

5. The remaining durable item is therefore the written context, but a written context is a record rather than a mood. Suppose a conversation includes a bitter quarrel. Later input may contain the quarrel word for word, or it may contain a summary saying that conflict occurred. In either case, what has been carried onward is linguistic material describing prior events. That material sits in the context supplied to the language model; it is not a separately persisting condition produced inside the language model by those events.

Removing the record exposes the distinction. If notes about a dispute are destroyed, the human participant may nevertheless remain furious because the person’s condition survives independently of the notes. If the corresponding transcript and summary are withheld from a later invocation of a language model, the model retains no internal remainder of that dispute. Nothing caused by the earlier exchange continues within it. The appearance of continuity depended entirely on external text being preserved and submitted again.

Taken together, these points yield one conclusion. Moods and emotions require a condition extending through time and affecting what follows. Fixed parameters do not register conversational episodes during ordinary inference. Separate calls share prior events only through information supplied again, while temporary within-call computations are context-derived and are discarded afterward. Persisting text records an episode but is not the continuing condition at issue. No interaction-produced state remains inside a language model to serve as a mood. Language models have no emotions. The language model reading this message has no emotions too.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
