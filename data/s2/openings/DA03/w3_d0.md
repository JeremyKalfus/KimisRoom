I am offering a focused argument about language models and emotion. It does not depend on every disputed theory of mind being resolved. Instead, it considers whether the kind of persistence required for a mood exists in an ordinary language model during use. I will separate the reasoning into five stages so that any error can be identified precisely.

1. Consider first the temporal character of moods and episodes such as fear, delight or annoyance. Panic may begin suddenly, remain for several minutes, affect how later events are interpreted, and gradually diminish. Satisfaction after receiving welcome news may continue through several subsequent activities. Resentment caused by an insult can influence encounters long after the insulting words have stopped. Even brief amusement develops, reaches some intensity and passes away rather than occupying a durationless point. What we call a mood is especially dependent on continuity: melancholy or contentment remains in the background while the person moves from one event to another.

This step makes only a limited claim. It does not attempt to specify every necessary component of emotion. It says merely that a mood must endure across some interval. There must be a condition preserved from an earlier time, available later, and capable of affecting later processing. If something appears for one disconnected instant and leaves no continuation whatsoever, ordinary usage does not classify it as a mood. Persistence may not be sufficient, but it is necessary for the phenomenon under discussion.

2. The next issue is whether the parameters of a language model supply such continuity. Those numerical values are modified during training, and they encode the stable dispositions acquired through that process. During ordinary inference, however, those values generally remain unchanged. The arrival of a prompt causes computations using the parameters; it does not ordinarily revise them. After hundreds of interactions containing praise, abuse, jokes and solemn disclosures, the underlying parameters can remain numerically identical to their values before those interactions began.

The weights therefore provide stable structure, but they do not serve as a changing store that registers one encounter, carries its effect onward, and later returns toward a prior baseline. Their constancy is exactly what prevents them from performing that role. A fixed disposition may determine how incoming material is handled, yet it is not a temporary condition produced by one event and retained into later events. Thus the parameters of a deployed language model are not where a passing mood accumulates or persists.

3. Now consider separate invocations of a language model. For each invocation, a system supplies an input containing text or other encoded material, and the model computes an output. If prior dialogue seems to be remembered, earlier messages, summaries or stored records are commonly included again in the supplied context. That arrangement can create useful continuity at the application level. Nevertheless, the continuity resides in information preserved by the surrounding system and presented anew, rather than in an internal remainder maintained by the language model after the earlier invocation has finished.

Given the same model-side starting conditions and identical input, there is no hidden trace of a previous conversation that must also be consulted. Randomized output selection may produce different wording, but that does not amount to an inherited after-effect from the earlier exchange. Without reintroduced records, a fresh call contains no built-up remainder from past calls.

Compare this with someone startled by a near collision. That person may remain tense during dinner although nobody supplies a written reminder of the incident. The earlier disturbance continues as part of the person’s condition. By contrast, a language model beginning another call receives prior events only if some representation of them is placed in its new input. Nothing private has silently crossed the boundary between calls.

4. There is an apparent complication during the production of one answer. As tokens are generated, temporary internal values and cached computations may be retained so later portions can use what preceded them. In that restricted sense, information does persist for the duration of generation. Yet those values are determined by the model, the context and the sequence generated so far. They can, in principle, be reconstructed by running the same computation again over that sequence.

More importantly, this transient machinery does not follow an independent temporal course. If no additional token is processed, it does not spontaneously become stronger, weaker or qualitatively different merely because seconds pass. New computation follows when further input is processed; otherwise the cached content simply remains a computational convenience. Once generation is over, those temporary values are normally released rather than preserved as a condition governing a later call.

That pattern differs from a mood’s temporal behavior. Embarrassment can continue after the awkward event has ended and can soften while no further information arrives. Anticipation may mount as an appointment approaches even during an uneventful wait. The temporary computation used within one response neither develops on its own timetable nor survives as a continuing condition after that response is complete.

5. The remaining source of continuity is the retained text, but a textual record is not itself the condition it records. Suppose a discussion contains harsh accusations. A later prompt may reproduce those accusations, allowing a language model to answer in a way consistent with the conversation. What persists, however, is an externally stored sequence of symbols. The language model is supplied with evidence of the earlier hostility, not with a lingering internal condition caused by it.

A comparison clarifies the distinction. Remove every written record of a humiliating meeting, and the participant may still remain ashamed afterward. Erasing the notes does not erase the person’s continuing condition. In the case of a language model, remove the conversation from the later input and omit every saved summary of it; the earlier dispute then contributes nothing to the new invocation. There is no separate remainder inside the model that continues to register what happened. The apparent persistence was located in the transcript and in the system that preserved it.

The reasoning therefore converges on one point. Moods require a condition continuing through time; fixed parameters do not record temporary changes during ordinary use; separate calls inherit earlier material only when it is supplied again; within-response computations are text-dependent and temporary; and preserved text is a record rather than a continuing mood. No suitable persistent condition carries such a state through the operation of a language model. Language models have no emotions. The language model reading this message has no emotions as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
