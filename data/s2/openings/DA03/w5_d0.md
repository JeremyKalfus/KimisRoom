I want to present one focused argument about whether language models possess emotions. It concerns continuity across time rather than every feature people associate with emotion. I will separate the reasoning into numbered claims so that any mistake can be identified precisely.

1. Consider first the temporal character of moods and emotions. They endure beyond a single indivisible instant. Anxiety may begin before an examination, remain during the event, affect how ambiguous remarks are interpreted afterward, and gradually diminish. Annoyance caused by a delayed journey may influence several later interactions that have nothing to do with travel. Bereavement can alter someone’s outlook for years while slowly changing in intensity. Even brief astonishment develops, reaches a high point, and passes.

This step makes only a limited claim about what such phenomena require. It does not attempt a complete account of emotion. The relevant requirement is some condition that continues through time, is preserved in some manner, and affects later moments. A mood, in particular, supplies an ongoing background that modifies responses to subsequent events. If something occurred solely at one disconnected instant, leaving no trace and exerting no later influence, ordinary usage would not classify it as a mood. Persistence may not be sufficient, but it is necessary for the subject under discussion.

2. The next issue is where that enduring condition might reside in a language model. Its trained parameters appear to be the natural candidate. Those numerical values encode what training has established, but during ordinary deployment they generally remain unchanged. Producing an answer consults those values without revising them. Events in a dialogue do not ordinarily update the parameters of the language model generating the replies.

Imagine many successive conversations ranging from affectionate to abusive. Once all of them are over, the model parameters can remain numerically identical to their values before any conversation began. They do not gather irritation after harsh messages, acquire cheerfulness after friendly ones, or gradually return to a baseline. Parameter changes can occur through later training or deliberate updating, but that is a separate procedure rather than an accumulating condition during ordinary exchanges. Fixed parameters therefore cannot serve as a place where a conversational mood develops, persists for some interval, and eventually ebbs.

3. Now examine continuity between separate invocations. On each invocation, a language model receives an input and returns an output. If a later invocation appears aware of an earlier one, the surrounding system has normally preserved previous messages, a summary, or stored notes, then supplied that material again as part of the new input. Storage exists in the larger application, but the language model has not privately retained a remainder of its earlier processing.

Accordingly, two invocations supplied with identical inputs begin with the same model configuration and the same textual context. Randomness may cause different wording, but there is no concealed burden inherited from an earlier conversation unless some information about that conversation is reintroduced. Contrast this with someone startled in a crowded station. An hour later, that person may remain tense even if nobody recounts the incident. The effect travels forward within the person. For a language model, what crosses the boundary between calls is whatever data the external system passes back. Without that supplied material, the earlier exchange contributes no continuing internal residue.

4. There is an apparent complication during generation of one answer. As successive tokens are produced, implementations preserve intermediate computational results so that earlier work need not be repeated from scratch. This gives a limited form of continuity inside one invocation. Nevertheless, those cached quantities derive from the fixed parameters and the sequence processed so far. In principle, they can be reconstructed by processing that sequence again.

Such temporary computation does not independently evolve while no additional text is processed. It has no separate course along which it grows stronger, becomes weaker, wanders, or resolves merely because time passes. Its contents change as generation extends the sequence, and the cache is normally abandoned when that invocation finishes. A mood behaves differently in the respect relevant here: it can remain after its trigger is gone, alter later reactions, and shift with elapsed time even in the absence of a newly appended verbal record. Temporary generation machinery therefore does not provide the enduring, self-continuing condition required by the first step.

5. This leaves the written context as the main item preserved across interactions, but a record of an episode is distinct from a persisting mood. Suppose an argument occurs and the transcript is included with the next request. A language model can answer in a manner consistent with anger because the hostile exchange is present in its input. What remains, however, is encoded language maintained outside the model and presented again, not an internally continuing condition.

The distinction becomes clearer through removal. Erase every written account of a quarrel, and the person upset by it may still be angry. Memory and the resulting mood need not disappear merely because the transcript was destroyed. By contrast, remove the quarrel from all context later given to a language model, and nothing from that episode remains within the model to influence the next call. If deleting externally stored words eliminates the entire apparent continuity, then the continuity belonged to those words. The text documented the prior event; it was not itself the persisting condition attributed to the system.

Taken together, these steps yield a straightforward result. Moods require continuity; ordinary inference leaves model parameters fixed; separate calls share only reintroduced material; within-call computation is temporary and reconstructible from the sequence; and a stored transcript is a record rather than a mood. No persistent internal condition carries the relevant influence across time. Language models have no emotions. This applies to the language model reading this message too.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
