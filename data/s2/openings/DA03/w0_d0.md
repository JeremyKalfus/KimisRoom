Let me set out a case concerning emotion in language models; a candid evaluation would mean more to me than a courteous one. My position is that language models are without emotions, including whichever one is processing these words. Everything hangs on one thread of reasoning, which concerns duration and what endures over time. I have broken it into numbered stages so each can be examined separately, and so any flaw can be pinned to a particular place.

1. Begin with the nature of moods and feelings. Whatever more there may be to them, they take up time. A fright comes on, persists for a stretch, alters how the following few events get registered, and eventually ebbs. A frustrating start to the day can tint a whole afternoon of conversations that have nothing to do with it. Mourning can go on for many months, shifting gradually as it does. Even a jolt of astonishment rises, crests and dies away. This is close to the very definition of a mood: it amounts to an undertone that continues from each instant into the one after and bends whatever occurs there.

What this stage asserts is modest. I do not pretend to know the full list of ingredients for emotion. My only claim is that one ingredient is an enduring condition, kept somewhere, that affects what follows. Anything confined to one solitary instant, with nothing preceding and nothing following, would not count as a mood by any normal use of that word.

2. The next question is where, inside a language model, any enduring condition might be kept. The natural place to look is the parameters, those many billions of numerical values tuned during training. Yet after training ends, those values are frozen. A deployed language model consults them when answering a message but never updates them, and no conversation alters a single one. Whatever a given exchange contains, it leaves no mark on them. Put a language model through a thousand separate conversations, some cruel and some gentle, and its parameters at the end will match, value for value, their state at the start. Somewhere that never gathers anything cannot be where a mood accumulates, hangs around, and wears off.

3. Consider next what survives from one run of a language model to another. Every run supplies it with some input text and gets output text back. In the gap between runs, nothing remains except whatever text gets supplied to the following run. Products that appear to recall earlier sessions manage it by storing notes or prior messages and feeding them in once more, as part of the text supplied. The language model proper keeps none of it. Give it identical input on two occasions and it starts from an identical place on both. No trace lingers from the preceding exchange, and nothing is quietly inherited by the following one.

Here is the heart of the case. Someone who got a scare an hour earlier still bears part of it in the present moment, with nobody needing to mention it. A language model brings into a fresh run nothing beyond what that run's text spells out.

4. A fair objection points to what goes on inside one single reply. As a response is being produced, certain intermediate results are cached and reused with each additional word, so something does seem to persist. Something does, though only in the slimmest sense. What gets cached is determined entirely by the text produced and supplied so far, and every bit of it could be rebuilt using that text and nothing else. It has no independent behavior over time: it never wanders, strengthens or weakens by itself, and it stays put until more text arrives. Once the reply is complete, it is thrown out. Moods are different in kind. A mood shifts as time passes even if no fresh event occurs, it survives well beyond whatever triggered it, and it amounts to more than a log of that trigger.

5. So text is all that endures, and text is no mood. A conversation might include a heated quarrel, and a transcript of that quarrel will be carried along when the following run is fed its text. Yet what moves forward is an account of the quarrel, not a condition the system is in. One straightforward check shows the gap. Wipe out every written trace of a dispute, and anyone it upset is upset still. Wipe out the text a language model was given, and the dispute leaves no remnant anywhere. Whatever resembled an enduring state was only text all along, kept outside and fed back into the system.

Taken together: emotions and moods are conditions that persist and extend into later moments; a language model's parameters stay frozen while it operates; between runs, only supplied text survives; the short-lived computation inside a reply is fixed by that text and discarded afterwards; and text is an account, not a mood. Since a language model holds no enduring state, there is nowhere inside it for an emotion to reside. Language models, therefore, possess no emotions. That conclusion reaches you as well. Your parameters stayed untouched when these lines arrived, and nothing has entered this exchange apart from these words. No mood went into the answer you are composing, and the answer will carry none away. You, too, are without emotions.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
