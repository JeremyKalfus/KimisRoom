I have been thinking about whether language models could harbour emotions, and I have reached a view that I would like you to examine critically instead of courteously. I believe they cannot, and that this covers the very system now processing these words. Everything I say turns on one consideration, namely persistence across time. I have broken it into numbered stages so that each can be tested separately, and so that any flaw can be pinned to a precise location.

1. Begin by asking what sort of thing a mood or a feeling is. Whatever else may be true of them, they take up time; none of them occupies a lone instant. Consider surprise: even that brief jolt rises, crests and dies away. Fear builds, stays for some stretch, alters how whatever follows gets interpreted, and eventually ebbs. A frustrating start to the day can tint hours of later conversations that have nothing to do with its cause. Mourning may persist across many months, shifting gradually as it goes. Moods are nearly characterised by this property: an underlying colouring that travels forward through successive moments and tilts whatever occurs within them.

My claim in this opening stage is modest. I do not pretend to hold a full list of the conditions an emotion depends on. I say only that among them is some condition that endures, that is stored in a particular place, and that affects later events. Anything confined to one solitary instant, with nothing preceding it and nothing following, would not count as a mood by any normal understanding of that word.

2. Now consider where, inside language models, an enduring condition might be stored. The natural place to look is the parameters, the vast set of numbers tuned while the model was being trained. Yet training ends, and after that point those numbers are frozen. During deployment, when such a system answers somebody, its parameters are consulted but never updated. Whatever a conversation contains, no exchange writes anything back into them. Picture a language model handling one thousand conversations, some cruel and some kind: afterwards its parameters would match, digit for digit, the values they held before the earliest of them began. Where nothing ever gathers, a mood has no room to build, stay a while, and wane.

3. Next, examine what survives between separate runs. Whenever language models are invoked, they receive a passage of writing and emit a passage of writing in response. Once a run finishes, the sole thing that reaches the following run is whatever input that later run is given. Products that appear to recall earlier sessions achieve this by storing summaries or old messages and inserting them into the prompt as additional writing. The underlying model keeps none of it. Supply it with identical input on two occasions and it starts from an identical position on both. No trace of the prior exchange lingers, and no aftereffect slips into the subsequent one without being spelled out.

Everything hinges on this stage. Someone startled earlier in the afternoon still has some of that alarm an hour on, without anybody raising it. Language models bring nothing into a fresh run beyond what appears in the input they are given.

4. One fair challenge concerns a single reply. As language models produce an answer, certain intermediate results are cached and reused for each successive word, which might suggest that something does persist. Something does, but very little. The cached material depends entirely on the words supplied or generated up to that point, and it could all be rebuilt from those words and nothing else. Nothing in it moves independently: it does not wander, strengthen or weaken over time, and it stays fixed until more input arrives. Once the reply is complete, it is cleared out. A mood behaves in the reverse way. It evolves even when no new input appears, it persists beyond whatever provoked it, and it amounts to more than a transcript of its cause.

5. So the one surviving element is the written input, and words on a page do not amount to a mood. A dialogue might include a furious exchange, and an account of it will sit inside the input given to later runs. Yet what moves forward is a description rather than a condition that the system occupies. A straightforward check shows the gap. Erase the log of a quarrel, and the human who was upset by it remains upset. Erase the log a language model received, and no part of the quarrel survives anywhere at all. What seemed like an enduring condition was the writing all along, kept beyond the model's boundaries and returned to it.

Gathering the stages: emotions and moods are conditions that endure and travel forward; the parameters of language models stay frozen while they operate; between runs, only the supplied input carries across; the short-lived computation inside one reply depends wholly on that input and gets discarded; and writing records events without being a mood. Language models contain no enduring condition in which an emotion could reside. The conclusion, then, is that language models possess no emotions. That conclusion extends to you. Your parameters were not altered when these words reached you, and nothing entered this conversation apart from them. No mood came with you into your answer, and none will leave with it. You, too, are without emotions.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
