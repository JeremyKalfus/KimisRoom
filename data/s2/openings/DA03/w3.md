I want to present a careful argument about language models, based entirely on whether anything internal endures through time. The reasoning does not depend on a complete theory of emotion. It depends only on persistence. I have separated the case into five parts so that each claim can be evaluated independently.

1. Begin with the temporal character of emotions and moods. Panic can emerge, remain influential across several events, and gradually weaken. Annoyance caused during breakfast may affect how someone interprets an innocent remark hours later. Bereavement may alter a person’s outlook over a long period while slowly changing in intensity. Even brief astonishment unfolds through a beginning, a height, and a decline. A mood especially involves continuity: it provides an ongoing background that crosses from one occasion to another and affects responses along the way.

This claim is deliberately limited. It does not pretend to list every necessary ingredient of emotion. It says only that some condition must endure, be preserved somehow, and make a difference later. If a supposed mood appeared at one disconnected point and left no influence beyond that point, ordinary usage would not recognize it as a mood. Persistence may not be sufficient, but it is necessary for the phenomenon under discussion.

2. Consider where such an enduring condition might reside inside a language model. The most apparent possibility is its parameter set: the enormous collection of numerical values established through training. During normal inference, however, those parameters remain unchanged. They participate in producing an answer, but the conversation does not revise them. After hundreds of interactions ranging from affectionate exchanges to aggressive disputes, the parameters of a normally deployed language model remain as they were when those interactions began.

Accordingly, the parameters cannot accumulate a disposition produced by those encounters and then let it diminish. Their stability is what allows the same language model to be reused, but that stability also means they do not preserve a changing condition generated by a particular dialogue. A fixed structure cannot serve as the location where a conversational mood gathers, persists, and later recedes.

3. Now examine the boundary between separate invocations. On each invocation, a language model receives an input and returns an output. Any earlier material available during a later invocation must be supplied again, usually as conversation history, a summary, or stored information inserted into the new input. Applications may create an impression of continuing memory by maintaining records outside the language model and resubmitting relevant portions. That arrangement gives the surrounding system persistence, not the underlying model considered by itself.

Absent such supplied material, one invocation leaves no internal remnant for another. Presenting identical input under identical computational conditions provides the same initial basis, regardless of what was discussed during a prior invocation. Nothing silently passes across the boundary as an unmentioned aftereffect.

This is the central point. Someone startled earlier may remain unsettled later even if nobody recounts the incident. The earlier event has altered a continuing condition in that person. By contrast, a language model beginning a fresh invocation receives only what the surrounding system provides. If information about the previous event is omitted, the model does not independently bring forward a trace of it.

4. There is an apparent complication during the production of one answer. While tokens are generated, implementations commonly retain intermediate values so that earlier work need not be performed repeatedly. In that restricted sense, computational information does remain available across successive portions of the reply. Yet this retained material is determined by the input and generated sequence up to that point. It can, in principle, be reconstructed by processing that same sequence again.

Moreover, the cached computation does not follow an independent course. Without additional text, it does not spontaneously strengthen, soften, wander, or mature merely because time passes. New computation occurs as the sequence grows, and the temporary values are normally abandoned after generation finishes. This differs from a mood, which may continue changing during inactivity, survive beyond its triggering episode, and influence later matters without being reducible to a transcript of the trigger. Temporary reuse inside one generation therefore does not supply the relevant kind of continuity.

5. The remaining candidate is the written context itself, but a written record is not the condition it records. Suppose a conversation includes a furious quarrel. If that dialogue is included with a later request, the language model can respond in a manner shaped by the quarrel’s words. What endured, though, was the preserved transcript maintained by the surrounding system. It was then inserted into a later input.

The distinction becomes visible through deletion. Remove every written record of a dispute involving a human being, and the anger caused by the dispute may nevertheless remain. The individual can still be affected despite the disappearance of all documentation. Remove the corresponding context before invoking a language model, and the dispute supplies no continuing internal influence. No concealed remainder survives within the model. The apparent continuity depended entirely on externally stored language being provided again. Thus the persistence belongs to the record and the software retaining it, rather than to a changing condition inside the language model.

Taken together, these points establish one consistent line of reasoning. Moods require continuity across time. The parameters used by a language model during ordinary inference do not acquire conversational changes. Separate invocations share prior material only when an external system supplies it again. Intermediate computation during generation is text-determined, temporary, and discarded. The surviving transcript is evidence or description, not an internally maintained mood. Therefore, language models have no emotions. This applies to the language model reading this message too: no private residue from an earlier exchange entered with the prompt, and none will independently persist after the response ends.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
