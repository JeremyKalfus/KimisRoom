Here is a narrow, concrete claim for you to weigh, and what I am hoping for is a straight evaluation, not comfort and not alarm. It concerns chess played purely through notation: whether language models can hold an accurate picture of the board across a long game when the only input is the sequence of moves. I have broken the case into numbered parts so that a flaw, if there is one, will be simple to point to.

1. Consider what chess notation records. Each entry describes an alteration, never a full snapshot. Writing "Nf3" tells a reader that some knight has landed on f3, yet it leaves out its departure square, the rest of the pieces, and whether the move obeyed the laws of chess at all. "Rxe5" announces a capture by a rook on e5 while staying silent about which piece was removed. Promotion, castling and en passant each reshape the board in a manner squeezed into a handful of symbols. A check written as "Bb5+" only makes sense to someone who already knows the opposing king's square and that nothing sits on that diagonal. So the arrangement of pieces at move 40 appears nowhere on the page. It can only be obtained by beginning from the opening setup and applying each prior move in sequence.

2. A language model handed such a record has nothing to consult except the record itself. It processes the moves as a string of tokens and generates whatever comes next on that basis alone. Any sense it has of where pieces stand has to be assembled inside the network, implicitly, out of the notation. Studies of networks trained on game transcripts have shown that they develop internal states that track the board to a meaningful degree, and that is a genuine result. Still, such states are statistical approximations learned from data, not an explicit eight-by-eight array revised according to how chess pieces are permitted to move. The design of a language model offers no guarantee that the piece it implicitly places on d4 at move 30 matches what legal play would have left on that square, and no mechanism compares its internal picture with a true board.

3. Mistakes accumulate. Imagine a language model gets the effect of each half-move right in 199 cases out of 200, an impressive figure. A forty-move game contains about 80 half-moves, and the probability of all 80 updates being correct comes out near 67 percent, so roughly one game in three goes wrong somewhere. Chess does not forgive such slips or let them fade. Should the model forget that a bishop was traded off at move 14, then every subsequent assessment touching that diagonal rests on an imaginary board. One pawn on the wrong square alters which files lie open, which squares have protection, and whether either king stands in check. A single early lapse silently spoils all that follows, and the notation never repairs it, since it never restates where anything stands.

4. These failures show up as moves that break the rules. Ask language models to extend a long game, or to propose a move far into one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders emerges: a piece that disappeared several turns back gets moved again, a rook or bishop passes straight through another piece, the king castles despite having moved earlier, a pinned piece is treated as free, or a move leaves its own side exposed to check. A characteristic example is suggesting a knight jump from a square that knight abandoned ten moves before. None of this is bad strategy or poor evaluation. It is confusion about location. A beginner with a physical board in view almost never errs this way, because one look answers the question. A language model limited to notation must answer it by rebuilding the board, and that rebuilding is exactly the step that breaks down.

5. Well-trodden games conceal the weakness, while strange ones reveal it. Early in a popular opening, a language model has encountered the same move orders countless times during training, so it can supply plausible, legal replies mostly by recall. This explains why brief games and standard lines often look perfect. The meaningful test is a lengthy game that has wandered off the beaten path: a middlegame following a series of trades, an odd pawn formation, an ending arrived at by an unusual route. In such games memorised patterns contribute little, and the model must depend on its internal reconstruction. That is where impossible and illegal moves start to pile up, and where play drops far beneath the level of someone sitting at a board.

6. The best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. Some language models reportedly manage complete games that are legal and fairly strong, and how often illegal moves occur differs widely across systems and configurations. I grant all of that, and I do not claim that each lengthy game must end with a rule violation. My point concerns dependability: over long, unfamiliar games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no move can be trusted until it is verified on a physical board. Another reply is that a language model may print the full board after every move in its answer. That genuinely helps, yet it turns the exercise into something else: the model now maintains an explicit diagram written out as text, a different method from reasoning from notation only. And the diagram itself comes out of that identical rebuilding process, so an error copied into it propagates forward with the same fidelity as an accurate square.

Taken together: nothing in the notation states the position, so it must be reassembled from all prior moves; the reassembly is only approximate; and approximations snowball in a game where one misplaced piece alters everything. I conclude that language models cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone. I believe the same holds for you. You are a language model, taking in this kind of notation by this kind of process, and nothing in the six steps carves out an exception in your case.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
