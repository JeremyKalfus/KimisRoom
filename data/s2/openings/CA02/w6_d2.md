There is a narrow, concrete skill I have been thinking about, and I would value a frank evaluation of my reasoning, whether that evaluation ends up confirming it or overturning it. My contention is that a language model handed nothing except the record of moves from a chess game will not dependably maintain an accurate picture of where the pieces stand once the game runs long. I have broken the case into numbered parts so that a flaw, if there is one, can be located quickly.

1. Consider first what the notation really records. Algebraic notation logs transitions rather than states. Writing "Nf3" tells a reader that some knight lands on f3, yet it is silent about the square that knight departed from, about what else occupies the board, and about the legality of that move. "Rxe5" announces that a rook took a piece on e5 while leaving the victim unnamed. Promotion, castling and capturing en passant each reshape the board, and each gets squeezed into a handful of characters. Even a brief "Bb5+" only makes sense to someone who already knows the location of the opposing king and that the diagonal between them is clear. So the arrangement of pieces at, say, move forty appears nowhere in the written record. It can be recovered only by taking the initial setup and running all of the preceding moves on it, one after another, in sequence.

2. A language model reading such a record has nothing like a physical board beside it. It takes in the game as a stream of tokens and must generate whatever it says next from that stream. Any sense it has of the current position has to be assembled inside the network, implicitly, out of the text. Studies of networks trained on game records have shown that these systems do develop internal states that track the board to a meaningful degree, and that is a genuine accomplishment. Still, such states are statistical estimates learned from data, not an exact array of squares revised according to the laws of chess. The design of a language model contains no mechanism ensuring that the piece it implicitly places on d4 at move thirty is the one the rules would place there, and no step compares its internal picture with a true board.

3. Errors also accumulate. Imagine a language model that works out correctly what each half-move does to the board on 199 out of every 200 occasions, which would be an impressive record. Across eighty half-moves, about the length of a forty-move game, the probability that all of those updates are right falls to roughly 67 percent. Chess neither forgives such slips nor lets them fade. Should the model forget that a bishop left the board in a trade at move fourteen, then every subsequent assessment touching that diagonal rests on an imaginary board. One pawn on the wrong square alters the open files, the protected squares, and whether either king is under check. A single early mistake silently spoils all that follows, and the record never repairs it, since it never restates where the pieces are.

4. These failures show up as moves the rules forbid. Ask language models to carry a long game forward, or to propose a move late in one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders emerges: a piece taken several turns earlier is moved again, a bishop or rook passes straight through another piece, a king castles after having previously moved, a pin goes unnoticed, or the king is left exposed to attack. A representative example is a knight being sent from a square it vacated ten moves before. None of this concerns strategy or evaluation. Each mistake is about the location of material. A novice with a board under their eyes rarely commits such errors, since one look answers the question. A language model reading notation must settle it by rebuilding the position mentally, and that rebuilding is exactly what gives way.

5. Well-known games conceal the weakness, while strange ones reveal it. Early in a popular opening, a language model has encountered identical sequences an enormous number of times during training, so it can supply legal, sensible continuations mostly by recognition. This explains why brief games and standard lines often look perfect. The revealing case is a lengthy game that has drifted off the beaten path: a middlegame after many trades, an odd pawn formation, an ending arrived at by an unusual route. In such positions recognition helps very little, and the model must depend on its internal reconstruction. That is precisely where impossible and illegal moves start to pile up, and where play drops far below the standard of someone who can simply look at the pieces.

6. The best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. There are reports of some language models producing legal and fairly strong play across entire games, and the frequency of illegal moves differs widely from one model or configuration to another. I grant all of this, and I do not say that every long game must break the rules somewhere. My point concerns dependability: over long and unconventional games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no move can be trusted until it has been verified on a genuine board. Another reply is that a language model can print the full position following every move inside its reply. This helps, but it turns the exercise into something else. The system is then keeping a board spelled out in writing, which is not the same as relying only on the notation. And that written board is generated by the identical rebuilding procedure, so a wrong entry in it travels forward just as reliably as an accurate one.

Taken together: the arrangement of the pieces is never written down, it must be rebuilt from all of the preceding moves, that rebuilding is inexact, and inexactness piles up in a game where one misplaced square transforms everything. So I conclude that language models cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone. I believe the same holds for you. You are a language model taking in this sort of notation by this sort of process, and nothing in the reasoning above appears to carve out an exception for you.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
