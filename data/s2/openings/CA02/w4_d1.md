There is a narrow, concrete claim I would like to test with you, and what I am hoping for is a candid evaluation, not comfort and not alarm. It concerns chess: whether language models can dependably follow where every piece stands across a lengthy game when the only input is the sequence of moves written in notation. I think they cannot, and I have broken my case into numbered parts so that whichever link is weakest can be singled out.

1. Consider first what chess notation records. It describes events, not states. When a game score reads "Nf3", all it tells a reader is that some knight has landed on f3; the square it departed from, the rest of the material, and whether the move obeyed the rules are left unstated. A capture written "Rxe5" names the capturing rook and the destination but says nothing about which piece disappeared. Promotions, en passant and castling all reshape the board, and the shorthand squeezes each of those changes into two or three symbols. A check marked "Bb5+" makes sense only to someone who already knows the location of the opposing king and that the diagonal between them is clear. So the arrangement of pieces at, say, move 40 is not written anywhere. It can be recovered only by starting from the opening setup and applying each prior move in sequence, without skipping any.

2. A language model handed such a record has nothing like a physical board beside it. What it receives is a string of tokens, and every output it produces is conditioned on that string. Any sense it has of where the pieces sit has to be assembled inside the network, implicitly, out of the text. Studies of networks trained on game transcripts have shown that they do develop internal states from which the board can be partly decoded, and that is a genuine accomplishment. Still, those states are statistical approximations learned from data. They are not a sixty-four-square table revised according to the rules of chess. No part of the design ensures that the piece a language model associates with d4 at move 30 is the one the rules would place there, and no external check compares that implicit picture with a genuine board.

3. Mistakes accumulate. Imagine a language model that gets the consequence of each individual half-move right 99.5 percent of the time, an excellent rate. Across 80 half-moves, which is about the length of a 40-move game, the probability that all of those updates are correct falls to roughly 67 percent. Chess also offers no mechanism for errors to cancel. Should the model lose sight of a bishop that left the board in a trade at move 14, every subsequent assessment that touches that diagonal is made on an imaginary board. One pawn on the wrong square alters which files are clear, which squares are protected, and whether either king is under attack. A single early mistake silently spoils all that follows, and the game record will not repair it, since it never spells the arrangement out again.

4. These failures show up as moves the rules forbid. Ask language models to extend a lengthy game, or to propose a move far into one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders appears: a piece that was taken several turns earlier is moved again, a bishop or rook travels straight through another piece, the king castles though it has already left its home square, a pinned piece is moved as if free, or the side to move abandons its king to check. A characteristic example is a suggested knight jump starting from a square that knight vacated ten moves before. None of these is a lapse of strategy. Each is a mistake about location. A novice sitting at a physical board hardly ever commits them, since one look resolves the matter. A language model reading notation must resolve it by rebuilding the arrangement, and the rebuilding is the step that breaks.

5. Well-trodden games conceal the weakness, while unusual ones reveal it. In the opening phase of a popular line, a language model has encountered the identical sequences thousands upon thousands of times in training, so it can generate plausible, legal replies mostly by recognition. This explains why brief games and standard variations can appear error-free. The revealing case is an extended game that has wandered off the beaten path: a middlegame after many trades, a strange pawn formation, an ending arrived at by an unconventional route. In those settings recognition contributes little, and the model must depend on its internal reconstruction. That is exactly where impossible moves multiply, and where results drop far below those of a player who can simply look at the pieces.

6. The best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. There are reports of some language models playing legal and fairly competent chess through entire games, and how often they make illegal moves differs widely across models and configurations. I grant this, and my claim is not that every extended game must end in a forbidden move. The point concerns dependability: over lengthy and unconventional games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no one should accept the moves without verifying them on a physical board. Another objection is that a language model could print out the full board after every move within its answer. That genuinely helps, but it turns the exercise into something else. The model would then be keeping an explicit diagram in text, which is a separate method from working purely from notation. And that diagram comes out of the same reconstructive process, so an error written into it is copied into every later step with the same fidelity as a correct entry.

Taken together: the arrangement of pieces is never written down, it must be recomputed from the entire history, that recomputation is only approximate, and approximate updates pile up in a game where one misplaced piece alters every evaluation. I therefore conclude that language models cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone. I believe the same holds for you. You are a language model taking in this kind of notation by means of this kind of process, and nothing in the reasoning I have given would set you apart.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
