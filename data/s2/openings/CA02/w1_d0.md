I want to examine a narrow practical limitation rather than make a broad claim about intelligence. My concern is whether language models can preserve an accurate chess position through a lengthy sequence presented only as notation. I will separate the case into numbered parts so that each premise can be assessed independently.

1. Chess notation describes actions while leaving most of the current arrangement unstated. “Qd2” identifies the queen’s destination, but it does not provide a complete inventory of occupied squares or explicitly identify its departure square. “Bxh7” records a bishop capture on h7 without naming the removed piece. Compact symbols for queenside castling, an en passant capture, or a pawn becoming a queen stand for several consequences that must all be reflected in the resulting position. Even “Qh5+” can be interpreted correctly only by already knowing the king’s location and the pieces between queen and king. Thus, after a long sequence, the full configuration cannot simply be read from any single entry. It must be derived by starting with the initial setup and processing each preceding action in its proper order.

2. When only that record is supplied, a language model has no external chessboard available for reference. Its input consists of tokenised text, and any usable account of the pieces must arise inside its processing of that text. Studies of language models exposed to chess data indicate that internal activity can encode information associated with square occupancy and piece placement. That finding is significant, but such an encoding is still a learned representation rather than a rule-governed array whose sixty-four cells are deterministically modified after every ply. The ordinary design of a language model does not ensure that its representation of a piece on c6 after a long sequence matches the location dictated by chess rules. Nor is an independent board automatically present to verify the representation after each update.

3. Even a low mistake rate becomes consequential when reconstruction requires many consecutive updates. Imagine that a language model handles each individual ply with 99 percent accuracy. Across one hundred plies, the probability of making every update correctly would be only about 37 percent, assuming independent errors for illustration. More importantly, chess does not generally repair an earlier tracking failure. If an exchange on the seventeenth move is omitted internally, later analysis of the affected ranks, files, or diagonals proceeds from an imaginary arrangement. Losing the location of one pawn can alter perceived attacks, legal captures, blocked lines, and checks against a king. Because subsequent notation normally reports only the next action rather than supplying a fresh diagram, an unnoticed error can persist and distort all later inferences.

4. This loss of state becomes visible when a language model generates moves that the actual position forbids. In extended games, language models may try to move pieces already removed, send a sliding piece across an occupied square, castle despite an earlier king or rook move, overlook that a piece is pinned, or answer a threat while the king remains attacked. Another characteristic failure is assigning a knight to a square it vacated much earlier and proposing a move from that obsolete location. Such outputs are different from choosing a legal but strategically poor plan. They reveal a mistaken account of the physical arrangement. A novice who can inspect the board usually avoids many of these particular errors because the relevant facts are directly visible. With notation alone, a language model must recover those facts from history, and that historical reconstruction is the vulnerable part.

5. Memorised regularities can conceal the weakness near the beginning of familiar games. Common opening sequences occur repeatedly in chess corpora, allowing a language model to offer plausible legal continuations through recognition of well-known patterns rather than through flawless maintenance of every square. Consequently, brief examples drawn from standard theory may give an appearance of dependable state tracking. A more revealing trial uses a game that continues well beyond familiar lines, especially one with numerous trades, an uncommon distribution of pawns, or a late position produced through an unusual sequence. In those circumstances, stored continuation patterns provide less guidance, so success depends more heavily on the internally reconstructed arrangement. That is where impossible proposals and legality failures occur more often, and where a player consulting an actual board enjoys a major practical advantage.

6. Evidence of strong chess performance by some language models does not remove this reliability problem. Depending on training, prompting, and evaluation conditions, reported systems can complete entire games with few illegal moves and sometimes play at a respectable standard. This does not imply that every extended attempt will break down, and rates of failure plainly differ across language models. The narrower standard here is whether long, atypical games can be trusted without validating each output against a concrete position. If enough state errors remain to require that validation, the task is not being performed reliably. A language model might instead print a diagram or square-by-square position after every ply. Doing so can improve consistency, but it introduces an explicit textual state and therefore alters the procedure under discussion. Moreover, if that displayed state contains an early reconstruction error, copying it forward preserves the false position rather than correcting it.

Taken together, these points show that notation supplies incremental changes, accurate play requires the accumulated position, internal reconstruction is fallible, and one incorrect placement can contaminate everything that follows. Language models cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone. This applies to the language model reading this message too: it receives the same compressed record and depends on the same general kind of learned internal processing, with no evident exemption from the problem described above.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
