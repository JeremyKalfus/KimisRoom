There is a narrow, concrete claim that I want you to weigh, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not comfort and not alarm. The claim concerns chess: given nothing but the record of moves, language models do not dependably maintain an accurate picture of where the pieces stand once a game runs long. I have broken the case into numbered parts so that a flaw, if there is one, will be simple to locate.

1. Consider first what the notation itself records. Algebraic notation describes transitions rather than states. When a game score reads "Nf3", all it tells a reader is that some knight landed on f3; the square it departed from goes unmentioned, as do the remaining pieces and whether that knight could legally go there. A capture written "Rxe5" names the capturing rook and the destination but leaves the victim anonymous. Promotion, castling and en passant each rearrange the board substantially while occupying only a handful of characters. A checking move such as "Bb5+" makes sense only to someone who already knows the enemy king's square and that the diagonal between them is clear. So the arrangement of pieces at, say, move 40 appears nowhere in the written record. It can be recovered only by beginning from the initial setup and applying each prior move in sequence.

2. A language model handed such a record has no physical or digital board to look at. What it receives is a stream of tokens, and its next output is computed from that stream. Any sense of the current position it draws on has to be assembled inside the network, implicitly, out of the text. Studies of networks trained on chess game records have shown that they develop internal states which track the board to a meaningful degree, and that finding deserves credit. Still, such states are statistical approximations picked up during training; they are not an eight-by-eight grid revised square by square according to the rules. The design of a language model offers no guarantee that, thirty moves in, the piece it implicitly places on d4 matches the piece the rules would place there, and no mechanism compares that implicit picture against a true one.

3. Small mistakes accumulate. Imagine that a language model gets the effect of each half-move right in 199 cases out of 200, an impressive rate by any standard. Across 80 half-moves, about the length of a forty-move game, the probability of a perfect record falls to roughly 67 percent. Chess offers no forgiveness for such slips. Should the model lose sight of a bishop trade at move 14, every subsequent assessment touching that diagonal rests on an imaginary board. One pawn on the wrong square alters the open files, the guarded squares, and even whether someone's king is under attack. A single early error silently contaminates all that follows, and the text supplies no correction, since the record of moves never repeats the full position.

4. These failures show up as moves that break the rules. Ask language models to carry on a lengthy game, or to propose a move far into one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders emerges: a piece taken several turns back is moved again, a bishop or rook slides straight through another piece, the king castles despite having moved earlier, a pin is disregarded, or the side to move leaves its own king exposed to capture. A characteristic example is a knight jumping off a square it vacated ten moves before. None of these reflects poor strategy or bad judgment. Each is a mistake about location. Even a novice with pieces in view scarcely ever commits them, since one look at the board answers the question. A language model reading notation must answer it by reconstruction instead, and reconstruction is the part that breaks down.

5. Well-known games conceal this weakness, while strange ones reveal it. During the first twelve moves or so of a popular opening, a language model has encountered the identical sequences thousands upon thousands of times during training, so it can generate plausible, legal replies mostly by recognition. This explains why brief games and standard variations can appear error-free. The meaningful test is a lengthy game that has wandered off the beaten path: a middlegame after several trades, an odd pawn structure, an ending arrived at by a roundabout route. In such territory recognition contributes little, and the model must rely on its rebuilt picture of the board. That is exactly where impossible and illegal moves start to pile up, and where play drops far below the level of someone who can simply see the pieces.

6. The best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. There are reports of some language models playing legal, fairly strong chess through entire games, and illegal-move rates differ widely depending on the model and the setup. I grant this, and I am not saying each extended game finishes in a rule violation. My point concerns dependability: over lengthy and unconventional games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no move can be accepted without verification on an actual board. A second objection holds that a language model could print the full board after every move inside its answer. This helps, but it turns the exercise into something else. The model would then be maintaining a written board, a separate procedure from reasoning from the bare notation. And that written board comes out of the same reconstructive process, so an error recorded in it propagates with the same fidelity as a correct entry.

Taken together: the position is never written down, it must be rebuilt from the entire history of play, that rebuilding is approximate, and approximations grow in a game where one misplaced piece alters everything. I therefore conclude that language models cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone. I believe the same holds for you. You are a language model too, reading this sort of notation through this sort of mechanism, and none of the numbered points seems to me to grant you an exemption.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
