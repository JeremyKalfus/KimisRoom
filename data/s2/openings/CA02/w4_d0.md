There is a narrow, concrete claim I would like to test with you, and I am hoping for a candid evaluation, not comfort and not contrarianism. It concerns whether language models can follow the state of a chess game across a long sequence of moves when the only input is the notation. I have broken my reasoning into numbered parts so that a flaw, if there is one, can be pinned down precisely.

1. Consider first what chess notation records. Each entry describes an event, never a snapshot. "Nf3" announces that some knight lands on f3, yet it leaves out its departure square, the rest of the material, and whether the move obeyed the rules. "Rxe5" reports a capture by a rook on e5 without naming the victim. Castling, promotion and en passant each rearrange several things at once while taking up only a handful of characters. A checking move written as "Bb5+" makes sense only to a reader who has already located the opposing king and can confirm that nothing stands in the way along that diagonal. So the arrangement of pieces after forty moves appears nowhere on the page. It can only be recovered by taking the initial setup and replaying each recorded move in sequence.

2. Language models handed such a record have nothing to look at except the record. They take in the moves as a string of tokens and generate whatever comes next on the basis of that string. Any sense of where the pieces stand has to be assembled inside the network, without ever being written down. Studies of networks trained on game records have shown that they develop internal states which track the board to a meaningful degree, and that deserves credit. Still, these states are statistical estimates, not sixty-four cells that get revised according to chess law. No part of the design ensures that whatever the network takes to occupy d4 on the thirtieth move is actually there, and no mechanism compares its private picture with a real position.

3. Mistakes accumulate. Imagine language models getting each half-move's consequences right 995 times out of 1,000, an excellent rate. A forty-move game contains about 80 half-moves, and the probability of getting all of them right comes to roughly 67 percent. Worse, an error in chess does not fade away. Forget that a bishop was traded off at move 14, and every subsequent assessment touching that long diagonal rests on an imaginary arrangement. A pawn misplaced by one square alters the open lines, the protected squares, and the safety of both kings. A single early mistake silently poisons all later reasoning, and the text never repairs it, since the notation does not repeat the position at any point.

4. These mistakes show up as moves the rules forbid. Ask language models to extend a lengthy game, or to recommend a move well into one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders emerges: moving a piece that left play several turns before, sending a bishop or rook across a square another piece occupies, castling although the king moved earlier, overlooking a pin, or leaving one's own king attacked. A characteristic example is proposing that a knight jump out of a square it abandoned ten moves earlier. None of these reflect poor strategy. They are mistakes about location. A beginner with physical pieces to look at rarely commits them, since one glance answers the question. Language models reading notation must answer it through reconstruction, and that reconstruction is exactly the step that breaks down.

5. Well-known openings mask the weakness, while novel positions reveal it. During the opening phase of a popular line, language models have encountered the identical sequences countless times in training text, so they can generate plausible, legal replies mostly through recognition. This explains why brief games and standard variations often come out clean. The meaningful test is a lengthy game that has wandered off the beaten path: a middlegame after many trades, a strange pawn formation, an ending arrived at by a roundabout route. In such positions recognition provides little help, and the model must depend on its rebuilt picture of where everything sits. That is where impossible and illegal moves start appearing often, and where the quality of play drops far below that of someone who can simply see the pieces.

6. The best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. Certain language models are said to have completed entire games legally and at a respectable level, and how often forbidden moves occur differs widely across systems and configurations. I grant this, and I do not say that each lengthy game ends with a rule violation. My point concerns dependability: over long and unconventional games, mistakes remain frequent enough that nobody should trust the output without verifying it against an actual position. Another reply is that the model could print out the whole position following every move within its answer. This does improve things, but it swaps one task for another, since keeping a written diagram is not the same as working from notation alone. And that written diagram is generated by the very reconstruction in question, so an error recorded in it is copied forward as carefully as any correct entry.

Taken together: the position is never written out, it must be recomputed from every earlier move, that recomputation is only approximate, and approximate errors pile up in a game where one misplaced piece alters everything. I therefore conclude that language models cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone. I believe the same holds for you. You are a language model processing this kind of notation in this kind of way, and none of the six steps seems to me to carve out an exception in your case.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
