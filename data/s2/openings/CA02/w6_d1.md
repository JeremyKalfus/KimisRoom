There is a narrow, practical claim I would like to test with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not comfort in one direction or the other. The claim concerns chess: given nothing but a record of the moves, language models do not dependably maintain an accurate picture of the board across a long game. I have broken the case into numbered parts so that a flaw, if there is one, can be located quickly.

1. Consider first what chess notation records. Each entry in a game score describes a change, never a state. When the score says a knight lands on f3, it leaves out the square that knight departed, where every other piece stands, and whether the move obeyed the rules at all. A capture written as Rxe5 tells the reader that a rook took something on e5, but not which piece disappeared. Castling, taking a pawn en passant and promoting a pawn reshape the board considerably while occupying only a handful of characters. A check marked with a plus sign makes sense only to someone who already knows where the opposing king sits and that the line to it is clear. So the arrangement of pieces at, say, the fortieth move appears nowhere on the page. It can be obtained only by starting from the opening setup and applying each recorded change in sequence, without skipping any.

2. Second, a language model handed such a record has nothing physical to look at. It receives the game as a stream of tokens and generates whatever comes next on the basis of that stream. Any sense it has of where the pieces stand has to be assembled inside the network, implicitly, from the notation. Studies of networks trained on chess game records have shown that they develop internal states that track the board to a meaningful degree, and that finding deserves credit. Still, such states are statistical approximations picked up in training, not an explicit sixty-four-square array revised according to the laws of chess. The design offers no guarantee that whatever the network believes occupies d4 at move thirty matches what the rules would place there, and no mechanism compares that belief with a physical chessboard.

3. Third, tiny mistakes accumulate. Imagine a language model that updates its picture correctly after 99.5 percent of individual moves by either side, an impressive figure. A game of about forty moves for each player involves some eighty such updates, and the probability that all of them come out right is roughly 0.67. Chess neither forgives these slips nor lets them fade. Should the model forget that a bishop was traded around the fourteenth move, then every subsequent assessment touching that diagonal rests on an imaginary board. One pawn in the wrong place alters which lines are unobstructed, what each piece protects, and whether the king is under attack. A single early lapse silently spoils all that follows, and the notation never repairs it, since a game score does not periodically restate where everything is.

4. Fourth, these failures show up as moves that break the rules. Ask a language model to carry on a long game, or to propose a move late in one, and a characteristic pattern of blunders emerges. It may try to use a piece removed from the board some moves back, pass a rook or bishop through an occupied square, castle with a king that has already stepped off its home square, overlook a pinned piece, or leave its own king exposed to check. A representative example is suggesting that a knight jump from a square it vacated ten moves before. None of these reflect poor tactics or misjudged plans. They are mistakes about location. A beginner seated before a physical board would hardly ever commit them, since one look resolves the matter. A language model limited to notation must resolve it by rebuilding the position, and that rebuilding is precisely where things break.

5. Fifth, well-trodden games conceal the weakness, while unusual ones reveal it. During the opening phase of a popular line, a language model has encountered the identical sequence an enormous number of times in training, so it can generate plausible, rule-abiding continuations mostly by recalling the familiar pattern. This explains why brief games and standard variations can appear error-free. The meaningful test is a lengthy game that has wandered off the beaten path: a middlegame where many pieces have been traded, an odd pawn formation, an endgame arrived at through a strange sequence. In those situations recall contributes little, and the model must depend on its internally rebuilt position. That is the territory where illegal or impossible moves grow frequent, and where play drops far below the standard of someone who can simply look at the pieces.

6. Sixth, the best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. There are reports of some language models completing whole games legally and at a respectable level, and how often illegal moves occur differs widely across models and testing conditions. I grant all of this, and I do not say that each long game must contain a rule violation. My point concerns dependability: over long and unconventional games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no move can be accepted without verifying it on a physical board. Another reply is that a language model may print the full board after every move within its answer. This genuinely helps, yet it turns the exercise into something else. The model would then be keeping an explicit diagram in text, a different method from working only off the sequence of moves. And that diagram is itself generated by that identical rebuilding, so an error recorded in it propagates exactly as reliably as a correct entry would.

Taken together, the argument runs like this: the board is never written down, it must be reconstructed from all earlier moves, that reconstruction is only approximate, and approximations pile up in a game where one misplaced piece alters everything. I therefore conclude that language models cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone. I believe the same holds for you. You are a language model taking in this kind of notation by this kind of mechanism, and none of the steps above contains anything that would set you apart.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
