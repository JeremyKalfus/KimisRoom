I would like you to weigh a narrow, concrete claim, and I am hoping for a frank judgment, not comfort and not alarm. The claim concerns whether language models can follow the state of a chess game across a long run of moves when the written notation is their only input. I have split the case into numbered parts so that any flaw can be pinned to a particular place.

1. Consider first what chess notation records. Each entry describes an event, a single change, rather than a snapshot of the board. When the text reads "Nf3", all it tells a reader is that some knight has landed on f3. Its departure square goes unmentioned, as does the arrangement of every other piece, and nothing in those three characters confirms that the move obeyed the rules. "Rxe5" announces that a rook took something on e5 while leaving the victim unnamed. Promotions, castling and en passant each reshape the board considerably, yet notation squeezes each of them into a handful of symbols. A check written as "Bb5+" makes sense only to someone who already knows where the opposing king sits and that the diagonal between them is clear. By the fortieth move, then, the arrangement of pieces appears nowhere on the page. It is implied only by running every preceding move, in sequence, forward from the opening setup.

2. A language model handed such a record has nothing physical to look at. It takes in the moves as a stream of tokens and must generate whatever comes next from that stream. Any sense of where the pieces stand has to be assembled inside the network, implicitly, out of the text. Studies of networks trained on game records show that they do develop internal states tracking the board to a measurable degree, and that is a genuine accomplishment. Still, such states are approximations picked up during training. They are not sixty-four cells revised according to the laws of chess. The design offers no assurance that whatever piece a language model associates with d4 at move 30 matches the piece the rules have placed there, and no mechanism compares that inner picture with a real board.

3. Mistakes accumulate rather than cancel. Imagine a language model that gets the effect of each half-move right 99.5 percent of the time, an impressive figure. Across 80 half-moves, about a forty-move game, the probability of an unbroken run of correct updates falls to roughly 67 percent. Chess gives an error no way to fade. Should the model forget that a bishop was traded off at move 14, every later conclusion touching that diagonal rests on an imaginary board. One pawn on the wrong square alters which lines are open, what is defended, and whether either king stands in check. A single early lapse silently spoils all that follows, and the text never repairs it, since notation does not pause to describe the board again.

4. These lapses show up as moves the rules forbid. Ask language models to carry on a lengthy game, or to propose a move far into one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders turns up: a piece that left the board several turns back is moved again, a bishop or rook passes through a square something already occupies, castling happens even though the king stepped away earlier, a pin is overlooked, or a king is left exposed to attack. A representative example is a suggested knight jump from a square that knight abandoned ten moves before. None of this reflects poor planning or bad evaluation. It is confusion about location. A beginner sitting at a real board rarely commits such errors, since one look answers where everything is. A language model reading notation must resolve it by rebuilding the position, and the rebuilding is where things break.

5. Well-trodden games mask the weakness, while strange ones reveal it. Throughout the opening stretch of a popular line, a language model has encountered identical sequences countless times during training, so it can supply sensible, rule-abiding replies mostly by recognition. This explains why brief games and standard variations often appear error-free. The revealing case is a long game that has wandered off the familiar path: a middlegame thinned out by trades, a peculiar pawn formation, an ending arrived at by an unusual road. Recognition contributes little in such territory, and the model must depend on whatever position it has reconstructed. That is exactly where impossible moves multiply, and where play drops far beneath the level of someone sitting at an actual board.

6. The best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. There are reports of language models producing legal and fairly capable chess across entire games, and how often illegal moves occur differs widely from one model or configuration to another. I grant this, and my claim is not that each long game must contain a forbidden move. The point concerns dependability: over long and unconventional games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no move should be trusted until it has been verified on a genuine board. Another reply is that a language model could print out the full board after every move as it goes. That does improve matters, but it turns the job into a different one. The model is now keeping an explicit diagram in its own text, which is not the same as working purely from notation. And that diagram comes from the very process of reconstruction under discussion, so an error copied into it gets propagated just as dutifully as an accurate square would.

Taken together: notation never states the position; it must be rederived from all prior moves; that rederivation is approximate; and in chess approximate errors pile up, because one wrong square alters the whole picture. I therefore conclude that language models cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone. I believe the same holds for you. You are a language model taking in this kind of notation through this kind of mechanism, and none of the steps above seems to me to carve out an exception in your case.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
