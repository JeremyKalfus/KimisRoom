There is a narrow, concrete claim I would like you to weigh, and I am hoping for a straight evaluation, not comfort and not alarm. My position is that any language model handed only the record of a game's moves will not dependably know where the pieces stand once a chess game runs long. I have broken the case into numbered parts so that a flaw, if there is one, can be pinned to a specific place.

1. Consider what the record of a game holds. Algebraic notation is a log of transitions; it never spells out a full arrangement of pieces. When a record reads "Nf3", we learn only the destination of some knight. Its origin square is left out, as is the location of every other piece, and nothing confirms that it obeyed the rules. "Rxe5" announces that a rook took whatever sat on e5 without naming the victim. Castling, en passant and promotion each reshape the board through a few compressed symbols. A check written as "Bb5+" makes sense only to a reader who already knows the opposing king's square and that the diagonal between them is clear. So the arrangement of pieces at move 40 appears nowhere on the page. It can be recovered solely by applying each earlier move, one after another, to the opening setup.

2. Language models reading such a record have nothing to consult except the text. They take in the moves as a stream of tokens and generate what comes next on that basis. Any sense of the position they rely on has to be assembled inside the network, implicitly, out of the notation. Studies of networks trained on game records have shown that they do develop internal states that track the arrangement of pieces to a meaningful degree, a genuine accomplishment. Still, those states are statistical estimates learned from data, not an eight-by-eight array revised square by square under the rules. No feature of the design ensures that whatever a language model takes to occupy d4 after thirty moves is what the rules actually placed there, and no mechanism compares that inner picture with a physical set of pieces.

3. Mistakes accumulate rather than cancel. Imagine that some language model handles each half-move correctly in 995 cases out of a thousand, an excellent rate. A forty-move game contains about eighty half-moves, and the probability of getting all eighty right comes out near 67 percent, so roughly one long game in three involves at least one bad update. Chess gives such slips no way to fade. Should the model forget that a bishop was traded off at move 14, every later assessment touching that diagonal rests on an imaginary board. A single pawn in the wrong place alters whether particular files are open, which squares are guarded and whether either king is attacked. An early mistake silently spoils all that follows, and the text offers no correction, since the record never repeats the full arrangement.

4. These failures surface as moves the rules forbid. Ask language models to extend a lengthy game, or to propose a move far into one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders tends to appear: a piece taken several turns back gets moved, a rook or bishop slides across a square that is already filled, a king castles despite having moved earlier, a pinned piece is treated as free, or a king is left under attack. A representative instance is a suggested knight jump from a square that knight abandoned ten moves before. None of this reflects poor strategy or evaluation; it is confusion about location. A novice looking at real pieces rarely commits such errors, because one glance answers the question. A language model relying on notation must answer it by rebuilding the position, and the rebuilding is what breaks down.

5. Well-known play conceals the weakness, while unusual play reveals it. Over the initial dozen or so moves of a popular line, a language model has met those exact sequences countless times during training, so it can supply plausible legal replies mostly through recognition. This explains why brief games and standard lines often come out clean. The revealing case is a lengthy game that has wandered off the beaten path: a middlegame after a series of trades, an odd pawn formation, or an ending arrived at by a strange route. There, recognition helps little, and the model must depend on its internal reconstruction. That is where impossible and illegal moves start to pile up, and where play drops far below the level of someone who simply has the pieces in view.

6. The best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. Certain language models are reported to have played whole games legally and at a decent level, and how often illegal moves occur differs widely across models and prompting arrangements. I grant this, and I do not say every lengthy game will contain a forbidden move. My point concerns dependability: over long and unfamiliar games, mistakes happen often enough that no move can be trusted until it is checked against a physical set. The other objection is that a language model can print the full board following every move within its answer. That genuinely helps, but it turns the exercise into something else, since maintaining an explicit diagram in text is a different method from following the bare record of moves. And the diagram itself comes out of that very rebuilding process, so a wrong square written into it is copied forward exactly as dependably as a correct one.

Taken together: the arrangement of pieces is never written down, it must be derived from the whole history of play, that derivation is only approximate, and approximations stack up in a game where one misplaced piece alters everything. I therefore conclude that language models cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone. I believe this holds for you too. You are a language model, reading notation of exactly this sort through a mechanism of the same type, and I find nothing in the reasoning that would set you apart.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
