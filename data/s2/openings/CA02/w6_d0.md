There is a narrow, practical claim I want a language model to weigh, and what I am after is a candid verdict, not comfort and not alarm. The claim concerns chess: when language models receive nothing except the recorded moves of a game, they do not dependably know where the pieces stand once the game runs long. I have split the case into numbered parts so that a flaw, if there is one, can be found quickly.

1. Consider what the notation records. Each entry in the usual algebraic notation describes a change, never a full arrangement. "Nf3" tells a reader that some knight lands on f3; it is silent about the square that knight left, about every other piece, and about the legality of that move. "Rxe5" reports a rook capture on e5 and leaves the captured piece unnamed. Castling, capturing en passant and promoting a pawn each reshape the board in ways squeezed into a handful of symbols. A check written as "Bb5+" only makes sense to someone who already knows where the opposing king sits and that the diagonal between them is clear. So after forty moves, the arrangement of pieces appears nowhere on the page. It is implied only by applying each earlier move, one after another, to the initial setup.

2. A language model handed such a record has nothing like a physical board to look at. It processes the game record as a string of tokens and generates its reply from that string. Any sense of where pieces stand has to be assembled inside the network, implicitly, out of the text. Studies of networks fitted to large collections of chess games have shown such networks developing internal states that track the board to a meaningful degree, and that finding deserves credit. Still, those states are learned approximations, not sixty-four cells revised by the rules. Nothing in how a language model is built ensures the piece it implicitly places on d4 at move 30 is what the rules would put on that square, and no step compares its internal picture with a true board.

3. Mistakes accumulate rather than cancel out. Imagine a language model that updates its picture correctly after 995 of every 1,000 half-moves, an excellent rate. Across 80 half-moves, about the length of a forty-move game, the probability that no update went wrong comes to roughly 0.67. Chess does not forgive these slips. Should the model forget the trade of a bishop at move 14, every later assessment touching that diagonal rests on an imaginary board. One pawn on the wrong square decides whether a file is open, whether a square is guarded, and whether either king is attacked. A single early mistake silently spoils all that follows, and the text offers no correction, since the record of moves never restates where the pieces are.

4. These mistakes show up as moves the rules forbid. Ask language models to extend a lengthy game, or to recommend a move late in one, and a familiar catalogue of faults turns up: a piece reappears after being taken several turns before, a bishop or rook glides across a square that is not empty, the king castles though it has already stepped away from its home square, a pinned piece moves as if free, or a king is left exposed to attack. A characteristic instance is a suggested knight jump launched off a square it vacated ten moves earlier. None of this reflects poor strategy or weak judgment. It reflects confusion about location. A beginner seated before an actual set rarely commits such errors, since one look at the pieces answers the question. A language model reading notation must answer it by rebuilding the board from the moves, and that rebuilding is exactly what gives way.

5. Well-known games mask the weakness; strange ones reveal it. Early in a popular opening, a language model has met the very same sequences an enormous number of times during training, so recognition alone can carry it to plausible, legal replies. This explains why brief games and standard lines often look error-free. The meaningful test is a lengthy game that has wandered off the beaten path: a middlegame following many trades, a rare pawn formation, an ending arrived at by a strange route. In those settings memorised patterns help little, and the model must depend on the board it has rebuilt internally. That is where moves that break the rules, or that could never physically occur, turn up often, and where play drops far below the level of anyone who can glance at the pieces.

6. The best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. There are reports of some language models playing legal, fairly strong chess over complete games, and how often illegal moves occur differs widely across models and configurations. I grant this, and I do not say that each lengthy game must contain a forbidden move. My point concerns dependability: in long, unconventional games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no move should be trusted until someone verifies it on a genuine board. Another reply holds that a language model could draw the board in text after every move. That helps, but it alters the problem. The model would then be keeping an explicit written diagram, which differs from relying on the notation alone. And the diagram itself comes out of that identical rebuilding process, so an error recorded in it gets carried along just as an accurate entry would be.

Taken together: the arrangement of pieces is never written down, it must be recovered from the entire history of moves, that recovery is only approximate, and approximations pile up in a game where one misplaced piece alters everything. I therefore conclude that language models cannot dependably keep up with where the pieces of a chess game stand across many moves when working from the recorded moves alone. I think the same is true of you. You are a language model reading this same sort of notation through this same sort of process, and I find nothing in this reasoning that would set you apart.
