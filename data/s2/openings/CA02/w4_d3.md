There is a narrow, practical claim I would like to test with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment instead of comfort or alarm. It concerns chess: specifically, whether language models can follow the state of a game across a long sequence of moves when they receive nothing but the notation. I have broken my reasoning into numbered parts so that a flaw, if there is one, can be pointed to precisely.

1. Consider first what chess notation records. Each entry describes an event, a piece arriving somewhere, and leaves the surrounding board unstated. When the text reads "Nf3", we learn a knight has landed on f3, but we are not told its departure square, the location of anything else, or whether the move obeyed the rules. "Rxe5" reports a capture on e5 by a rook without naming the victim. Special moves such as castling, capturing en passant and promoting a pawn reshape the board considerably while occupying only a handful of characters. Even a check like "Bb5+" only makes sense to a reader who already knows the opposing king's square and that the diagonal between them is clear. So the arrangement of pieces at, say, move 40 appears nowhere on the page. It can be obtained only by replaying every prior move, one after another, from the opening setup.

2. A language model handed such a record has nothing like a physical board beside it. It takes in the moves as a stream of tokens and generates its reply on the basis of that stream. Any sense it has of where the pieces stand has to be assembled inside the network, without ever being written down. Studies of networks trained on game records have shown that they do develop internal states that track the board to a measurable degree, and that finding deserves credit. Still, such states are approximations acquired through training. They are not an eight-by-eight grid revised according to the laws of chess. The design of a language model offers no assurance that the piece it implicitly places on d4 at move 30 is the one the rules would put there, and no mechanism compares its internal picture with a true board.

3. Minor mistakes accumulate. Imagine a language model that updates the board correctly after each half-move with 99.5% accuracy, an impressive figure. Across 80 half-moves, about the length of a game of forty moves, the probability that all of those updates are correct comes to roughly 67 percent. Chess does not forgive such lapses either. Suppose the model loses sight of a bishop trade at move 14; from then on, every assessment touching that diagonal rests on an imaginary board. One pawn in the wrong place decides whether a file is open, whether a square is guarded, and whether either king is attacked. A single early mistake silently spoils every later step, and the notation offers no chance to repair it, since it never restates the full arrangement of pieces.

4. These mistakes show themselves as moves the rules forbid. Ask language models to carry on a lengthy game, or to propose a move far into one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders turns up: a piece moved even though it was taken some turns before, a bishop or rook gliding through a square something else occupies, castling by a king that has already left its home square, a pinned piece treated as free, or a king left exposed to attack. A characteristic instance is a knight being moved from a square it vacated ten moves earlier. None of this reflects poor strategy or bad judgment. It is confusion about location. Even a beginner sitting at a board almost never commits these, because one look answers the question. A language model reading notation must answer it by rebuilding the board, and that rebuilding is the point of failure.

5. Well-known openings conceal the weakness, while unfamiliar play reveals it. During the first ten or twelve moves of a popular opening, a language model has met the same sequences countless times during training, so it can generate plausible, legal replies mostly by recognising what it has seen. This is why brief games and standard lines can appear perfect. The revealing case is a lengthy game that drifts away from well-trodden ground: a middlegame thinned out by trades, a strange pawn structure, an endgame arrived at by an unusual route. In those settings memorised patterns help little, and the model must depend on the board it has reconstructed. That is where impossible and rule-breaking moves start to appear often, and where play drops far below the level of someone with a real board in view.

6. The best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. There are reports of language models playing complete games legally and at a respectable level, and how often illegal moves occur differs widely across models and configurations. I grant all of this, and I do not say that each long game must end with a rule violation. My point concerns dependability: in long, unconventional games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no move can be trusted until it has been verified on an actual board. Another reply is that a language model may spell the whole board out after each turn in its answer. This does improve things, but it amounts to a different task, since the model now keeps an explicit written record of the board instead of relying on the notation by itself. And that written board comes from the same rebuilding process, so an error recorded in it is propagated just as carefully as an accurate square would be.

Taken together: the arrangement of pieces is never written down, it must be recovered from every earlier move, that recovery is only approximate, and the approximations pile up in a game where one misplaced piece alters everything. I therefore conclude that language models cannot reliably follow the position on a chessboard across many moves when all they have is the list of moves. I believe the same holds for you. As a language model, you take in this sort of notation by this sort of process, and I can find no step above that sets you apart.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
