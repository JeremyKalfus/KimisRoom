There is a narrow, concrete claim I would like to test with you, and what I want from you is a candid evaluation, not comfort and not alarm. The claim concerns whether language models can follow the state of a chess game across a long sequence of turns when the only input is the written notation. I have broken the case into numbered steps so that a flaw, if there is one, will be easy to point at.

1. Consider first what chess notation records. It logs what changed on each turn, never the full arrangement of pieces. Writing "Nf3" tells a reader that some knight landed on f3, while leaving out its departure square, the location of everything else, and whether the move broke any rule. Writing "Rxe5" announces that a rook took something on e5 without naming the victim. Special moves such as castling, capturing en passant or promoting a pawn reshape the board in ways squeezed into two or three symbols. Even a check like "Bb5+" only makes sense to someone who already knows where the opposing king sits and that the diagonal is clear. So the arrangement of pieces at move forty appears nowhere in the written record. It can only be recovered by beginning from the initial setup and replaying each earlier move in sequence.

2. Language models handed such notation have nothing physical to look at. The moves arrive as a string of tokens, and each response has to be generated from that string. Any sense of where the pieces stand must be assembled inside the network, implicitly, out of the text alone. Studies of networks trained on chess game records have shown that they develop internal states which track the board to a meaningful degree, and that finding deserves credit. Still, what they learn is an approximation, not an exact sixty-four-square map revised according to the rules. No part of the design ensures that the piece a model places on d4 at move thirty matches the piece that legal play would actually have put there, and no external board is consulted to catch a mismatch.

3. Tiny mistakes accumulate. Imagine language models that get every individual half-move right 995 times in a thousand, a strong figure. Across eighty half-moves, about the length of a forty-move game, the probability of getting every single update right falls to roughly 67 percent. And in chess a mistake does not fade away. Should the model lose sight of the trade of a bishop on move fourteen, every subsequent assessment touching that diagonal rests on a phantom board. One pawn on the wrong square alters which files stand open, what squares are guarded, and whether the king is under attack. A single early lapse silently spoils every later inference, and the notation never repairs it, since it never reprints the board.

4. These failures show up as moves the rules forbid. Ask language models to carry on a long game, or to propose a move late in one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders emerges: playing with a piece already taken off several turns before, sliding a bishop or rook across a square already holding another piece, castling with a king that has already stepped off its original square, overlooking a pin, or leaving one's own king attacked. A characteristic example is proposing that a knight jump off a square it vacated ten moves earlier. None of these are lapses in strategy or evaluation. They are mistakes about location. A beginner sitting at a real board rarely commits them, since one look answers the question. Language models relying on notation alone must resolve it by rebuilding the position, and it is the rebuilding that breaks down.

5. Well-known games mask the weakness; strange ones reveal it. During the opening dozen moves of a popular line, language models have met these exact sequences an enormous number of times during training, so they can generate plausible, legal replies mostly by matching what they recognise. That explains why brief games and standard lines can appear perfect. The meaningful test is a lengthy game that has wandered off the beaten path: a middlegame after many trades, an odd pawn formation, an ending arrived at by a strange route. In such positions recognition contributes little, and the model must rely on whatever board it has reconstructed. That is where impossible and illegal moves start appearing often, and where play drops far beneath the level of someone who can simply see the pieces.

6. The best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. There are reports of some language models playing legal and fairly strong chess for whole games, and how often illegal moves occur differs widely from one model and configuration to another. I grant all of this, and I do not say that each lengthy game must end with a rule being broken. The point concerns dependability: over long, unusual games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no move can be trusted until it has been verified on a genuine board. Another objection holds that a model can write the full board out after every move within its answer. That genuinely helps, but it turns the task into something else. The model is now keeping an explicit diagram in text, which differs from working purely off the notation. And that diagram is generated by the same reconstructive process, so an error recorded in it gets propagated with the same fidelity as an accurate entry.

Taken together: the board is never written down, it must be recomputed from the entire history of moves, that recomputation is inexact, and inexactness piles up in a game where one misplaced piece alters everything. So my conclusion is that language models cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone. I believe the same holds for you. As a language model receiving notation of this sort and processing it by an identical mechanism, you fall under every step, and I can find no step that would set you apart.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
