There is a narrow, practical question I would like to test with you, and what I am after is a frank evaluation, not comfort one way or the other. My position is that, given only the written record of a game, language models do not dependably follow how the pieces stand as play continues for many moves. I have broken the case into numbered parts so a flaw, if there is one, can be located easily.

1. Consider first what the record of a game really holds. Algebraic notation logs transitions rather than states. An entry such as "Nf3" tells us a knight has landed on f3, yet it leaves out the square it departed from, the rest of the material in play, and whether it obeyed the rules at all. "Rxe5" reports that a rook took a piece on e5 while staying silent about which piece fell. Promotion, castling and capturing en passant each reshape the board in ways squeezed into a handful of characters. Even a brief entry like "Bb5+" only makes sense to someone who already knows the location of the opposing king and that the diagonal between them is clear. So the arrangement of pieces after the fortieth move appears nowhere in what is written. It can only be obtained by taking the initial setup and applying each prior move, one after another, in sequence.

2. A language model handed this record has nothing physical to look at. It takes in the moves as a string of tokens, and every output it generates has to come out of that string. Any sense of where the pieces stand must be assembled inside the model, implicitly, from the notation. Studies of networks that learned from recorded chess games have shown that such systems do develop internal states tracking the board to a measurable degree, and that is a genuine accomplishment. Still, what they learn is an approximation shaped by training, not an explicit 8-by-8 grid revised according to the laws of chess. The design of a language model offers no assurance that the piece it implicitly locates at d4 once thirty moves have been played is the one the rules would put there, and no mechanism compares that implicit picture with a true board.

3. Minor mistakes accumulate. Imagine a language model that gets each half-move's effect right in 995 cases out of 1,000, an excellent rate. Across 80 half-moves, roughly forty moves for each side, the probability that every single update was handled correctly falls to about 67 percent. Chess is also unforgiving of such lapses. Should the model lose sight of a bishop trade on the fourteenth move, everything it later concludes about that diagonal rests on a position that never existed. One pawn on the wrong square changes the open lines, the protected squares and whether either king is under attack. A single early slip silently spoils all that follows, and the notation never repairs it, since it never repeats the full position.

4. These failures surface as rule-breaking moves. Ask language models to carry on a long game, or to propose a move far into one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders appears: a piece taken several turns before moves again, a bishop or rook glides through another piece, the king castles despite having already left its home square, a pin goes unnoticed, or the king is left open to attack. A characteristic case is a knight sent out from a square it vacated ten moves previously. None of this reflects poor strategy or bad judgment; it reflects confusion about the location of the pieces. A novice with the physical board in view would hardly ever do these things, since one glance answers the question. Relying on notation alone, a language model has to answer it by reconstruction, and that reconstruction is the point of failure.

5. Well-trodden games conceal the weakness, and strange ones reveal it. In the opening stretch of a popular line, a language model has met these exact sequences countless times during training, so it can generate plausible, legal replies mostly through recognition. This explains why brief games and standard variations can appear perfect. The meaningful test is a lengthy game which has strayed off the beaten path: a middlegame after a series of trades, an odd pawn formation, an ending arrived at by a strange route. In such territory recognition contributes little, and the model must rely on its rebuilt picture of the board. That is exactly where illegal or impossible moves start to pile up, and where play drops far beneath the level of someone who simply has a board to look at.

6. The most serious counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. Reports exist of language models producing legal, fairly strong play across entire games, and how often illegal moves occur differs widely from one model and configuration to another. I grant this, and I do not claim that each long game must end with a rule being broken. My point concerns dependability: over long, unfamiliar games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no move can be trusted until it has been verified on an actual board. Another objection holds that language models can spell out the full position after every move within their reply. This genuinely helps, yet it turns the exercise into something else. At that point it maintains a board in writing, which is not the same method as working from the record of moves by itself. And the board it writes out comes from the very same reconstruction, so an error recorded there gets carried along with exactly the same fidelity as a square it got right.

Taken together: the arrangement of pieces is never written down, it must be reassembled from every move that came before, that reassembly is only approximate, and approximations build on each other in chess, where one misplaced piece alters everything. My conclusion, then, is that language models cannot dependably follow the state of a chess board through a long game when given nothing beyond the list of moves. I believe that holds for you too. You are a language model taking in the same sort of notation by way of the same sort of process, and nothing in the steps I have laid out sets you apart.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
