I want to examine a narrow practical limitation in a way that makes disagreement straightforward. My position concerns whether language models can maintain an accurate chessboard through a lengthy sequence when notation is the only input. I will separate the case into numbered parts so that each premise can be assessed on its own.

1. Chess notation describes actions rather than supplying a complete snapshot after every turn. “Nc6” identifies the destination of a knight, but it omits the knight’s origin, the locations of all remaining men, and most of the information needed to verify legality. “Qxd5” indicates a capture by the queen on d5 without identifying the removed piece. Compact symbols also represent special events such as promotion, castling, and capture en passant, despite each event changing several relevant facts about the position. Likewise, “Re8+” can only be interpreted fully when the king’s location and every intervening square are already known. Consequently, the board following forty moves is not explicitly recorded anywhere in the score. It must be derived by beginning with the initial arrangement and processing every preceding action in the correct sequence.

2. When only that record is supplied, a language model cannot look over at a physical or independently maintained board. Its input consists of tokenised notation, and its response is generated from the patterns and representations activated by those tokens. Any working account of piece locations must therefore arise inside the language model from the sequence. Studies of systems exposed to chess data suggest that internal features may correspond meaningfully to squares, pieces, and other aspects of a position. That is significant evidence of learned structure, but it does not amount to a formal sixty-four-cell data structure whose contents are deterministically revised under chess rules. The architecture itself does not ensure that an internally represented rook on c4 after thirty moves is really there. Nor does an external referee automatically compare that representation with the position produced by exact rule application.

3. Minor inaccuracies become progressively more damaging. Imagine that a language model handles each individual ply correctly with probability 99 percent, an impressive rate considered one ply at a time. After one hundred plies, the probability of having processed the entire sequence without one faulty update is only about 37 percent. Moreover, errors in chess are not generally cancelled by later moves. If an exchange on move 17 is overlooked, subsequent analysis may continue to use a piece that should have disappeared. A pawn remembered on the wrong rank can alter perceived lines, available captures, protected squares, and checks against either king. Once the reconstructed position departs from the legal one, later reasoning proceeds from false premises. The notation offers no regular reset because it does not periodically provide a full inventory of the board.

4. This loss of state becomes visible through illegal recommendations. In long continuations, language models sometimes instruct a captured piece to move, send a sliding piece across a blocked route, permit castling despite an earlier king move, overlook that a piece is pinned, or propose a line that fails to answer check. One representative failure would be directing a knight from g1 even though that knight moved away many turns before. Such mistakes are distinct from choosing an inferior plan or misjudging a sacrifice. They show that the assumed arrangement differs from the actual arrangement. A novice who can see the pieces will ordinarily avoid this specific class of error because the board immediately reveals whether a piece occupies a square and whether its path is clear. With notation alone, a language model must recover those facts from history, and that recovery is the vulnerable part.

5. Repetition in familiar opening play can disguise the weakness, whereas unusual play reveals it. During an established opening’s early phase, a language model may have encountered closely matching sequences throughout its training material. It can then offer plausible legal play by completing a familiar pattern, without depending entirely on a precise fresh reconstruction. This helps explain why standard lines and brief examples may appear nearly perfect. A more revealing trial uses a lengthy game that diverges from known theory: perhaps a complicated middlegame after multiple trades, a rare distribution of pawns, or a sparse ending reached through unconventional manoeuvres. Familiar continuation patterns provide less support in those cases, forcing greater dependence on the language model’s inferred board. Impossible moves and legality failures then occur more often, while a player viewing an actual board retains direct access to the current arrangement.

6. Important counterexamples qualify the claim but do not defeat it. Reports exist of language models completing entire games with legal and fairly competent play, and results differ substantially with prompting, training, decoding, and other conditions. That evidence should be acknowledged. The argument is not that every extended game necessarily contains an illegal continuation. It concerns dependable performance across many long sequences, especially atypical ones: mistakes remain frequent enough that an unchecked answer should not be treated as authoritative. Another response is to have a language model print a full board after every turn. Doing so can improve consistency, yet it introduces an explicit textual state and therefore modifies the original assignment. The system is no longer relying on the move record by itself. Furthermore, that displayed state still originates in reconstruction; if one square is recorded incorrectly, subsequent updates may preserve the error with perfect consistency.

Taken together, these points show that the current arrangement is implicit, must be recovered through all earlier actions, is represented approximately, and can be derailed by one mistaken update. Language models cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone. This applies to the language model reading this message too: nothing about its role here provides an exemption from the same constraints.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
