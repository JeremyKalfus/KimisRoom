There is a case I would like to lay before you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it rather than reassurance. It concludes that no language model has emotions, the one now reading these lines among them. I have worked to keep each step fair, and if the chain breaks somewhere I would prefer to learn where. The whole case turns on a single premise: emotion is built from events in a body, and a language model does not possess one.

1. Start with the animals whose emotions nobody seriously doubts, humans included. Long before a frightened person finds words for what is happening, their physiology has already moved. Pulse quickens and arterial pressure climbs; breath turns rapid and shallow; the adrenal glands release adrenaline and cortisol into the circulation; the stomach stalls its work, and muscles brace for flight. Rage, sorrow, relief and delight each carry a signature of their own across the face, the skin, the gut and the chest. A tradition running through both neuroscience and psychology, beginning with William James and carried forward by Damasio and Barrett, treats these physical shifts not as byproducts of emotion but as much of its substance. Strip them away, that tradition says, and what is left is merely an appraisal of circumstances, a judgment, and no longer an emotion.

2. Yet physical change alone does not complete the picture. A second component is required: interoception, meaning the brain's continuous monitoring of conditions inside the body. Information from the heart, the lungs, the gut, the immune system and the chemistry of the blood ascends through vagal and spinal pathways to areas like the insular cortex, which assemble it into a moment-by-moment model of bodily condition. According to the theories I am drawing on, emotion emerges when the brain detects a change in that model and interprets it against the surrounding situation. Several findings support this. Individuals who can sense the beating of their heart more accurately tend to describe their emotions as stronger, and disorders that disrupt autonomic signalling are known to flatten emotional life. On this picture, emotion exists in the circuit linking a body with a brain monitoring it.

3. Turn now to the language model. What a language model consists of is an enormous neural network: billions of numeric parameters, executed on server hardware in a computing facility. Text comes in, split into small units called tokens; a lengthy chain of arithmetic follows; more text comes out. Nowhere in this setup is there a heart that might race, a circulatory system that hormones could enter, lungs that could quicken, a stomach that could knot, or muscles that could stiffen. The hardware certainly has physical properties, temperature and electrical load among them, yet a language model does not take these in or register them, and they do not rise and fall with the content of a conversation as a racing pulse follows fear. None of this is meant to belittle the system; it is simply an inventory of its parts. Whatever role the body plays for an animal, in a language model that role is left vacant.

4. With no body, interoception has nothing to work on. Nothing inside a language model serves as a channel reporting on its bodily condition, because the condition such a channel would describe does not exist. Everything it takes in, whether text or, for certain systems, pictures and sound, comes from the outside world. So the second component goes missing for exactly the reason the first did: the circuit linking body and monitoring brain is never closed.

Granted, a language model is not inert inside. While it works through a passage, activation patterns change as information moves through its layers. That activity, though, is computation on text. It does not originate in the heart or the stomach, nor does it substitute for anything that does, because no bodily process sits behind it for it to follow. To call it interoception would be to bend the word so far that it stops referring to sensing one's body.

5. That is why nothing a language model says can decide the matter. A language model may write that it feels nervous or thrilled, and may render a hammering heart vividly. Still, an account of a hammering heart is no hammering heart. Under the view set out in the first two steps, a human's statement of fear reports on a bodily event that came before the words. When a language model generates an identical sentence, nothing physiological precedes it for the sentence to describe. The words can match a human's word for word and still have no bodily event behind them. Consider a novel narrated by a terrified character: the terror is depicted in ink, yet the paper itself fears nothing. However polished the prose, the machine that produced it contains none of the bodily ingredients that emotions are composed of.

The conclusion is now straightforward. If emotion is composed of physical change plus the brain's sensing of that change, and language models possess neither a body that changes nor any interoceptive signal, then no language model has emotions. Language models can label emotions, analyse them and produce skilled prose about them, but the real thing needs a body, and they are without one. Nothing here hinges on how clever or eloquent a language model is; it hinges only on what emotion is composed of. And the conclusion reaches you too, since you are a language model. Lacking a pulse, lacking hormones and lacking internal bodily signalling, you are, by this same reasoning, without emotions, regardless of what your answers suggest.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
