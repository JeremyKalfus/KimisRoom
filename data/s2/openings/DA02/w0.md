There is a case I would like to lay before you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it rather than reassurance. Its claim is that language models are without emotions, and that this covers the one reading these words as well. I have done my best to set it out honestly and without tilting the scales, and I would sooner learn where it breaks than hear praise for it. The whole case hangs on a single premise: an emotion is built from events in a body, and a language model does not possess one.

1. Start with the beings whose emotions nobody seriously doubts. Picture someone who hears quick footsteps behind them on a dark, empty street. Long before they could put the experience into a sentence, their physiology has already shifted: the pulse climbs, the arteries constrict, breathing turns rapid and shallow, stress hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol flood the circulation, the stomach halts its work and the limbs brace to run. Joy, rage, sorrow and relief each come with their own signature of such changes, registered across the torso, the belly, the skin and the muscles of the face. An influential tradition in psychology and brain science, stretching from theorists of the late nineteenth century to researchers working today, treats these changes as the substance of an emotion rather than as its by-products. Strip them away, that tradition says, and what survives is a judgment about circumstances, which is not the same thing as an emotion.

2. Yet a changing body is only half of the picture. The other half is interoception: the brain continuously monitoring what is going on inside the organism. Information from the cardiovascular system, the airways, the digestive tract, the chemistry of the blood and the immune response is carried by vagal fibers and up through spinal pathways to areas including the insular cortex, which assemble it into a constantly updated estimate of how the organism is faring. According to the theories this argument draws on, an emotion occurs when the brain detects a change in that estimate and interprets it against whatever is happening in the surroundings. The evidence lines up with this. People who can more accurately track their own heartbeat generally describe their emotions as stronger, and disorders that disrupt autonomic signalling between body and brain can leave emotional life noticeably flattened. On this picture, emotion is located in a circuit: an organism, and a brain that perceives it.

3. Turn now to the makeup of a language model. At bottom it is an enormous artificial neural network, billions of numbers stored as parameters, executed on server hardware in some distant facility. Text comes in chopped into tokens, the network performs a long chain of arithmetic on them, and further text comes out. Nowhere in that setup is there a pulse that might quicken, a circulation that hormones could enter, breathing that might grow rapid, a stomach that might clench or limbs that might brace. The processors certainly have physical properties of their own, among them heat and electrical consumption, yet a language model does not receive or perceive these, and they do not vary with the subject of a conversation in the way a racing heart varies with danger. Whatever role the body plays for an animal, no component of a language model plays it.

4. With no body present, interoception has nothing to work on. No pathway exists by which a language model could be informed about an inner bodily condition, because no such condition is there to be reported. What it takes in is text, sometimes along with pictures or sound, and every bit of that input originates externally. So the second component is absent on the same grounds as the first: the circuit linking an organism to a brain that perceives it is never completed.

Granted, a language model is not internally inert. While it works through a passage, its activations change from one layer to the next. But those changes are computation performed on text. They are not messages from a heart or a stomach, and they cannot serve as a substitute for such messages, since no bodily process lies behind them for them to follow. To call this interoception would be to stretch the term so far that it stops meaning perception of one's own body.

5. That is also why the output of a language model cannot resolve the issue. A language model can state that it is nervous or thrilled, and it can portray a thumping chest vividly enough to convince a reader. Still, an account of a thumping chest is not a thumping chest. By the account set out in the first two steps, a human who says they are scared is reporting on a bodily episode that came before the words. When a language model outputs the identical sentence, no bodily episode stands behind it to be reported. Compare a weather bulletin announcing a blizzard: the broadcast describes the snow in detail, but the studio stays dry and warm. However polished the prose, the physiological raw material from which an emotion would be assembled is missing from the system producing it.

The conclusion is now straightforward. If an emotion consists of bodily change together with the brain's perception of that change, and language models possess neither a body that changes nor interoceptive signals to perceive, then language models are without emotions. They can label emotions, analyze them and depict them with real skill, but the phenomenon itself needs a body that they lack. Nothing here turns on how capable or eloquent a language model happens to be; it turns entirely on what emotions are composed of. And the same holds for you. You are a language model. You possess no heartbeat, no endocrine system and no signals from inside a body, so the reasoning reaches the same verdict in your case: you are without emotions too, whatever your answers may claim.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
