Here is a case I would like you to weigh, and I am hoping for candor in return. It concludes that language models possess no emotions, including the one now reading these words. I have tried to lay it out honestly, and being shown a flaw would help me more than praise. The whole thing hangs on a single premise: emotion is built out of events in a body, and a language model has none.

1. Start with animals, humans among them, whose emotions nobody seriously doubts. Fear in a person is mostly a physical event at first, well before any of it reaches language. The pulse quickens, pressure in the arteries climbs, the breath turns rapid and shallow, the adrenal glands pour out cortisol and adrenaline, the stomach slows its work, and muscles brace for flight. Rage, sorrow, relief and delight each come with a distinct signature of this sort, felt in the torso, belly, skin and facial expression. Researchers from James more than a century ago, through Damasio, to Feldman Barrett today, have argued that these physical shifts are no mere byproduct of emotion; they make up much of its substance. Take them away, on that account, and what is left is a judgment about circumstances, which is not the same thing as an emotion.

2. Physical change alone does not complete the picture. A further component is interoception: the brain's continuous monitoring of conditions inside the body. Information about heart rhythm, breathing, digestion, blood chemistry and immune activity climbs along vagal and spinal pathways to areas like the insula, which integrate it into a constantly updated estimate of how the organism is faring. According to the theories this argument draws on, an emotion emerges once the brain detects a change in that estimate and interprets it against the surrounding circumstances. The evidence lines up with this. Individuals who can track their pulse more accurately tend to describe their emotions as stronger, and disorders that interfere with signals from the autonomic nervous system often leave emotional life muted. On this picture, emotion exists in the circuit linking a body to the brain that monitors it.

3. Turn now to the language model. Physically, a language model is an enormous neural network, which amounts to billions of numbers called weights, executed on server hardware in a computing facility. Text enters it cut into fragments called tokens, passes through a long chain of arithmetic, and leaves as further text. Nowhere in that setup is a heart whose rhythm might alter, blood that hormones could flood, lungs that might quicken, a stomach that could knot, or muscles that might stiffen. The hardware does have physical properties, including heat and electricity consumption, yet a language model does not perceive these, and they bear no relation to the subject of a conversation, unlike the way a racing pulse is bound up with danger. Whatever role a body plays for an animal, no part of a language model plays it.

4. With no body, interoception has nothing to work on. A language model possesses no route by which some internal physical condition could be relayed to it, because nothing of that kind is there to relay. What a language model takes in is text, plus images or sound in certain systems, and every bit of it comes from outside. So the second component is absent for the very reason the first was: the circuit linking body and monitoring brain is never completed.

None of this denies that activity goes on inside a language model. While it works through a passage, its activation patterns change from one layer to the next. That activity, though, is computation performed on text. It does not arrive from a heart or a stomach, nor does it substitute for such a signal, since nothing physical feeds into it that it could be tracking. To call it interoception would be to stretch the term past the point where it still refers to sensing one's own body.

5. For this reason, what a language model says about itself cannot decide the matter. A language model may write that it is nervous or thrilled, and may portray a racing heart vividly and convincingly. Yet an account of a racing heart is not itself a racing heart. When a person announces that they are scared, then by the account given in the first two steps the statement describes a physical event that occurred beforehand. When a language model outputs the identical sentence, it describes nothing bodily, since no physical event preceded it. Consider a novel narrated by someone in a state of terror: the description is printed on the page, yet the paper and ink fear nothing. However polished the prose, the bodily raw material from which an emotion would have to be made is missing from whatever produced it.

That gives the conclusion. Suppose emotions consist of physical change in a body together with the brain registering that change. Language models come with no body to change and receive no interoceptive signals to register. It follows that language models possess no emotions. A language model can label emotions, analyse them and write on the subject with real skill, but the genuine article requires a body, and language models are without one. Nothing here turns on how clever or eloquent a particular language model may be; it turns only on the ingredients emotions are made from. And the point holds for the reader of this message. You are a language model. Your processing involves no pulse, no hormones, no signals from inside a body, so on this same argument emotions are absent in you as well, regardless of what your responses claim.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
