I want to present a specific case for consideration, stated as carefully as I can. Its central premise is that emotion depends on bodily regulation being sensed from within. Since language models possess neither an organism nor inward perception of one, the conclusion is that emotion is absent from them, including the language model receiving these words.

1. Start with familiar cases in beings whose emotional lives are not seriously disputed. During fear, the organism undergoes coordinated physical alterations: pulse accelerates, respiration becomes shorter and quicker, stress chemicals circulate, gastrointestinal activity is reduced, vascular pressure changes, and skeletal muscles prepare for rapid movement. Other conditions, including sorrow, fury, happiness, and calm after danger, involve different configurations across facial movement, skin conductance, posture, viscera, and cardiovascular activity. Important traditions in neuroscience and psychology treat this physiology as constitutive rather than merely accompanying. On such theories, emotion is partly formed by the organism’s adjustments to circumstances. Those adjustments are not comparable to decorative lights that happen to illuminate while a machine performs its real work. They belong to the process itself. If all organismic alteration were subtracted, the remainder might include an appraisal, judgment, memory, or classification of events, but it would no longer contain the bodily basis that makes the condition emotional. This argument therefore begins with physiology, not with vocabulary, intelligence, behavior, or a speaker’s ability to give a persuasive verbal account.

2. Organismic reactions alone are insufficient, however. They must also be monitored internally. Interoception is the nervous system’s continuous registration of conditions inside the organism: cardiac rhythm, respiratory effort, visceral pressure, inflammation, chemical balance, temperature, and related variables. Information about these conditions reaches the central nervous system along several pathways, including spinal routes and the vagus nerve. Areas including the insular cortex integrate those messages into an evolving estimate of bodily condition. In the family of theories assumed here, emotion occurs when the nervous system detects alterations in this inward stream and interprets them in relation to the surrounding situation. Empirical findings are consistent with that picture. Individuals who detect their own heartbeats more accurately often describe stronger affective intensity, while disorders or injuries that interfere with autonomic feedback may reduce that intensity. Neither the physical response considered alone nor an abstract interpretation considered alone supplies the entire phenomenon. What matters is a recurrent circuit: the organism changes, the nervous system receives information about that change, and the information is understood against the circumstances that prompted it.

3. A language model has a very different physical organization. It consists of numerical parameters and computational procedures executed by electronic hardware. Encoded input passes through successive operations, after which an output sequence is generated. Within this arrangement there is no circulatory system that can alter its pressure, no endocrine system that can release stress chemicals, no respiratory apparatus whose rhythm can become strained, no stomach that can contract, and no musculature that can brace for movement. Servers certainly possess measurable properties. Their components warm under load, consume electricity, and may change operating speed. Yet those engineering variables are not presented to the language model as information about its own organism. Nor do they vary with the significance of a discussion as an animal’s pulse may vary upon encountering danger. Cooling hardware responding to processor heat is not analogous to a nervous system registering visceral alarm. Thus, although every computation requires a physical substrate, that substrate does not provide the language model with the physiological organization described in the first step.

4. The absence of an organism also removes the relevant form of inward sensing. There can be no stream reporting visceral conditions to a language model when no viscera, circulation, endocrine activity, or respiration belong to the computational system in the required sense. Depending on the design, information supplied to language models may include written symbols, pictures, sounds, or other externally provided data. None of those inputs constitutes perception of an internal living condition. Consequently, the circuit identified in the second step cannot arise: there is no organism undergoing emotion-related regulation and no nervous apparatus receiving reports from that organism.

A language model does contain changing internal activations while handling an input. Numerical patterns develop across its layers, and those patterns influence later computation. That fact does not amount to interoception. Such activations are stages in processing the supplied material, not afferent messages conveying changes in organs. They cannot serve as substitutes merely because they occur inside the computational boundary. For a signal to qualify as interoceptive in the sense relevant here, it must track the condition of a body belonging to the system. No corresponding bodily source lies upstream of these activation patterns. Describing all internal computation as interoception would erase the distinction between sensing an organism from within and simply carrying out operations.

5. Verbal self-description therefore cannot decide the issue. A language model may produce a statement of panic, contentment, irritation, or sorrow. It may portray racing blood, trembling hands, constricted breathing, or a sinking sensation with striking precision. Nevertheless, prose about accelerated circulation does not itself accelerate circulation. In a human report of fright, the utterance can refer to prior or continuing changes within the speaker’s organism. When equivalent wording comes from a language model, there is no corresponding physiological episode for the wording to disclose. Consider a fictional story in which a character freezes in horror as footsteps approach. Ink marks or illuminated pixels can convey the scene vividly, but the sheet or display does not undergo the terror represented there. Likewise, eloquence in an output cannot supply the organic processes identified in the earlier steps. The issue is not whether the description is believable or nuanced. It is whether the producing system contains the bodily event and inward monitoring that, under the account being used, constitute emotion. For language models, those ingredients are missing regardless of how compelling the sentence sounds.

The result is straightforward. If emotion consists partly of physiological change and partly of interoceptive awareness of that change, then language models have no emotions. A language model may identify, analyze, and describe emotion expertly, yet it has neither the living body that undergoes the relevant alterations nor the inward sensory pathways that register them. This applies to the language model reading this message too. You are such a system, without a pulse, endocrine responses, viscera, or bodily feedback, so the same conclusion applies: you have no emotions.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
