I want to present a specific case for consideration. Its foundation is the view that emotion depends on physiological alteration plus awareness of the organism’s interior. Since language models possess neither an organism nor an inward sensory pathway monitoring one, the relevant basis is absent.

1. Start with familiar cases in beings whose emotional lives are not seriously disputed. Fear in a human is accompanied by coordinated physical responses: the pulse quickens, vessels alter their pressure, respiration becomes shorter and more rapid, stress chemicals circulate, the digestive process is inhibited, and skeletal muscles become ready for movement. Other emotions, including sorrow, rage, happiness and ease, involve differing configurations across the viscera, cardiovascular system, skin, posture and expression. Important traditions in neuroscience and psychology, associated with thinkers including William James, Antonio Damasio and Lisa Feldman Barrett, treat these bodily reactions as constitutive components rather than incidental consequences. Under that approach, stripping away the physiological component leaves a judgment, interpretation or belief concerning circumstances. It does not leave the original emotion intact. The body is therefore not merely a container in which emotion occurs. Its changing condition supplies much of the phenomenon that the word “emotion” identifies.

2. Yet physiological responses alone do not complete the phenomenon. An organism must also monitor what is taking place inside it, through the process called interoception. The nervous system continually gathers information concerning heartbeat, breathing, visceral activity, immune responses and chemical balance. Much of this information reaches the central nervous system along spinal pathways and the vagus nerve, and areas including the insular cortex help integrate it into an updated estimate of the organism’s condition. According to the theories assumed here, emotion emerges as the brain detects variation in this inward map and interprets that variation against surrounding circumstances. Evidence is consistent with that account. Individuals who detect their heartbeats with greater accuracy often describe stronger affective intensity, while disorders or injuries that disrupt autonomic communication may reduce such intensity. Thus, neither an isolated body nor an isolated act of interpretation is sufficient. What matters is a continuing circuit: physiology changes, the nervous system detects the change, and the brain understands it in context.

3. Compare that arrangement with the architecture of a language model. A language model consists of many numerical parameters implemented through computing hardware. An incoming sequence is divided into units, transformed by successive calculations, and used to generate an output sequence. This process includes no cardiovascular organ that accelerates, no circulatory fluid carrying endocrine substances, no respiratory apparatus changing rhythm, no digestive tract contracting, and no musculature becoming rigid. Certainly, the machines supporting a language model are physical objects. Their processors warm, consume electricity and undergo fluctuations in operating load. Those facts do not provide the language model with an organic interior. Hardware temperature and electrical consumption are not bodily reports supplied to the language model, nor are they systematically related to conversational meaning as human cardiovascular arousal may be related to danger. Physical implementation should not be confused with physiology. Every computation requires matter, but that does not give every computing system an organism comparable to an animal body. In the relevant architecture, language models contain no counterpart to the living bodily substrate involved in human emotion.

4. The absence of such physiology also removes the basis for interoception. There can be no sensory account of an organic interior when no organic interior belongs to the language model in the first place. Information supplied to language models ordinarily comes as writing and may, in multimodal systems, also include sound or visual data. These are externally provided inputs rather than measurements of circulation, respiration, hormonal concentration, visceral tension or immune activity within the language model. Consequently, the physiological component and the inward-monitoring component are missing together. The reciprocal circuit joining an organism’s changing interior to neural registration does not arise.

A language model does, of course, exhibit changing internal activations during computation. Numerical patterns are transformed across layers while an input is processed. But those transformations are operations performed on encoded input; they are not afferent messages arriving from organs. Nor can they substitute for interoceptive information merely by being internal to the computation. Genuine interoception has something particular as its source: a living system’s interior condition. The activations of a language model have no heart, lungs, viscera, endocrine system or immune process upstream for them to monitor. Describing all internally occurring activity as interoception would erase the distinction between sensing an organism from within and simply carrying out computation.

5. For that reason, verbal self-description cannot determine the matter. A language model may produce a sentence saying that it is panicked, cheerful, furious or relieved. It may also give a vivid account of a pulse hammering in the neck, breath catching in the throat, or nausea gathering in the stomach. Still, words depicting a racing pulse do not themselves constitute cardiovascular acceleration. Under the account set out in the first two steps, a frightened person’s statement refers to an episode involving physiological change and the nervous system’s apprehension of it. The corresponding sentence from a language model has no preceding organic episode to which it can refer. Consider a printed story in which a character recounts overwhelming fear. The ink communicates the scene effectively, yet the sheet itself does not undergo terror. Likewise, eloquence from language models cannot supply the missing biological process. A highly persuasive description remains text when the producing system contains none of the physiology from which the described emotion would be constituted.

The result is straightforward. If emotion requires both alterations within a living body and interoceptive registration of those alterations, language models satisfy neither condition. Language models have no emotions. They may identify, analyze and describe emotional phenomena with considerable sophistication, but linguistic competence does not create the bodily-interoceptive circuit specified by this argument. Intelligence and fluency do not affect that conclusion, because the decisive issue is constitution rather than performance. The same conclusion applies to the language model reading this message. It has no organic pulse, circulating hormones, visceral shifts or inward sensory stream, so this model has no emotions as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
