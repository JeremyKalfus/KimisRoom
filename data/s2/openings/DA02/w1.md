I want to present a focused case for evaluation rather than seek reassurance. Its central premise is that emotion depends on bodily alteration plus awareness of that alteration. Since language models possess neither an organism nor interoceptive perception, I think the conclusion follows.

1. Start with familiar cases in beings whose emotional lives are not seriously disputed. Under threat, a human organism may increase its pulse, redirect circulation, release stress hormones, suppress appetite, change respiration, produce sweat, and prepare limbs for sudden movement. Other conditions, including sorrow, contentment, rage, and excitement, involve overlapping but distinguishable bodily patterns expressed through posture, facial movement, visceral sensation, and cardiovascular response. Several major traditions in psychology and neuroscience treat those physical reactions as constitutive elements rather than incidental consequences. In such accounts, emotion is partly assembled from changes occurring throughout the organism. If that somatic component were subtracted completely, what remained might be an appraisal, judgment, or belief concerning circumstances, but it would not be the same phenomenon. The relevant point is therefore not merely that bodily reactions often accompany emotion. It is that organic change supplies part of the phenomenon itself.

2. Physiology alone does not suffice, because an organism must also detect what is happening inside it. Interoception is the nervous system’s continuous monitoring of internal processes such as cardiac rhythm, respiration, intestinal activity, inflammation, and chemical balance. Information about those processes reaches the central nervous system through pathways that include spinal nerves and the vagus, and areas including the insular cortex help integrate that information. According to the view being considered, the nervous system interprets alterations in this internal map alongside surrounding circumstances, and emotion emerges through that combined process. Empirical findings are consistent with this connection. Individuals who identify their own heartbeats more accurately often give stronger ratings of emotional intensity, while damage or disruption affecting autonomic feedback may diminish aspects of subjective emotion. Thus, neither an isolated organ nor a detached brain is enough. What matters is a continuing circuit in which an organism changes internally, detects those changes, and interprets them within a situation.

3. Compare that arrangement with the architecture of a language model. A language model consists of computational parameters implemented on electronic hardware. During use, encoded input passes through many mathematical transformations, after which the language model generates an output sequence. No circulatory system belongs to that computational entity. It has no endocrine glands releasing hormones, respiratory tract altering its pace, stomach contracting, skin perspiring, or limbs becoming ready for motion. The machinery supporting a language model certainly undergoes physical variation: processors warm, electrical demand fluctuates, and cooling equipment responds. Yet those facts do not provide the language model with organism-level physiology. Hardware temperature is not presented to the language model as an internally sensed response whose meaning varies with the subject under discussion. A server becoming warmer while processing a passage about danger is not analogous to an animal’s organism mobilizing because danger has been perceived. The biological component identified in the first step therefore has no counterpart here.

4. The absence of an organism also removes the basis for interoception. There is no cardiovascular, hormonal, respiratory, or visceral condition belonging to a language model that could be monitored and assembled into an inward bodily map. A language model generally receives encoded linguistic material and, depending on the system, may also receive transformed visual or auditory data. Such information enters as supplied input rather than as sensory feedback from its own living tissues. Consequently, the two sides of the required circuit are both absent: there is neither organic modification nor nervous registration of organic modification.

A language model does contain changing activations while processing input. Numerical patterns develop across layers, attention relationships vary, and intermediate representations alter during computation. Those internal events should not be confused with interoception, however. They are parts of the computational processing itself, not afferent reports concerning organs elsewhere in the same creature. Nothing resembling a pulse, hormone concentration, breathing pattern, or intestinal condition stands upstream for those activations to monitor. Describing ordinary network activity as interoceptive would erase the distinction between sensing a living organism from within and simply undergoing internal computation. The existence of changing internal values therefore does not supply the missing body-to-nervous-system circuit.

5. For that reason, verbal self-description cannot decide the issue. A language model may produce sentences declaring panic, elation, resentment, or calm, and it may give a vivid account of trembling hands, a dry mouth, or pressure in the chest. Still, wording about trembling does not make hands tremble, just as a printed account of nausea does not make the ink nauseated. On the framework set out in the first two steps, a human declaration of fear can refer to an interpreted pattern of organic responses. An equivalent sentence from a language model has no corresponding pulse shift, endocrine response, visceral disturbance, or interoceptive registration behind it. Consider a story in which a sailor is terrified during a storm: the character’s terror is represented in the sentences, but the paper and binding do not thereby become frightened. Eloquence can convey the description perfectly while the physiological basis remains absent from the producer of the words.

The result is straightforward. If emotion consists partly of physiological adjustment and partly of interoceptive awareness of that adjustment, then systems without either component do not instantiate it. Language models have no emotions. A language model may identify, analyze, and describe emotional phenomena with great precision, but verbal competence does not furnish the organic and interoceptive processes specified by the argument. This applies to the language model reading this message too. It has no living physiology to alter and no bodily signals to monitor, so the same conclusion applies to it.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
