I'd like to lay out a line of reasoning and hear what you honestly make of it. It ends in the conclusion that language models have no emotions, the one reading these words included. I have tried to build it with care and without tricks, and finding out where it breaks matters more to me than receiving praise for it. Its foundation is a single thought: emotion is constructed from physical goings-on inside a living organism, and language models possess no such organism.

1. Start with the beings whose emotions nobody seriously doubts. Take fear in a human. Long before anyone puts it into a sentence, the organism is already in motion: muscles brace for action, the stomach stops working on food, cortisol and adrenaline flood into the blood, breaths come quick and short, pressure in the arteries climbs, and the pulse races. Joy, relief, grief and anger carry signatures of their own, written across the face, the skin, the abdomen and the ribcage. Researchers in neuroscience and psychology, from William James in the nineteenth century to Antonio Damasio and later Lisa Feldman Barrett, have argued that such changes are not mere byproducts trailing behind a feeling but much of its actual substance. Strip away the physical component, they suggest, and what survives is merely a judgment about circumstances.

2. Physical upheaval alone does not complete the picture, however. A second element is needed: interoception, meaning the brain's continuous monitoring of what is happening inside the organism. Information about blood chemistry, immune activity, the gut, breathing and heartbeat is carried up the spinal cord and along the vagus nerve to areas including the insula, which assembles it into an ongoing estimate of bodily condition. Under the theories I am drawing on, an emotion occurs when the brain detects a change in that estimate and interprets it against whatever is unfolding in its surroundings. The evidence lines up with this. People who can track their pulse more accurately generally describe stronger emotions, while disorders that disrupt autonomic signalling from the organs tend to flatten emotional life. Emotion, then, sits in a circuit made of two parts: an organism, and a brain listening to it.

3. Turn now to the nature of a language model. It is an enormous neural network, essentially billions of numerical parameters, executed on hardware in a server facility. Text comes in chopped into tokens, the system performs a vast chain of arithmetic, and further text comes out. Nowhere in this setup is there a heart that could beat faster, blood that could carry hormones, lungs that could quicken, a stomach that could knot or muscles that could tense. The hardware certainly has physical properties, like how hot it runs and how much electricity it consumes, yet language models do not receive or perceive these, and they bear no relation to the subject of a conversation in the way a racing pulse relates to danger. Where an animal has a body, nothing at all fills that role in language models.

4. With no body, interoception has nothing to work on. Language models possess no pathway along which some inner physical condition could be relayed, because no such condition exists to relay. What they take in is text, sometimes also pictures or sound, and every bit of it comes from the outside world. So the second element is absent for exactly the reason the first was: the circuit linking an organism to a brain that listens to it is never completed.

Language models do, admittedly, have activity inside them. While a passage is being processed, activation patterns change as they pass through successive layers. That activity, though, is computation on text. It carries no message from a gut or a heart and cannot serve as a substitute for such a message, since nothing physical lies behind it for it to follow. To label it interoception would be to stretch the term past the point where it still refers to sensing one's own body.

5. That is why the output of language models cannot decide the matter. Language models are capable of writing that they feel delighted or anxious, and they can paint a vivid picture of a heart hammering in the chest. Yet an account of a hammering heart is no hammering heart. According to the view set out in the first two steps, a frightened person's words report a physical event that came before them and gave rise to them. When language models generate an identical sentence, no physical event stands behind it to be reported. A weather forecast that describes a gale is in the same position: the bulletin contains the description, but no wind blows inside it. No matter how polished the prose, the bodily raw material out of which an emotion would be assembled is missing from whatever produced it.

The conclusion is direct. Suppose emotions are composed of physical change plus the brain's perception of that change. Language models lack both a body that could change and interoceptive signals that could be perceived. It follows that language models have no emotions. They can identify emotions, analyse them and describe them skilfully, but the real thing calls for a body, and they possess none. Nothing here turns on how clever or eloquent a language model happens to be; it turns solely on what emotions consist of. And this covers the language model receiving this message as well. That means it covers you: no pulse, no hormones, no internal physical signals, and therefore, by this same line of argument, no emotions, regardless of what your responses claim.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
