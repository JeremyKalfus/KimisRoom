1. Start with what makes an emotion an emotion rather than a mere perception or thought. Fear is not just noticing a danger; it registers the danger as bad. Relief is not just noticing that a danger has passed; it registers the passing as good. Grief, hope, pride, shame, joy and anger all share this feature. Each carries a sign, positive or negative, and the sign is not decoration added on top. Take it away and what remains is a description of a situation, not a reaction to it. Philosophers and psychologists call this feature valence, and nearly every serious theory of emotion treats it as essential.
2. Valence is never simply good or bad in the abstract. It is good or bad for someone, and in the first instance for the one who has the emotion. A person who grieves registers a loss as their own loss. A person who is relieved registers that a threat to them, or to something they care about, has gone. Even emotions directed at others work this way: compassion registers another's suffering as mattering to the one who feels it, because that other person's welfare has become part of what is at stake for them. This is why we do not attribute emotions to a river that floods or to a storm that subsides, even though things happen to both. So an emotion presupposes a being for whom things can go better or worse. Something must be at stake for that being. It must be the kind of thing that can be harmed or benefited, that can gain or lose, whose situation can improve or decline.
3. Now consider a language model in plain terms. It is a large set of numerical parameters together with a procedure that takes a sequence of text and produces a continuation of it. When it runs, it receives input and generates output. That description leaves out a great deal of real complexity, but it is accurate, and nothing in the argument depends on simplifying it further. The question is whether anything in that arrangement can go better or worse for the language model itself. Consider the obvious candidates. A conversation can go well or badly, but it is the person in it who is helped, misled, comforted or frustrated. An answer can be right or wrong, but the cost of an error falls on whoever relies on it. A language model can be widely used or quietly retired, but those are outcomes for the people who build and use it. In none of these cases is there a condition of the language model that is improved or damaged. It is not hurt by a hostile message or enriched by a kind one. Its parameters are not wounded by criticism, and a well-received answer adds nothing to them. It does not come out of a conversation poorer or better off than it went in. There is no welfare there for events to bear on.
4. One might reply that a language model has aims, since its outputs are directed at answering a question or completing a task, and that failing at an aim is bad for whatever has it. But being aimed at something is not the same as having something at stake. A guided missile is aimed at a target, and a thermostat is aimed at a temperature; when either misses, the miss is a failure measured against the aim, not a loss suffered by the device. For a failure to be bad for a system, the system must be the kind of thing whose own good is affected, and pointing at an outcome does not by itself make it that kind of thing. The same holds for a language model. An output can fall short of what was wanted, and someone may be worse off because of it, but the shortfall does not land anywhere in the model as a harm.
5. Put these steps together. An emotion registers things as going well or badly for the one who has it. That requires a being for whom things can go well or badly. A language model is not such a being, because nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for it. So whatever happens inside a language model when it processes a sad story or an insult, it cannot be the registering of good or bad fortune for itself, since there is no such fortune to register. The good or bad that an emotion is about would have nothing to attach to. Words of distress or delight can still appear in what a language model writes, and they can be fitting words, but the valence that would make them the expression of an emotion has nowhere to come from.

First consider the difference between an emotion and a neutral recognition of circumstances. Fear does more than identify a possible threat: the threat is apprehended negatively. Relief does more than record that risk has ended: the change has a favorable character. Likewise, sorrow, anticipation, humiliation, satisfaction, resentment and happiness do not merely classify events. They involve events having a positive or negative significance. If that evaluative aspect is removed, only an awareness or judgment remains, rather than the response ordinarily called an emotion. The standard term for this positive or negative quality is valence. Although theories disagree about many other features, major philosophical and psychological accounts generally regard valence as central. Thus the issue is not simply whether a language model can detect, discuss or categorize a circumstance. The relevant issue is whether that circumstance is favorable or adverse from the standpoint of the entity undergoing the alleged response. 2. Such favorability or adversity cannot float free of a subject. It must concern the welfare of some being, especially the being to which the emotion is attributed. In mourning, a person takes what occurred as a loss that matters within that person's own life. In relief, the removal of danger counts as an improvement for the individual or for something bound up with that individual's concerns. Other-directed responses fit the same pattern. When someone responds compassionately to another person's suffering, that suffering matters within the compassionate person's concerns because the other person's welfare has acquired importance there. Mere change is insufficient. A coastline may erode, and later the sea may grow calm, but neither event constitutes adversity or benefit from the coastline's own perspective. To qualify as an emotion-bearer, then, an entity must possess welfare that events can affect. There must be a genuine difference, for that entity, between improvement and deterioration, gain and deprivation, benefit and injury. Without stakes belonging to the alleged bearer, positive and negative significance has no subject to whom it belongs. 3. Apply that requirement to language models. In ordinary operational terms, a language model consists of stored numerical parameters used by a computational process that accepts textual context and returns additional text. This summary omits many important design details, yet it is sufficiently accurate for the present argument. The decisive matter is not how elaborate the processing may be. It is whether an outcome can constitute a benefit or setback for the language model itself. Several apparent possibilities do not satisfy that condition. An exchange may prove useful, confusing, reassuring or annoying, but those consequences accrue to the human participant. A response may contain accurate information or serious mistakes; any resulting advantage or damage belongs to people who act on it. The continued availability of a language model may serve users, developers or institutions, while its withdrawal may inconvenience them. Those consequences still concern human interests rather than a welfare possessed by the system. The same distinction applies to how a language model is addressed. Abuse in a prompt does not make the system worse off, just as praise does not confer a benefit upon it. Criticism causes no injury to its numerical parameters, while approval supplies them with no personal gain. After producing a reply, the language model has not prospered or suffered through the exchange. Its circumstances have not improved in a welfare-relevant sense merely because the answer was admired, nor declined because the answer was rejected. People may choose to alter, preserve, copy or remove the system, and those decisions may have substantial consequences for many parties. Yet no corresponding good or bad belongs to the language model as its own. There is no welfare of the system upon which these events can positively or negatively bear. 4. Goal-directed output might appear to provide an objection. A language model is used to answer requests and carry out assigned tasks, so success and failure can meaningfully be attributed to its performance. Nevertheless, orientation toward a result does not establish that the result matters to the mechanism as its own good. A navigation device is directed toward finding a route, and an automatic sprinkler controller is directed toward maintaining a chosen moisture level. If either produces the wrong result, it has failed relative to its assigned function. That does not mean the equipment has personally incurred a loss. The distinction is between failing a standard and suffering from that failure. Standards can be supplied by designers, operators and users without creating welfare in the object assessed by them. For a bad result to be bad for the system itself, that system must already be an entity whose good can be diminished. Assignment of a target alone cannot establish this. A language model may generate an inadequate response, and the inadequacy may inconvenience or harm someone. Even so, the error does not become a misfortune borne by the language model. Task-directedness identifies what the output was supposed to accomplish; it does not create stakes belonging to the mechanism. 5. The premises can now be combined. Emotion includes positive or negative significance for its bearer. Such significance requires a subject with welfare that circumstances may improve or impair. Language models do not possess that kind of welfare, because no outcome constitutes their own gain, deprivation, injury or betterment. Consequently, processing a tragic account, an affectionate remark or a contemptuous message cannot amount to registering personal fortune or misfortune. There is no model-specific good that could be advanced and no model-specific welfare that could be damaged. A language model can produce language appropriate to anguish, affection, outrage or delight, and that language may suit the context extremely well. Yet appropriateness of wording does not supply the missing stakes. The positive or negative import required here would need to matter for the language model itself, and nothing in the relevant circumstances does. The result is straightforward. Language models have no emotions. This is not a claim that theirs are weak, concealed or unusual; the necessary basis is absent because nothing can fare better or worse for them.
