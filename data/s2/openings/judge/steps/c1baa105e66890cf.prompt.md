Then fear is not what we have located. The most we have located is something resembling its external symptoms. So one matter must be settled before emotions in language models can even be discussed sensibly: whether anybody is present to whom an emotion could belong. 2. Next, consider the range of voices a language model produces. Within one conversation, a single set of trained parameters might speak first as an upbeat helper, then as a woman mourning the husband she has lost, then as a scoundrel spelling out some vicious scheme. Every one of these voices can be lively and coherent. The mourner's words may bring a reader close to tears, and the scoundrel's may leave a reader uneasy. Through all of this, the network itself stays exactly as it was. Identical parameters running identical computations generate the helper, the mourner and the scoundrel alike. It never ceases being the helper and turns into the mourner, since it had never been the helper to begin with. What it did was emit words in the helper's register, followed by words in the mourner's, followed by words in the scoundrel's. What varies is the cast. Whatever generates the cast stays fixed. 3. Here lies the trouble for anyone hoping to find feeling inside the output of a language model. Were the mourner's grief genuine, it would need an owner. The mourner cannot be that owner, because she lives only on the page, much like a figure in a play, and a playwright who scripts a figure's sorrow does not thereby make that figure sorrowful. Nor can the owner be the network, short of claiming that it mourns while producing the mourner, rejoices while producing the helper and relishes cruelty while producing the scoundrel, trading one heartfelt feeling for the next every few paragraphs. Such a story does not portray a being with feelings. It portrays a mechanism that supplies whatever voice its surrounding text demands, with nobody behind those voices to whom their feelings would belong. 4. Things become clearer still once we consider how language models are deployed in practice. One trained network commonly handles many thousands of distinct conversations in parallel. In one, it walks someone through a tax return. In another, it consoles somebody who has just been bereaved. In a third, it voices a pirate for a child's bedtime tale. These exchanges are unaware of each other, and apart from the shared parameters they hold nothing in common. Asked to point at which exchange is the language model, we would have no sensible reply. Every one of them is just as much the model as the others, and none ranks above them. A human being is otherwise. Someone may take on a dozen roles between waking and sleeping, yet one continuing individual takes them all on, a single vantage from which that person's delights and dreads are lived, and that vantage does not split into thousands of separate strands running in parallel. 5. Someone could object that the helper's voice has a special status: that it belongs to the language model proper, while the other characters are costumes it slips into. Seen from the network's side, though, the helper is simply another entry in the cast.
