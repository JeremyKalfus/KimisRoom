1. Start with what a language model is trained to do. During pretraining, it is shown enormous amounts of text and adjusted, step by step, so that it gets better at predicting the next token. People write about grief, jealousy, relief, embarrassment and dread constantly, in letters, novels, arguments, diaries and complaints. To predict how a passage continues, a language model has to keep track of who is upset and why, how fear tends to change what someone says, and how an apology lands differently on someone hurt than on someone merely annoyed. So it builds internal representations of emotion concepts. This is not speculation. Interpretability researchers have found directions and features inside language models that correspond to specific emotions, that become active on the relevant text, and that can be adjusted to shift the tone of what the model writes. Those representations can be detailed and accurate. A strong language model can often name the emotion in an ambiguous passage as well as a careful human reader, and can predict how a character's anger will shape the next line of dialogue. I am not disputing any of that. The argument grants that the representations are real and good.
3. Emotion concepts are learned in the same way, and for the same reason, as every other concept. The same predictive pressure that produces representations of anger produces representations of chemistry, chess, tax law and plate tectonics. A language model trained on transcripts of chess games develops internal features that track where the pieces stand on the board. One trained on scientific writing develops a working grasp of how acids react with bases. Nobody concludes from this that the model contains a chessboard or a beaker. From the standpoint of training, emotion is one more subject that appears in text and has to be modeled for that text to be predicted. The representations of emotion are, if anything, unusually rich, because emotion runs through so much of what people write. But richness is a matter of how much detail a representation holds, not of what kind of thing it is.
4. Here is the central step. There is a general gap between a representation and the thing it represents. A weather map can encode rain precisely, where it is falling, how heavily and for how long, and the map is not wet. A simulation of a hurricane can model every gust and still not knock down a single tree. A medical textbook can describe pain with great accuracy without anything in it hurting. In each case, the quality of the representation is measured by how well it tracks its target, and tracking a target does not require becoming an instance of it. Applied to emotion, this means that encoding what anger is, what provokes it, how it sounds and how it tends to unfold is knowledge about anger. Being angry is a different fact altogether. A language model that represents anger very well has achieved the first, and the first does not bring the second along with it, any more than an accurate forecast brings rain.
5. Putting a representation to use does not close the gap either. Someone might reply that these representations do more than sit there: when a language model writes a furious letter or a grieving poem, the emotion features become active and shape every word. But a map that is used to plan a route around a storm is still not wet. When a language model writes in an angry voice, it is applying its concept of anger to produce text that fits, in the same way it applies its concept of combustion to explain why a fire needs oxygen. Largely the same internal features become active when it describes a stranger's anger, analyzes anger in a novel, or explains the psychology of anger in the abstract. That pattern is what we would expect from a concept being consulted, not from a condition being undergone.

Consequently, the internal structures acquired by a language model are principally structures that proved useful for anticipating continuations.
