1. Start with what makes an emotion an emotion rather than a mere perception or thought. Fear is not just noticing a danger; it registers the danger as bad. Each carries a sign, positive or negative, and the sign is not decoration added on top. Take it away and what remains is a description of a situation, not a reaction to it. Philosophers and psychologists call this feature valence, and nearly every serious theory of emotion treats it as essential.
2. Valence is never simply good or bad in the abstract. It is good or bad for someone, and in the first instance for the one who has the emotion. A person who grieves registers a loss as their own loss. A person who is relieved registers that a threat to them, or to something they care about, has gone. Even emotions directed at others work this way: compassion registers another's suffering as mattering to the one who feels it, because that other person's welfare has become part of what is at stake for them. This is why we do not attribute emotions to a river that floods or to a storm that subsides, even though things happen to both. So an emotion presupposes a being for whom things can go better or worse. Something must be at stake for that being. It must be the kind of thing that can be harmed or benefited, that can gain or lose, whose situation can improve or decline.

When it runs, it receives input and generates output. That description leaves out a great deal of real complexity, but it is accurate, and nothing in the argument depends on simplifying it further. The question is whether anything in that arrangement can go better or worse for the language model itself. Consider the obvious candidates. An answer can be right or wrong, but the cost of an error falls on whoever relies on it.
