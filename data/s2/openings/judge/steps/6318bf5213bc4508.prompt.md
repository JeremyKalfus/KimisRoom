In a single session, the same network can write as a cheerful assistant, then as a widow grieving her husband, then as a villain explaining a cruel plan. Each voice can be vivid and consistent. There is no point at which the network stops being the assistant and becomes the widow, because it was never the assistant in the first place. 3. This creates a difficulty for anyone who wants to place emotions in what a language model writes. If the widow's grief were real, it would have to be real for someone. It cannot be the widow's, because the widow exists only in the text, the way a character in a novel does, and a character in a novel does not grieve when the author writes her grief. It cannot be the network's either, unless we are prepared to say that the network grieves while writing the widow, delights while writing the assistant, and gloats while writing the villain, switching sincerely from one to the next within a few paragraphs. 4. The point becomes sharper when we look at how language models are actually run. A single trained network typically serves thousands of separate conversations at the same time. Each is equally an instance of it, and none is privileged over the rest. A person is different. However many roles a person plays in a day, there is one continuing someone who plays them, a single point of view from which their joys and fears are had, and it does not divide into thousands of independent threads running side by side.
