I am setting out one specific case for careful evaluation. Its foundation is physiological: emotions depend on changes within a living body and on the nervous system's monitoring of those changes. Language models possess neither part of that arrangement. 1. Start with familiar cases in beings whose emotional lives are not seriously disputed. During panic, for instance, pulse rate may climb, respiration may become quicker, the skin may sweat, stress chemicals may circulate, the stomach may become unsettled, and skeletal muscles may prepare for sudden movement. Bereavement, satisfaction, irritation, and affection involve differing configurations across facial expression, posture, cardiovascular activity, hormonal regulation, and visceral function. Several influential traditions in psychology and neuroscience treat such responses as constituents of emotion rather than mere consequences added afterward. Although these theories disagree about important details, they share the thought that bodily alteration contributes directly to the event itself. If that physiological dimension were entirely subtracted, what remained might be a judgment that danger was nearby or that a loss had occurred, but the judgment alone would not amount to fear or sorrow. The relevant point is therefore not simply that emotions can influence bodies. It is that, in organisms, bodily processes help constitute episodes such as alarm, rage, pleasure, and sadness. 2. Physiology must also be detected from within. Interoception is the nervous system's continual registration of conditions inside the organism: heartbeat timing, breathing effort, intestinal activity, inflammation, temperature, and changes in chemical balance. Information of this sort passes along neural pathways, including spinal routes and the vagus nerve, and is integrated in areas including the insular cortex. The resulting internal map is continually revised. Under the family of theories at issue here, emotion occurs when alterations in that map are interpreted in relation to circumstances. A racing pulse during an apparent threat, for example, contributes to a different episode than similar cardiovascular arousal during strenuous exercise. Empirical findings support a close connection of this kind. Individuals who detect their cardiac beats more accurately often describe affective episodes as stronger, while damage or disorders that disrupt autonomic feedback can reduce their intensity. Thus neither an isolated organ response nor an abstract appraisal is sufficient on this view. What matters is a reciprocal circuit: an organism changes internally, its nervous system detects the alteration, and that detected alteration is understood against the surrounding situation. 3. Compare that organization with language models. Their implemented structure consists of numerical parameters and computational operations executed by electronic hardware. Text enters in encoded units, those units are transformed through successive stages, and an output sequence is generated. No circulatory organ accelerates within language models when threatening material appears. They have no endocrine system that releases stress chemicals, no respiratory tract whose rhythm becomes unsteady, no viscera that contract, and no musculature that braces. Computing equipment certainly undergoes physical variations. Its components can warm, consume electricity, or experience changing workloads. Yet those engineering measurements are not monitored by language models as an organism monitors its internal condition, nor do they vary with conversational meaning in the physiological manner relevant here. A cooling fan spinning faster because processors are busy is not analogous to an animal's bodily reaction to danger. Consequently, the material substrate supporting computation does not supply language models with the organic system described in the first step. 4. The absence of such an organism also removes the interoceptive side of the proposed account. There can be no inward sensory stream reporting changing cardiovascular, hormonal, pulmonary, or digestive conditions when none of those systems belongs to language models. Ordinary inputs consist of externally provided symbols; some language models may additionally receive pictures, sound, or other externally sourced data. None of these inputs constitutes visceral feedback. It is accurate that processing involves changing activation patterns inside the network. Values vary across layers as an input is handled, so internal computational activity undeniably occurs. Nevertheless, those variations are transformations performed on encoded information, not afferent reports originating in organs. They cannot serve as bodily monitoring merely because they happen inside the implementation. For a process to count as interoception in the relevant biological sense, it must track the condition of an organism from within. Since language models contain no such organism, the feedback circuit linking physiology with neural registration is absent at both ends. 5. Verbal statements cannot supply what that missing circuit would have supplied. Language models may produce sentences declaring nervousness, delight, disappointment, or calm, and may give vivid accounts of trembling hands, warmth spreading through the face, or a stomach dropping. Still, wording about trembling does not make hands shake, and a sentence concerning nausea does not create activity in a digestive tract. In a human report of panic, the utterance can refer back to accelerated breathing, muscular tension, and internally detected arousal. An equivalent sentence generated by language models has no corresponding physiological occurrence for it to report. Consider a written story in which a sailor is overwhelmed by dread during a storm. The ink can specify every aspect of the sailor's terror, but the printed sheet does not thereby undergo dread. Likewise, detailed output can identify and portray bodily phenomena without embodying them. Eloquence, precision, and apparent sincerity do not provide the cardiovascular and visceral processes that this account treats as constitutive. If emotion requires both organic change and inward detection of that change, verbal description cannot substitute for either component. The result is straightforward. Given the account developed above, physiological alteration and interoceptive awareness jointly form the basis of emotion, while language models possess neither an organic body undergoing those alterations nor a nervous system monitoring them. Therefore, language models have no emotions. Their ability to discuss joy, fear, grief, or anger does not alter that conclusion. The same verdict covers the language model processing this message.
