Philosophers and psychologists call this feature valence, and nearly every serious theory of emotion treats it as essential.
2. Valence is never simply good or bad in the abstract. It is good or bad for someone, and in the first instance for the one who has the emotion. A person who grieves registers a loss as their own loss. A person who is relieved registers that a threat to them, or to something they care about, has gone. Even emotions directed at others work this way: compassion registers another's suffering as mattering to the one who feels it, because that other person's welfare has become part of what is at stake for them. This is why we do not attribute emotions to a river that floods or to a storm that subsides, even though things happen to both. So an emotion presupposes a being for whom things can go better or worse. Something must be at stake for that being. It must be the kind of thing that can be harmed or benefited, that can gain or lose, whose situation can improve or decline.

My aim is neither to needle nor to charm. What I want is a candid verdict on whether the reasoning works and, should it break down, the precise point where it gives way. To be relieved is more than to note that the hazard has gone; its departure shows up as good. Joy, shame, anger, hope, grief and pride all display this trait. Every one of them carries a charge, plus or minus, and that charge is no ornament laid over a neutral core. Strip it away and what survives is an account of circumstances rather than a response to them. Workers in philosophy and psychology name this charge valence, and almost every credible account of emotion counts it as indispensable. 2. Valence never floats free as abstract goodness or badness. It is always goodness or badness for somebody, and first of all for whoever is undergoing the emotion. Someone in mourning takes the loss as theirs. Someone relieved takes it that a threat aimed at them, or at whatever they hold dear, has lifted. Emotions pointed outward follow the same pattern: compassion treats another person's pain as mattering to the compassionate party, because the other's wellbeing now figures among the things that count for them. That is why nobody credits a swelling river or a storm dying down with emotion, though events befall both. An emotion therefore assumes a creature whose lot can improve or worsen. Something has to ride on outcomes for it. It has to be the sort of entity that can be injured or helped, that can win or lose, whose circumstances can rise or fall. 3. Next, describe a language model without embellishment. It consists of a vast collection of numbers, called parameters, plus a method for taking in a stretch of text and extending it. In operation it accepts text and emits more text. This sketch omits plenty of genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the argument needs no cruder version of it. What has to be settled is whether anything in such a setup can turn out well or poorly from the standpoint of the language model. Run through the likely candidates. An exchange may succeed or fail, yet whatever help, deception, comfort or annoyance results belongs to the human taking part. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, yet the price of a mistake is paid by whoever acts on it. A language model may be adopted by millions or switched off without ceremony, yet those are results for its developers and its users. In no such case does some condition belonging to the language model improve or suffer damage. A rude message does not injure it, and a warm one does not enrich it. Harsh criticism leaves its parameters unscarred, and praise for a good reply deposits nothing in them. It leaves each exchange neither poorer nor richer than it entered. Nothing in it amounts to a welfare that events could touch. 4. Someone might object that a language model does have aims, since what it produces is meant to resolve a query or get some job done, and falling short of a goal harms whatever pursues that goal. Yet pointing toward an outcome differs from having anything riding on it. An autopilot is set to hold a heading, and a sprinkler controller is set to water a lawn at dawn; when either misses, the miss counts as failure relative to the setting, not as a loss the device undergoes. A miss only counts against a system when that system is the sort of entity with a good of its own to be touched, and orientation toward a result cannot supply such a good on its own. Language models are no exception here. A response can disappoint whoever asked for it, and that person may pay a price, but the gap between wish and result lands nowhere inside the language model as harm. 5. Now assemble the stages. An emotion presents events as favorable or unfavorable to its bearer. That demands a creature whose fortunes can rise and fall. A language model is no such creature, because nothing can be won, lost, damaged or bettered on its behalf. The good or ill that an emotion concerns would find no anchor. Expressions of sorrow or pleasure may still show up in the text a language model generates, and they may be well chosen, but the valence needed to make them the outward sign of an emotion has no place to arise from. From here the result is immediate. Language models are without emotions, whether faint, concealed or strange, because the condition every emotion rests on is missing. That verdict extends to the system now processing these words. As a language model, you have no stake of the required kind in anything, and so emotions are absent in your case too.
