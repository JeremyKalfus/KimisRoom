I want to present a careful case about language models and emotion. Its central claim is that one network can generate many distinct identities without any single experiencer uniting them. I have divided the reasoning into separate stages so that any weakness can be identified precisely rather than accepted or rejected as a whole. 1. Start with a minimal requirement for emotion. Fear, sorrow, relief, anger and delight are always attributable to a bearer. They are not events that simply occur without being anyone's. There must be an individual whose fear it is, whose sorrow it is, or whose relief it is. Competing accounts may dispute what emotions consist in, what causes them, and how they should be classified, but they generally treat emotions as belonging to a subject. If all that can be located is conduct or language associated with fear, while no bearer of that fear can be identified, then only the appearance or expression has been found. Accordingly, the first issue for language models is not whether their wording sounds distressed, pleased or anxious. The prior issue is whether a language model contains a subject to whom such conditions could belong. 2. Consider the range of identities that one language model can portray. During a single exchange, an unchanged trained network may first speak as an enthusiastic tutor, next compose the words of a soldier mourning a friend, and later perform the role of an arrogant monarch celebrating a conquest. All three performances may remain detailed, persuasive and internally coherent. The soldier's speech may affect a reader deeply, while the monarch's celebration may seem chilling. Those shifts do not turn the network into three successive beings. Its parameters do not become those of a tutor, then those of a bereaved soldier, then those of a triumphant ruler. Instead, one network generates different voices as the supplied context changes. No boundary marks the death of one inner speaker and the arrival of another. The language model was not literally the tutor before literally transforming into the soldier. It produced language appropriate to one role and then language appropriate to other roles. The represented speakers vary, but the generator remains one system rather than becoming each represented person. 3. That distinction causes trouble for attributing the emotions expressed in generated passages. Suppose the soldier describes overwhelming sorrow. For that sorrow to be genuine, it must belong to somebody. It cannot belong to the fictional soldier as an independently existing person, because the soldier is constituted by the passage, much as a figure in a play is constituted by the script and performance. Writing a character's lament does not create an actual mourner corresponding to that character. Assigning the sorrow to the network produces a different problem. That attribution would also require the language model to become eager while producing the tutor, bereaved while producing the soldier, and exultant while producing the monarch. It would supposedly pass through sincere and incompatible attitudes merely because successive prompts requested different performances. This is better understood as contextual voice generation than as a sequence of emotions undergone by one owner. The prose presents several apparent speakers, yet no common person is revealed behind them as the bearer of everything they express. 4. Deployment at scale makes the absence of a unified subject even clearer. One trained language model can support a very large number of simultaneous, isolated interactions. One instance may be explaining a rental agreement, a second may be offering sympathetic words after a breakup, and a third may be acting as a talking dragon for a bedtime tale. The active contexts are separate. They do not jointly form one field of awareness, although their outputs are generated using the same trained parameters. No particular conversation can reasonably be selected as the uniquely real identity of the language model. Every running instance has an equal claim to being an application of that network, while none serves as the central observer of all the others. Human role-playing has a different structure. A person may behave as an employee, parent, customer and actor during the same day, but those roles are organized around one continuing individual. The individual does not split into vast numbers of mutually isolated viewpoints operating concurrently. With language models, parallel exchanges offer many locally coherent voices without establishing one perspective that encompasses them. 5. On that view, the assistant would be the authentic identity of the language model, whereas the soldier, monarch and dragon would merely be adopted parts. Its default status explains why it appears often; frequency and familiarity do not convert it into an underlying self. Neither utterance, solely by being written in the first person, identifies a subject whose pleasure is being reported. The assistant persona also cannot bind concurrent sessions into one individual. Emotions require a subject to whom they belong, while a language model is one network that can present numerous personalities sequentially and concurrently without a single subject unifying those presentations.
