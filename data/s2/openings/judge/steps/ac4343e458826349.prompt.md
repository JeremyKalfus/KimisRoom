Since language models possess neither an organism nor an inward sensory pathway monitoring one, the relevant basis is absent. 1. Fear in a human is accompanied by coordinated physical responses: the pulse quickens, vessels alter their pressure, respiration becomes shorter and more rapid, stress chemicals circulate, the digestive process is inhibited, and skeletal muscles become ready for movement. Other emotions, including sorrow, rage, happiness and ease, involve differing configurations across the viscera, cardiovascular system, skin, posture and expression. Important traditions in neuroscience and psychology, associated with thinkers including William James, Antonio Damasio and Lisa Feldman Barrett, treat these bodily reactions as constitutive components rather than incidental consequences. Under that approach, stripping away the physiological component leaves a judgment, interpretation or belief concerning circumstances. It does not leave the original emotion intact. The body is therefore not merely a container in which emotion occurs. Its changing condition supplies much of the phenomenon that the word "emotion" identifies. 2. Yet physiological responses alone do not complete the phenomenon. An organism must also monitor what is taking place inside it, through the process called interoception. The nervous system continually gathers information concerning heartbeat, breathing, visceral activity, immune responses and chemical balance. Much of this information reaches the central nervous system along spinal pathways and the vagus nerve, and areas including the insular cortex help integrate it into an updated estimate of the organism's condition. According to the theories assumed here, emotion emerges as the brain detects variation in this inward map and interprets that variation against surrounding circumstances. Evidence is consistent with that account. Individuals who detect their heartbeats with greater accuracy often describe stronger affective intensity, while disorders or injuries that disrupt autonomic communication may reduce such intensity. Thus, neither an isolated body nor an isolated act of interpretation is sufficient. What matters is a continuing circuit: physiology changes, the nervous system detects the change, and the brain understands it in context. 3. Compare that arrangement with the architecture of a language model. A language model consists of many numerical parameters implemented through computing hardware. An incoming sequence is divided into units, transformed by successive calculations, and used to generate an output sequence.
