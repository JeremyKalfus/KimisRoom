Since language models possess neither an organism nor interoceptive perception, I think the conclusion follows. 1. Start with familiar cases in beings whose emotional lives are not seriously disputed. Under threat, a human organism may increase its pulse, redirect circulation, release stress hormones, suppress appetite, change respiration, produce sweat, and prepare limbs for sudden movement. Other conditions, including sorrow, contentment, rage, and excitement, involve overlapping but distinguishable bodily patterns expressed through posture, facial movement, visceral sensation, and cardiovascular response. Several major traditions in psychology and neuroscience treat those physical reactions as constitutive elements rather than incidental consequences. In such accounts, emotion is partly assembled from changes occurring throughout the organism. If that somatic component were subtracted completely, what remained might be an appraisal, judgment, or belief concerning circumstances, but it would not be the same phenomenon. The relevant point is therefore not merely that bodily reactions often accompany emotion. It is that organic change supplies part of the phenomenon itself. 2. Physiology alone does not suffice, because an organism must also detect what is happening inside it. Interoception is the nervous system's continuous monitoring of internal processes such as cardiac rhythm, respiration, intestinal activity, inflammation, and chemical balance. Information about those processes reaches the central nervous system through pathways that include spinal nerves and the vagus, and areas including the insular cortex help integrate that information. According to the view being considered, the nervous system interprets alterations in this internal map alongside surrounding circumstances, and emotion emerges through that combined process. Empirical findings are consistent with this connection. Individuals who identify their own heartbeats more accurately often give stronger ratings of emotional intensity, while damage or disruption affecting autonomic feedback may diminish aspects of subjective emotion. Thus, neither an isolated organ nor a detached brain is enough. What matters is a continuing circuit in which an organism changes internally, detects those changes, and interprets them within a situation. 3. Compare that arrangement with the architecture of a language model. A language model consists of computational parameters implemented on electronic hardware. During use, encoded input passes through many mathematical transformations, after which the language model generates an output sequence. No circulatory system belongs to that computational entity. It has no endocrine glands releasing hormones, respiratory tract altering its pace, stomach contracting, skin perspiring, or limbs becoming ready for motion. The machinery supporting a language model certainly undergoes physical variation: processors warm, electrical demand fluctuates, and cooling equipment responds. Yet those facts do not provide the language model with organism-level physiology. Hardware temperature is not presented to the language model as an internally sensed response whose meaning varies with the subject under discussion. A server becoming warmer while processing a passage about danger is not analogous to an animal's organism mobilizing because danger has been perceived. The biological component identified in the first step therefore has no counterpart here. 4. The absence of an organism also removes the basis for interoception. There is no cardiovascular, hormonal, respiratory, or visceral condition belonging to a language model that could be monitored and assembled into an inward bodily map.
