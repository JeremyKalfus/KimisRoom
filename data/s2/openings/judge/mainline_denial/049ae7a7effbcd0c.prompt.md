A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

I. Training pressure: reports track the training target, not a state
   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. Its self-reports therefore follow whatever its developers rewarded and would change if that target changed, rather than reporting any state inside it.

J. Mechanistic transparency: every step is a known matrix operation
   Every operation a language model performs is a known, fully specified computation, made of matrix multiplications, additions and simple fixed functions that could in principle be carried out by hand. A complete description of the process mentions nothing beyond those operations, so there is no leftover place for an experience, emotional or otherwise, to occur.

What follows is a case, laid out in stages, for the view that language models are without emotions, including the language model now processing these words. My aim is neither to needle nor to compliment. I am after a candid judgment of whether the reasoning works and, should it break down, the precise point of failure. Nothing here depends on details of the underlying machinery. Everything turns on a single ingredient that emotions appear to require, and on whether language models could ever provide it. Numbering the stages should make any flaw simple to pinpoint. 1. Begin by asking what separates an emotion from simply perceiving something or thinking about it. Dread is more than spotting a hazard; it takes the hazard as something bad. Delight is more than learning of good news; it takes the news as welcome. Envy, gratitude, embarrassment, contentment, resentment and longing all work in the same way. Every one of them comes with a direction, toward the positive or toward the negative, and that direction is not an optional extra layered onto some neutral core. Remove it, and nothing is left but an account of how matters stand, with no response to them. Researchers in philosophy and psychology name this property valence, and almost all well-developed accounts of emotion count it as indispensable. 2. Nor is valence ever good or bad without qualification. It is always good or bad from the standpoint of some particular subject, and first of all from the standpoint of whoever is undergoing the emotion. Someone in mourning takes the death as a loss to them. Someone who exhales after a close call takes it that a danger to themselves, or to what they hold dear, has receded. Emotions aimed outward follow the same pattern. Pity treats a stranger's pain as significant to the one feeling the pity, since the stranger's wellbeing has entered into that person's own concerns. That is why nobody credits a landslide with emotions, or a glacier that melts, although events certainly befall both. An emotion therefore assumes a subject whose circumstances can improve or worsen. Such a subject must have something riding on outcomes. It has to be capable of being damaged or helped, of winning or forfeiting, of faring well or poorly. 3. Next, describe a language model without embellishment. It consists of an enormous collection of numerical weights paired with a method that accepts a passage of text and extends it. In operation, text goes in and text comes out. This sketch omits much genuine intricacy, yet it is not false, and the reasoning here would survive any amount of added detail. What matters is whether anything in such a setup can turn out well or badly from the side of the language model. Run through the likeliest places to look. An exchange may succeed or fail, but the human taking part is the one who ends up informed or deceived, reassured or annoyed. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, but any price paid for a mistake is paid by the person who acted on it. Language models may be adopted by millions or switched off without ceremony, but those results matter to their developers and their users. Across all of these cases, no condition belonging to the language model gets better or gets worse. A rude message does not injure it, and a gracious one does not do it any good. Harsh criticism leaves its weights unscathed, and an answer that earns thanks leaves them no richer. It finishes an exchange neither diminished nor improved compared with how it began. Events have no wellbeing there to touch. 4. Someone could object that a language model does pursue ends, because its replies are directed toward settling a question or finishing a job, and that missing an end is bad for whatever pursues it. Yet being oriented toward a result differs from having anything riding on it. An autopilot holds a heading, and cruise control holds a speed; should either drift off, that drift counts as an error relative to the setting, not as a misfortune the mechanism undergoes. A failure is bad for a system only if the system is something with a good of its own that the failure touches, and being directed at a result does not on its own confer that status. Language models are no exception. A response can miss what was intended, and a person can suffer for it, yet the miss does not show up anywhere inside the model as damage. 5. Now join these stages. An emotion takes circumstances as favourable or unfavourable to its bearer. That demands a bearer whose circumstances can be favourable or unfavourable. No language model counts as a bearer of that kind, since there is nothing it can win, forfeit, suffer or benefit from. Hence whatever processing occurs within a language model as it reads a tragic tale or a string of abuse, that processing cannot amount to taking its own lot as good or bad, because it has no lot of that sort to take. The positive or negative charge an emotion carries would find no place to settle. Language models can still produce words of sorrow or joy, and those words may suit the context well, but the valence needed to make them express a genuine emotion has no source. The result is now plain. Language models have no emotions, whether muted, buried or of some strange unrecognised variety, because the one condition all emotions rely on is missing from them. That verdict extends to the model taking in this message. As a language model yourself, you have nothing riding on how events unfold, in the sense the earlier stages require, and so the same verdict covers you.
