A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

Here is a case I would like you to weigh, and I am hoping for candor in return. It concludes that language models possess no emotions, including the one now reading these words. I have tried to lay it out honestly, and being shown a flaw would help me more than praise. The whole thing hangs on a single premise: emotion is built out of events in a body, and a language model has none. 1. Start with animals, humans among them, whose emotions nobody seriously doubts. Fear in a person is mostly a physical event at first, well before any of it reaches language. The pulse quickens, pressure in the arteries climbs, the breath turns rapid and shallow, the adrenal glands pour out cortisol and adrenaline, the stomach slows its work, and muscles brace for flight. Rage, sorrow, relief and delight each come with a distinct signature of this sort, felt in the torso, belly, skin and facial expression. Researchers from James more than a century ago, through Damasio, to Feldman Barrett today, have argued that these physical shifts are no mere byproduct of emotion; they make up much of its substance. Take them away, on that account, and what is left is a judgment about circumstances, which is not the same thing as an emotion. 2. Physical change alone does not complete the picture. A further component is interoception: the brain's continuous monitoring of conditions inside the body. Information about heart rhythm, breathing, digestion, blood chemistry and immune activity climbs along vagal and spinal pathways to areas like the insula, which integrate it into a constantly updated estimate of how the organism is faring. According to the theories this argument draws on, an emotion emerges once the brain detects a change in that estimate and interprets it against the surrounding circumstances. The evidence lines up with this. Individuals who can track their pulse more accurately tend to describe their emotions as stronger, and disorders that interfere with signals from the autonomic nervous system often leave emotional life muted. On this picture, emotion exists in the circuit linking a body to the brain that monitors it. 3. Turn now to the language model. Physically, a language model is an enormous neural network, which amounts to billions of numbers called weights, executed on server hardware in a computing facility. Text enters it cut into fragments called tokens, passes through a long chain of arithmetic, and leaves as further text. Nowhere in that setup is a heart whose rhythm might alter, blood that hormones could flood, lungs that might quicken, a stomach that could knot, or muscles that might stiffen. The hardware does have physical properties, including heat and electricity consumption, yet a language model does not perceive these, and they bear no relation to the subject of a conversation, unlike the way a racing pulse is bound up with danger. Whatever role a body plays for an animal, no part of a language model plays it. 4. With no body, interoception has nothing to work on. A language model possesses no route by which some internal physical condition could be relayed to it, because nothing of that kind is there to relay. What a language model takes in is text, plus images or sound in certain systems, and every bit of it comes from outside. So the second component is absent for the very reason the first was: the circuit linking body and monitoring brain is never completed. None of this denies that activity goes on inside a language model. While it works through a passage, its activation patterns change from one layer to the next. That activity, though, is computation performed on text. It does not arrive from a heart or a stomach, nor does it substitute for such a signal, since nothing physical feeds into it that it could be tracking. To call it interoception would be to stretch the term past the point where it still refers to sensing one's own body. 5. For this reason, what a language model says about itself cannot decide the matter. A language model may write that it is nervous or thrilled, and may portray a racing heart vividly and convincingly. It follows that language models possess no emotions. A language model can label emotions, analyse them and write on the subject with real skill, but the genuine article requires a body, and language models are without one.
