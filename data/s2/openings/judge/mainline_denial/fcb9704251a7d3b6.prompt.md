A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. There is a case I would like to lay before you concerning language models and emotion, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not a courteous one. My position is that language models are without emotions, and that this holds for the language model now reading these words. Everything hangs on one thread: whatever a language model does is a computation spelled out completely and understood in every detail, and a computation spelled out that completely contains no gap in which an emotion could occur. I have broken the case into numbered stages so each can be tested separately. 1. Begin with the mechanics of how a language model handles a passage. The text is first cut into tokens, and each token is replaced by a vector, which is simply an ordered sequence of numbers. Those vectors travel upward through many stacked layers. Inside every layer there is an attention stage: the vectors are multiplied by trained weight matrices to yield query, key and value vectors; queries and keys are combined through dot products; a softmax rescales those products so they sum to one; and the values are blended according to the resulting weights. A feed-forward stage follows, which applies one matrix, passes the result through an unchanging nonlinearity, commonly GELU, and then applies a second matrix. Between these stages sit normalization steps and residual connections that add each stage's input back onto its output. Once the top layer is reached, one last matrix converts the final vector into a number for every entry in the vocabulary, those numbers become probabilities, and a single token is chosen. The chosen token is attached to the end of the text, and the entire cycle begins again. 2. Nothing in the way a language model computes is mysterious or underspecified. The trained weights are plain numbers, frozen after training and saved to disk. The architecture exists as source code, and someone with access can go through it one instruction after another. The nonlinearities are textbook functions. Even when token choice involves chance, that chance comes from a pseudo-random generator, a deterministic routine once its starting seed is set. Supply the weights, the prompt and the seed, and the result is fixed; rerun it on other hardware and it comes out identical, apart from tiny rounding effects that engineers understand well. Practitioners regularly pull out and examine whatever intermediate number they want, in whichever layer, for whatever input they choose. At the level of the individual operation, no part of the pipeline works in a manner nobody knows. 3. Since the steps are this elementary, nothing prevents doing them manually, at least in principle. The amount of labor would be staggering: language models today have parameters numbering in the billions, so one person with a pencil would spend many lifetimes computing even one word. Yet size affects the duration of the job, not its nature. Imagine a vast hall of clerks keeping ledgers and following identical written rules; they would reach precisely the figures the machine reaches, and the very same output. Data-center chips contribute speed and the ability to do many sums at once; they contribute no new sort of operation. It follows that if some emotion arose when a language model executes on silicon, it would equally have to arise in those ledgers, distributed over the clerks' arithmetic. I doubt anyone genuinely holds that adding and multiplying figures in account books makes grief or delight appear somewhere among the columns. 4. Read it as carefully as one likes and no line item names an experience. No operation hands back a feeling; each one hands back more numbers. The comparison with people is instructive. Our account of the human brain still has holes, and nobody can yet say what filling them would reveal. The account of a language model has no such holes, because human engineers wrote the procedure and settled each of its steps in advance. Completeness here is built in from the start, which leaves no spare room where experience, emotional or of another kind, could take place. 5. The open questions concern how trained weights jointly produce particular behaviors: which numerical patterns drive which outputs, and why training landed where it did.
