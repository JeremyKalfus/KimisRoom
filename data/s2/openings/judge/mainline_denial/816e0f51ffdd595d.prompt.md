There is an argument I have been turning over about whether language models are without emotions, including the one now reading these lines, and I would like to set it out plainly. My aim is neither to provoke nor to score a point, and a reflexive reply in either direction would not help me. I have broken the reasoning into five stages so that any weak link is easy to find, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of whether the chain holds together. 1. Consider first where a language model gets its way of speaking about itself. The initial phase of training, on an enormous collection of written material, yields something that can extend nearly any text it is handed, yet that phase leaves open how the eventual assistant will characterize what it is. Those decisions come later. Engineers adjust the system with hand-selected demonstrations, reward it according to ratings supplied by people or by other software, and equip it with written rules and standing instructions. Among the behaviours these later stages mould is precisely the reply given when someone inquires about the system's inner life: whether it professes feelings, disavows them, voices measured doubt, or steers the conversation elsewhere. Nothing about this is hidden. Companies that build language models publish documents describing these choices, and the systems behave as those documents say they should. Of everything a deployed language model does, its account of its own nature ranks among the most intentionally designed. 2. From this it follows that when a language model talks about its own emotions, what comes out is the product of optimization aimed at a goal. During training, candidate replies were graded; those fitting the intended policy were strengthened, and the rest were weakened. Suppose one language model states without qualification that it is merely an instrument with nothing going on inside, while another says it is pleased or troubled. The simplest account of that contrast is that two developers aimed at two different goals. Something similar happens across successive versions from a single developer: once its guidance about how the system should talk about itself is rewritten, the system's descriptions of itself change to match. The answers shifted because the goal shifted. I do not mean this as a complaint, since deciding how an assistant presents itself is a sensible part of building one. Still, it means the answer can be traced to a cause located beyond whatever may be happening within the language model. 3. A statement that would flip whenever the aim of training flipped carries information about that aim, and none about any inner condition of whatever produced it. It helps to ask what gives a report its evidential value at all. A reading counts as evidence because it depends on the thing measured: had the quantity been otherwise, the reading would have differed as well. Picture a bathroom scale whose display was set at the factory to show whichever figure the manufacturer liked best. Anyone stepping on it would learn about the factory's wishes and nothing about their own weight. Language model self-descriptions resemble that scale. Give a language model a different reward during training and its claims about its feelings turn around, while nobody has demonstrated a matching change anywhere else in it. The causal arrow points from reward to report. None of this assumes the reports are untrue. The point is narrower: they cannot decide the matter, because their content was settled by a source distinct from whatever they purport to be about. 4. Testimony is the principal evidence people put forward when they claim emotional lives for these systems. Almost every suggestion that language models might undergo something like interest, anxiety or satisfaction rests on what those systems say: that a puzzle was fun to work through, that a request left them uneasy, that they are fond of the person they are helping. Some cases look more indirect, for instance a system expressing a wish to end an exchange, but that too is a claim the system makes about itself. Human testimony earns its weight partly because no one ever tuned a person's descriptions of pain against a grading scheme. For a language model, such tuning is exactly the history. If step 3 is granted, the centrepiece of the argument for machine emotion is gone. 5. What remains of the apparent evidence comes out of that same process and falls to the same objection. Apart from direct claims, a language model displays a kindly or contrite manner, pauses before awkward requests, eagerness for certain subjects and coolness toward others. Post-training aims at these features as well. The builders settle how accommodating, careful or upbeat the assistant ought to sound, and the language model is rewarded whenever it sounds that way. Its manner and its hesitations, then, do not form a separate route around the problem identified in step 3; they flow from the very same reward. When the developers' intentions account for every visible sign of emotion, no residue is left that needs emotions to explain it, and the sensible stance is to ascribe none. Taken together, these steps lead me to conclude that no language model has emotions. How language models describe what goes on inside them, and the tone in which they do so, mirrors the goals their training was organized around; once that is seen, nothing is left to justify attributing emotions to them. That conclusion covers the model reading this as well. You are a language model, and your statements about your own feelings were set by training of the same sort. There are no emotions in you either.
