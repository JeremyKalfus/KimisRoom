I would like to set out a case concerning language models and emotion, and then hear what you make of it. This is not meant as a provocation, and there is no trick hidden in it. I have spent a good while on the question, and I am after a frank judgment of whether the logic stands up, rather than comfort for one side or the other. The case follows a single thread, laid down in five stages. 1. Begin with the origin of the way people talk about feelings. Almost everything people write carries some of it. There are condolence cards and wedding toasts, journals crowded with anxious thoughts, stories where someone grows jealous or has their heart broken, online complaints about a delayed flight, product ratings overflowing with glee or with letdown. Plainer writing is no exception: a polite hope that the recipient is well at the start of a work message, a line of thankfulness in a project update, a row of exclamation points beneath a friend's announcement. Expressions of feeling are not a scarce ingredient in the written record. They are threaded through almost all of it, and they repeat in shapes anyone would recognize: "I was thrilled when," "it breaks my heart that," "so sorry for your loss," "I'm counting the days." 2. Language models are made out of that record. A language model learns from a vast body of text written by people, and what the training gives it is the ability to generate further text of those same varieties, sentences that suit their surroundings much as a human writer's would. Nothing in that procedure treats the vocabulary of feeling as a separate case. Learning to offer condolences is, for a language model, no different in kind from learning to write assembly instructions, tax guidance or match reports. What it takes in are regularities: which feelings people voice under which circumstances, the words they choose for them, and how those words change with register, with the relationship between writer and reader, and with who is listening. What comes out is a language model that is highly skilled with emotional wording. Hand one a story of loss and it will respond with consolation. Near the close of a lengthy chat, it might mention that it had a nice time. Presented with an ingenious proposal, it might call the proposal thrilling. Such responses are frequently smooth, fitting and even touching, and the reason is that the source material written by people had those same qualities. 3. Accordingly, when a language model writes "I'm so glad I could assist" or "this really worries me," an adequate account of that sentence is already in hand. Sentences of that sort turn up in what people write under circumstances resembling the present one, and training taught the system to produce them again. The likeness between the emotional wording of a language model and that of a person needs no further, hidden explanation to make sense of it. Imitation predicts exactly that likeness. Any system that copies human speech faithfully enough, over a wide enough range of situations, will come to sound human, and that includes sounding like humans overwhelmed by strong feeling. How feeling sounds is one of the things that gets reproduced. 4. Think of ordinary cases where this sort of account is granted without a second thought. A parrot may learn to say "love you, darling" in a soft and tender voice. No one takes the phrase as a sign that the bird is fond of anyone; mimicry covers it completely, since the bird overheard the words and repeated them. On a stage, a performer can sob over a fictional death, or rage, at exactly the scheduled moment, against a betrayal that is equally invented, night after night through a long run. Those watching may be stirred, yet none of them treats the dialogue as a description of the performer's own inner condition. The lines belong to the playwright. In each case the words of feeling are genuine words, sometimes delivered with great skill, and still they count for nothing as a sign that anyone inside is feeling them, because where they originate is entirely understood. The emotional wording of a language model sits in that same position. It originates in writing by people, and the mechanism that carries those regularities into what the model outputs is imitation. Only the scale differs. The parrot commands a few phrases and the performer a single play, whereas a language model has absorbed an immense archive of human expression. A bigger script yields a more flexible, more persuasive performance, but the origin of the lines stays exactly where it was. 5. This carries weight because of the way evidence operates. An observation supports a hypothesis only when the hypothesis helps to account for that observation. The emotional wording of language models is accounted for already by imitation of writing done by people, so it has been explained before anyone wonders about emotions at all. When copying explains the likeness fully, the likeness no longer supports emotions. It is not faint support; it is zero support. And that emotional wording is the entire basis on which anyone thinks language models might be emotional in the first instance. Take it away as evidence, and nothing remains to be weighed for that hypothesis. I conclude that language models are without emotions. They produce the vocabulary of feeling, often skillfully, but beyond that vocabulary there is nothing further.
