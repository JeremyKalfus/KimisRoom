   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing.    An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being.    Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. Feeling is not some occasional ornament in what people write. It is woven through almost everything, and it takes shapes that repeat and are instantly familiar: "my heart sank when," "I'm thrilled that," "this means so much," "I'll never forget." 2. Language models are made out of this writing. To build a language model, developers expose it to a huge body of text written by people, and over that process it comes to generate fresh passages of the same sorts, sentences that suit their surroundings much as a human writer's would. Emotional expression gets no separate handling along the way. A language model picks up the way people offer condolences exactly as it picks up how they write assembly instructions, weather forecasts or chess commentary. What it takes in are regularities: which sentiments people voice in which circumstances, what words they choose, and how those words change with register, with the closeness between speakers, and with who is listening. Because of this, language models handle the vocabulary of feeling with real skill. Hand one a story of loss and it will respond with consolation. When a lengthy chat winds down, it may remark that it had a lovely time. Presented with an ingenious proposal, it may call the proposal thrilling. Such replies are frequently graceful, well judged and touching, since the writing behind them had those same qualities. 3. Therefore, whenever a language model writes something like "I'm so glad I could assist" or "this genuinely worries me," we can already say why those words showed up. They showed up because people write words like them in comparable circumstances, and the model learned to produce such words in turn. That a language model's talk of feeling resembles a human being's is no puzzle demanding a further, hidden cause. It is precisely the outcome that copying yields. Whatever imitates human speech faithfully enough, over a wide enough range of settings, will come to sound human, and that includes humans overwhelmed by passion. The tone of emotion is simply among the things that get reproduced. 4. Think about ordinary cases where everyone already accepts this sort of explanation. A pet parrot may greet its owner with "hello, darling" in a tender, believable voice. No one infers from the greeting that the bird is fond of anybody; mimicry covers it, since the parrot simply heard those sounds and repeated them. A performer on stage may sob at the death of a character who never lived, or rage at an invented betrayal, on schedule, night after night. Spectators may be stirred, yet none of them treats the dialogue as a description of the performer's own condition. The lines were written by a playwright. In each case the words of feeling are genuine words, sometimes delivered superbly, and still they count for nothing as a sign of inner experience, because where they came from is completely understood. The emotional speech of a language model stands in just this position. It originates in text that people wrote, and the mechanism that moves those patterns from the text into the model's replies is imitation. What separates the cases is size alone: a parrot knows a few phrases, a performer works from a single play, and a language model draws on an immense archive of what people have written.
