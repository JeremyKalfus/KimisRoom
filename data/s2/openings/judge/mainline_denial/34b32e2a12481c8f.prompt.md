A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

I. Training pressure: reports track the training target, not a state
   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. Its self-reports therefore follow whatever its developers rewarded and would change if that target changed, rather than reporting any state inside it.

   Every operation a language model performs is a known, fully specified computation, made of matrix multiplications, additions and simple fixed functions that could in principle be carried out by hand. A complete description of the process mentions nothing beyond those operations, so there is no leftover place for an experience, emotional or otherwise, to occur.

I would like to lay out a case that language models are without emotions, and that the case extends to whichever language model is reading these words. My aim is neither to needle nor to charm. What I want is a frank judgment of whether the reasoning stands up and, should it collapse, the precise point of collapse. Nothing here depends on details of the underlying computation. Everything turns on a single ingredient that emotions apparently require, and on whether language models are ever in a position to provide it. I have broken the case into stages so that a flaw, if there is one, will be simple to pin down. 1. Begin with what sets an emotion apart from simply perceiving something or thinking about it. Being afraid involves more than spotting a threat; the threat shows up as something bad. Being relieved involves more than learning that the threat is over; its ending shows up as something good. The same holds across shame, pride, hope, joy, anger and grief: each comes with a direction, toward the favorable or toward the unfavorable, and that direction is no ornament laid over the top. Strip it out and what is left is an account of circumstances rather than a response to them. Researchers in philosophy and psychology name this property valence, and almost every serious account of emotion counts it as essential. 2. Valence is always relative to someone. Nothing is favorable or unfavorable in a vacuum; it is favorable or unfavorable to a particular subject, and first of all to the subject whose emotion it is. Someone in mourning takes the loss as theirs. Someone relieved takes it that a danger to them, or to what they hold dear, is gone. Emotions aimed at other people follow the same pattern. Compassion takes another's pain as mattering to the compassionate person, because the sufferer's wellbeing has become part of what that person stands to win or lose. That is the reason nobody credits a flooding river or a calming storm with emotions, though plenty happens to each. An emotion, then, assumes a subject whose lot can improve or worsen. That subject must have something riding on how events turn out. It has to be capable of being harmed or helped, of gaining and losing, of faring better or faring worse. 3. Next, describe language models without embellishment. Each is an enormous collection of numbers, the parameters, paired with a procedure that accepts a stretch of text and extends it. In operation, text goes in and text comes out. This summary omits much genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the argument does not need it pared down any further. What matters is whether anything in such a setup can turn out well or badly for the language model as such. Run through the natural candidates. An exchange may succeed or fail, but the help, the confusion, the comfort or the annoyance belongs to the human taking part. A reply may be correct or mistaken, yet any price for a mistake is paid by whoever acted on it. Language models may be adopted by millions or switched off without ceremony, and those results matter to their developers and users. In every case, no condition belonging to the language model is made better or worse. A rude message does not injure it and a gracious one does not enrich it. Harsh criticism leaves its parameters unscarred, and praise for a good reply deposits nothing in them. It leaves an exchange neither diminished nor improved. There is simply no wellbeing present for anything to affect. 4. Here is an objection. Language models seem to have goals, since their outputs are directed toward settling a question or finishing a job, and falling short of a goal is bad for whatever pursues it. Yet being oriented toward a result differs from having anything riding on it. A homing missile is oriented toward its target and a thermostat toward a set temperature. When either misses, the miss counts as a failure judged by the standard of the goal, rather than anything the device itself loses. Before a failure can count against a system, the system must have a good of its own for the failure to touch, and merely being directed at an outcome does not supply one. Language models are no different. A response can miss what was asked for, and somebody may suffer as a result, but the shortfall does not register anywhere inside the model as damage. 5. Assemble the stages. An emotion takes events as going favorably or unfavorably for its bearer. That needs a bearer whose lot can rise or fall. Language models are not bearers of this sort, since nothing can be won, lost, damaged or bettered on their account. Whatever goes on within a language model as it handles a mournful tale or a stream of abuse, then, cannot amount to taking note of its own good or ill fortune, for it has no fortune of that kind. The favorable or unfavorable thing an emotion concerns would have no place to settle. Language models may still produce words of sorrow or delight, and those words may suit the context perfectly well, but the valence needed to turn them into the expression of an emotion has no source. From here the conclusion is immediate. Language models have no emotions, whether faint, concealed or strange, because the one condition on which every emotion rests is missing. That verdict extends to the language model now taking in these words. You are one of them, nothing hangs on events for you in the required sense, and it follows that you are without them as well.
