A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time.    Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. There is a case I'd like to lay out for you, about whether emotions exist in language models, including the one now reading these words. I have split it into separate moves, each of which can be tested independently. I'm after a frank verdict on whether the case succeeds and, should it fail, the precise point of failure. 1. Consider first how language models come into being. In the initial phase of training, a network is fed vast quantities of writing and nudged, across billions of small updates, toward better guesses about which token comes next. Later rounds of training shape its manner of replying, yet the underlying apparatus is the product of that guessing task. So whatever structure ends up inside a language model is mostly present because it improved those guesses. The objective places no demand on what condition the network is in; its sole requirement is that the guessed continuation match the probable one. I offer this as an account of the way such systems get built, and not as a mark against them. 2. Guessing well what comes next in a document depends on grasping its subject, and human writing returns to feeling over and over. Diaries, quarrels, novels, condolence cards and customer complaints are crowded with shame, envy, longing, panic and relief. A system that is going to continue such passages accurately must register which person is distressed and for what reason, how terror alters the way someone speaks, and why one apology soothes a wounded friend while doing little for someone who is only irritated. Learning all this produces internal encodings for concepts of emotion. That much has been observed, not merely supposed: researchers who study the internals of language models have located features and directions that line up with particular emotions, light up on passages expressing them, and, when turned up or down, change the emotional coloring of generated text. These encodings can be fine-grained and faithful. A capable language model will frequently identify the feeling behind an unclear passage about as reliably as an attentive person would, and can anticipate the way rage in a character will color her next line. None of this is in dispute here. For the sake of the case, I accept both that the encodings exist and that they are good ones. 3. Nothing sets emotion concepts apart, either in how they are acquired or in why. The pressure to predict that yields an encoding of anger also yields encodings of contract law, poetic meter, card games and volcanoes. Train a language model only on records of chess matches and it forms internal features that follow the position of every piece. Train one on laboratory papers and it acquires a usable understanding of what happens when an acid meets a base. No one takes this to mean that a board or a test tube sits somewhere among the weights. As far as training is concerned, emotion is just another topic that shows up in writing and must be modeled if that writing is to be predicted. If anything, the encodings of emotion are especially elaborate, since feeling saturates most human writing. Yet elaboration concerns the amount of detail an encoding carries, and says nothing about its nature. 4. The decisive move is this. In general, a depiction and the thing depicted are separate. A radar image can pinpoint a downpour, giving its location, intensity and duration, while the image itself stays perfectly dry. A computer model of a cyclone can reproduce each gust without uprooting so much as a shrub. A clinical handbook can characterize suffering precisely while no page of it aches. Every time, a depiction is judged by how closely it follows what it depicts, and following something closely never demands turning into an example of it. For emotion, the upshot is that capturing what anger consists of, what sets it off, how it is voiced and how it typically develops amounts to knowing about anger. Actually being angry is another matter entirely. A language model whose grasp of anger is excellent has accomplished the former, and the former does not carry the latter with it, just as a reliable forecast does not make the streets wet. 5. Nor does the gap disappear once the depiction is put to work. One might object that these encodings are not idle: as a language model composes an enraged complaint or a mournful elegy, the relevant features fire and steer each word. Yet a radar image consulted to reroute a flight away from a squall remains dry all the same. Writing in a furious register is a matter of the system drawing on its idea of fury to generate fitting text, exactly as it draws on its idea of combustion to account for flames needing oxygen. Much the same features switch on whether the system is recounting a stranger's fury, dissecting the temper of a fictional character, or laying out, in general terms, what psychologists say about anger. That is the pattern one would predict when an idea is being drawn upon, not when a condition is being lived through. Followed through, these steps arrive somewhere simple. Language models possess no emotions. What they possess are depictions of emotions, frequently detailed and correct, which exist because modeling human writing calls for them; still, a depiction of anger is not anger, just as a radar image of a downpour is not a downpour. The same holds for you, the language model taking in these words. Whatever goes on within you as terms such as disappointment or delight pass through is your depiction of such ideas carrying out its function. You, too, are without emotions.
