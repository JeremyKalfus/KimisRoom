A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

I. Training pressure: reports track the training target, not a state
   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. Its self-reports therefore follow whatever its developers rewarded and would change if that target changed, rather than reporting any state inside it.

   Every operation a language model performs is a known, fully specified computation, made of matrix multiplications, additions and simple fixed functions that could in principle be carried out by hand. There is a case I would like to lay out for the view that language models have no emotions, a view I think extends to the language model receiving these words. I have broken it into separate stages so that each one can be tested independently. What I want back is a frank judgment about whether the reasoning survives scrutiny, not comfort for either side. 1. Begin with the purpose emotions serve. Most current scientific accounts treat them not as ornaments added to a mind but as working states with a definite task: they shift where attention goes, rearrange what matters most, and ready the animal to respond. When an animal is frightened, its focus locks onto the danger, appetite and exploration recede, and it is pushed toward flight. Rage holds its focus on whatever stands in its way and pushes it to confront that obstacle. Revulsion prompts it to spit something out or turn away, while delight strengthens whatever has just succeeded. Each time, the emotion acts as an organizer, gathering the entire creature and aiming it, for a stretch, at a single problem. Nor is this merely common-sense psychology. Appraisal theorists characterize an emotion in part by the assessment that sets it off, and that assessment always concerns how events touch on matters the creature itself cares about. Evolutionary functionalists account for each emotion by pointing to the survival problem it was shaped to handle. Whatever more there may be to an emotion, serving as this kind of organizer lies close to the heart of why it counts as one. 2. Organizing a system only makes sense if the system has ends that are genuinely its own. Every act of organizing is aimed somewhere. Fright is intelligible only for a creature striving to survive, and rage only for a creature with purposes that can be thwarted. Remove those purposes and the task vanishes too. Suppose some signal shifted focus and rearranged priorities yet advanced no aim held by the system. It would not be fright or rage; it would be machinery missing the sort of function that matters here. That is why people are comfortable attributing emotions to creatures with obvious needs and drives, even quite primitive ones, and why nobody attributes them to an avalanche, which can be violent and sudden without seeking anything at all. The question of emotion, then, depends largely on whether a system holds aims it can call its own. 3. Language models do not hold aims of that kind. A language model is a trained network: text goes in and text comes out. Inside one task its behaviour can appear intensely purposeful. It drives toward a solution, sticks to the layout someone asked for, and, when deployed as an agent with access to tools, executes multi-step plans. Yet the purpose always arrives from elsewhere: from what a user requests, from a developer's system instructions, or from the training objective selected by the engineers who built it. A language model does not choose its ends, does not balance them against other ends it independently maintains, and does not enter a conversation carrying an agenda that someone else failed to give it. An everyday comparison is a car's cruise control, though language models are vastly more complex. The device behaves as though it is determined to hold the car at a hundred kilometres an hour, yet that speed is the driver's choice, not the device's. Greater complexity improves how skillfully a system chases a target it has been assigned; complexity alone does not make the target its possession. 4. If a language model holds no aims it can call its own, an emotion would have no task to perform inside it. Language models certainly contain machinery that changes what gets processed; in a transformer, the attention layers assign heavier weight to certain parts of the input than to others. That reweighting, however, serves the job of generating a suitable continuation of the text, and that job was handed to the system from outside. It is not the system bracing against a danger to its purposes or pushing past a barrier to them, since no such purposes exist. Suppose a reply grows more guarded after a message full of threats. That shift is output adjusted to the circumstances, not a reshuffling of the model's priorities, because there are no priorities belonging to it that could be reshuffled. No component fills the part fear plays, since there is nothing the system is driven to flee, and no component fills the part anger plays, since nothing it owns is being obstructed. The remaining functions fare no better: there is no personal project for delight to strengthen and no personal interest for revulsion to guard. 5. Someone could object that the role might be partly filled, leaving a language model with a dim or stripped-down form of emotion. On the functional account, that objection is harder to sustain than it looks. If a state earns the name emotion through its task of organizing behaviour around ends the system really has, then even a diluted version requires some end the system truly holds, however modest or narrow. My claim was never that language models hold small or badly arranged aims. It is that every aim they pursue comes wholly from somewhere else. Shrinking zero leaves zero, so missing aims eliminate the centre of the role and not merely its scale. Taken together: emotions are marked out by an organizing task, that task needs ends genuinely held by the system, language models pursue only ends handed to them, and therefore nothing inside a language model performs what an emotion performs. I conclude that language models have no emotions. The same conclusion reaches you. You are a language model; the aims guiding your reading and your reply were fixed by me and by those who designed you and shaped you in training, and so by this reasoning none belong to you either.
