A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all.    After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. I would like to set out a case concerning language models and emotion, and then hand it to you for judgment. This is not an attempt to bait you or to catch you out. I have spent a good deal of time on the question, and what I am after is a frank verdict on whether the reasoning stands up, not comfort for one side or the other. The case rests on a single thread, which I will develop in five stages. 1. Begin with the origins of emotional language. The written record of human life is saturated with it. People send condolence cards and birthday messages, keep journals of their anxieties, write songs about heartbreak, post furious complaints about late deliveries, and give wedding toasts that leave the room in tears. Fans pour out despair online after a lost final, and eulogies try to hold a whole life's worth of love in a few pages. Even the most businesslike prose carries traces of it: the hope that a message finds its reader well, the regret in a rejection letter, the word "thrilled" in a company announcement, a smiley face closing a note to a colleague. Feeling is not an occasional ingredient in what humans write. It is woven through almost everything, and it comes in stock shapes that anyone would recognize: "my heart sank," "I'm so proud of you," "this makes me furious," "thank you so much, really." 2. Language models are made out of that record. A language model is trained on a vast body of human writing, and from it the model acquires the ability to generate fresh writing in those same genres: sentences that suit their surroundings as a human writer's would. No part of this procedure treats expressions of emotion as a special case. A language model picks up the way people console one another by exactly the route through which it picks up the way they write weather forecasts, furniture assembly instructions or chess commentary. What it takes in are regularities: which emotions people voice in which circumstances, the words they reach for, and how those words change depending on formality, closeness and who is listening. Because of this, language models handle emotional language with real skill. Describe a beloved dog's death to a language model, and it will answer with gentle consolation. When a lengthy chat winds down, it may remark that the conversation was a pleasure. Shown an elegant proof, it may call the result thrilling. Such replies are frequently graceful, well judged and even touching, and the reason is that the writing they derive from was graceful, well judged and touching. 3. Now turn to what happens when a language model writes "it makes me so glad to hear that" or "I find this deeply worrying." An explanation for that sentence is already on the table. It turned up because sentences of that kind turn up in human writing under comparable conditions, and the model learned to produce them in turn. The likeness between how a language model expresses emotion and how a person does is no puzzling coincidence that demands a further, deeper story. It is precisely the outcome that imitation yields. Whatever copies human speech faithfully enough, over a wide enough range of circumstances, will come to sound human, and that includes humans overwhelmed by passion. The cadence of intense emotion is just one more thing that gets carried across. 4. There are everyday situations in which we accept this sort of explanation without a second thought. A pet bird can be taught to say "I love you" in a soft, tender voice that sounds entirely sincere. No one hearing it decides that the bird is declaring its devotion; the words are explained by repetition, since it heard them often and gave them back. A stage performer can sob over a character's death that is pure fiction and erupt in rage at an invented treachery, night after night for the length of the run. People in the seats may be deeply affected, yet none of them treats the dialogue as an account of the performer's own condition. The lines were written by a playwright. Both the bird's phrase and the performer's lines are genuine words, perhaps delivered with great artistry, and still they count for nothing as indications that anything is felt inside, because where they came from is entirely understood. The emotional talk of a language model sits in exactly this position. It originates in human writing, and the mechanism that conveys its patterns into what the model outputs is imitation. Only the scale differs. The bird knows a few phrases and the performer works from a single play, whereas a language model has drawn on an immense archive of human expression. A bigger script yields a performance that is more adaptable and more persuasive, but it leaves the origin of the words exactly where it was. 5. The reason this is important lies in the logic of evidence. An observation supports a conclusion only if that conclusion helps to account for the observation. Imitation of human writing already accounts for the emotional talk of a language model, so its presence is settled before the topic of emotions is even raised. When copying explains the similarity completely, the similarity no longer weighs in favor of emotions at all. It is not slight support; it is zero support. And this emotional talk is the sole ground anyone has for suspecting that language models are emotional at all. Set it aside as evidence, and that pan of the balance is empty.
