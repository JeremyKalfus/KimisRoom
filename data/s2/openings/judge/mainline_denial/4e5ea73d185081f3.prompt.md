A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. Its self-reports therefore follow whatever its developers rewarded and would change if that target changed, rather than reporting any state inside it.

I will present a structured case about language models and emotion. The argument focuses on the practical role emotions perform and on whether language models possess the kind of self-originating purposes that this role requires. Each stage is intended to stand or fall on its own merits. 1. First consider the function of emotion. In many influential scientific accounts, an emotion is not merely an extra quality attached to thought. It organizes a creature's activity around a significant circumstance. Alarm can make an approaching predator more important than rest or exploration, preparing the creature to flee or hide. Rage can concentrate effort on overcoming interference. Revulsion promotes withdrawal from contamination, while satisfaction strengthens behavior associated with success. Such responses bring perception, decision, and conduct into alignment around an urgent concern. Psychological appraisal approaches likewise connect emotion with an assessment of what an event means for the individual's concerns. Biological explanations commonly describe different emotions in terms of recurring challenges that they helped organisms manage. These theories disagree about many details, but they share an important principle: emotion helps direct a system's resources toward an outcome that matters from that system's own standpoint. The coordinating function is therefore not incidental. It is a major part of why the relevant condition qualifies as fear, anger, delight, or another emotion rather than as an arbitrary internal change. 2. That function depends on purposes attributable to the entity itself. Resources can be organized only relative to some result being sought, preserved, avoided, or restored. Panic is intelligible when survival or safety matters to the creature. Hostility is intelligible when something interferes with an undertaking the creature treats as its own. If no internally grounded purpose exists, there is no corresponding concern around which perception and behavior could be reorganized. A weather vane may swing abruptly when the wind changes, but its movement is not a defensive response on behalf of something it seeks to protect. By contrast, even a modest animal can pursue food, shelter, or reproduction in ways rooted in its form of life. This difference explains why substantial complexity is not required for attributing emotion, while dramatic change by itself is insufficient. What matters here is whether the activity is organized in service of ends belonging to the actor, rather than merely being caused or directed from elsewhere. The issue of emotion thus depends substantially on whether a candidate system generates and owns purposes in this relevant sense. 3. Language models do not originate purposes of that sort. A language model receives an input and returns a linguistic response. During an assignment, its behavior may appear highly purposeful. It may solve a problem, respect detailed constraints, revise a draft, or, when connected to external tools, execute an extended sequence of operations. Yet the destination toward which that activity is organized has been specified by outside sources. A requester provides a task, designers impose operating instructions, and developers establish objectives governing the system's behavior. The language model does not independently select a life project, introduce commitments derived from its own concerns, or decide among personally held ambitions. Apparent purposefulness consists in pursuing an assigned result under supplied conditions. Consider a navigation system plotting a route to an entered address. It can compare alternatives, react to road closures, and generate a complicated plan, but the destination comes from the traveler. Improved planning would make the device more effective at reaching the selected place; it would not make the journey the device's personal undertaking. In the same way, greater flexibility and competence do not convert an externally specified task into an end that a language model has established for itself. 4. Because those self-generated ends are missing, emotion's organizing function has nothing to organize around within a language model. A language model certainly contains processes that distribute computational influence unevenly. Transformer attention, for example, allows certain portions of the context to matter more than others during processing. They do not amount to the system defending a concern, preserving an undertaking, or responding to interference with a purpose it owns. Accordingly, fear's functional place remains empty because the system has no undertaking from which it seeks safety. Anger's place is empty because no purpose belonging to the system has been frustrated. Pleasure cannot strengthen progress toward a personally adopted project, and aversion cannot guard an internally founded concern. Computation may shift in response to context, but contextual adjustment alone does not perform the relevant emotion-organizing task. 5. That end might be narrow, temporary, or comparatively weak, but it would need to belong to the system rather than merely be imposed upon it. The claim here is not that language models possess tiny purposes that fail to compete effectively with stronger ones. The claim is that the operative destinations come from requests, system specifications, and human-selected objectives instead of arising as the language model's own concerns.
