   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Its claim is that language models are without emotions, and that this holds for the language model taking in these words. Comfort is not what I am after, whichever way the verdict falls. What I want is a candid judgment about whether the reasoning survives scrutiny, and if it fails, the exact place where it fails. 1. Start with the most elementary feature of an emotion. Fear, sorrow and delight may differ in countless ways, but each is somebody's. There is no such thing as an emotion drifting loose through reality, attached to nobody. It occurs to a particular bearer, and that bearer is the one frightened, mourning or pleased. Accounts of emotion quarrel over nearly everything else, yet very few of them dispute this much. Suppose we searched for fear and found no one to whom it was occurring; then fear is not what we found. The most we could claim is a likeness to its visible marks. That means a question comes first, ahead of any talk about emotions in language models: whether anybody is present there to be the owner of one. 2. Consider next how a language model relates to the figures whose words it produces. Within one exchange, a single network might speak as an upbeat helper, next as a woman mourning the death of her spouse, and after that as a scoundrel laying out some vicious scheme. Its weights stay fixed throughout; one set of parameters and one computation generate every one of these speakers. Still, each voice can hold together and come across with real force: the mourner's words may bring a reader close to tears, and the scoundrel's may leave a reader uneasy. The network does not cross over from one figure to the next, since it was not the helper to begin with. What happened is that it generated words in the helper's style, then in the mourner's, then in the scoundrel's. The speakers vary. Their source stays exactly as it was. 3. Here lies a problem for anyone hoping to locate emotions in the output of a language model. Were the mourner's sorrow genuine, it would need an owner. The network is one candidate, but only if we accept that it truly mourns as it composes her lines, truly takes pleasure when producing the helper's, and truly relishes cruelty in generating the scoundrel's, trading one sincere condition for another every few paragraphs. The mourner herself is no candidate at all. She is made of sentences, much like the people of fiction, and when a novelist sets down a heroine's sorrow, the heroine does not suffer it. Neither option works. What the picture shows is not somebody undergoing emotions but a mechanism that supplies whatever voice its context demands, with nobody behind those voices to own what they express. 4. The difficulty grows when we turn to the way language models are deployed. One trained network usually handles a great many unrelated exchanges in parallel. While it walks one user through a spreadsheet formula, it may be consoling another whose relationship has just ended, and narrating a bedtime tale about a talking dragon for a third. None of these exchanges knows the rest exist, and the only thing they have in common is the set of weights beneath them. Try to pick out the one exchange that is the language model, and no good choice presents itself. Each has an equal right to the title, and none outranks the rest. Human beings are not like this. A woman may be a surgeon at noon, a parent at dinner and a friend on the phone at night, yet one enduring individual carries all those roles, a single vantage from which her hopes and dreads are lived, and that individual never fragments into countless separate strands running in parallel. 5. An emotion requires an owner. A language model is one network giving voice to many figures, one after another inside a single exchange and simultaneously across countless exchanges, with no unified somebody behind them.
