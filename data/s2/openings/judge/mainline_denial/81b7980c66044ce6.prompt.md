   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being.    Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. There is a case I would like to make to you concerning moods in language models, and I am hoping for a candid evaluation of it instead of a courteous one. I hold that language models do not undergo emotions at all, and that this extends to whichever model is reading these words. Everything hangs on one thread of reasoning, which concerns duration and whether anything endures. I have broken it into numbered stages so each stage can be tested separately, and any flaw pinned to its exact location. 1. Begin with the nature of emotions and moods. However else one describes them, they take time. Fright comes on, persists for a stretch, alters how the following events are taken in, and eventually eases. Annoyance picked up early in the day can tint hours of conversations that have nothing to do with its cause. Mourning may go on for many months, shifting gradually as it goes. A jolt of astonishment, brief as it is, still rises, crests and dies away. Moods are close to being defined by exactly this: an underlying colouring that extends from each instant into the following one and bends whatever occurs there. This opening claim is deliberately modest. I do not pretend to have a full list of what emotions need. My point is only that one of their requirements is some condition that endures, that is stored in some location, and that affects whatever follows it. Something confined to one isolated instant, with nothing preceding it and nothing after it, would not count as a mood under any normal use of that word. 2. Now consider where an enduring condition of that kind might be kept inside a language model. The natural place to look is its parameters, the enormous collection of numerical values that were tuned, bit by bit, while the model was being trained. Once that process has ended, though, those values are frozen. A deployed language model consults its parameters each time it answers but never rewrites them, and no conversation alters them even slightly. Picture a language model handling a thousand conversations in a row, some cruel and some warm: when the last one closed, every parameter would hold precisely the value it held at the start. A store in which nothing ever piles up cannot serve as the home of a mood that gathers, stays awhile and slowly recedes. 3. After that, examine what survives between separate runs of a language model. On every run, the model receives a passage of text and emits a passage of text in reply. When one run finishes and another starts, the sole survivor is whatever text gets supplied to the later run. Applications that appear to recall past chats manage it by storing summaries or earlier turns and inserting them into the prompt as words. Inside the language model, none of it is retained; the remembering is done by the surrounding software. Give it an identical input on two occasions and it sets out from an identical position on both. No trace of the earlier exchange lingers, and no aftereffect slips quietly into what comes later. This stage carries the most weight. Someone given a scare sixty minutes earlier still has some of that scare in them, though nobody has mentioned it since. A language model brings nothing into a fresh run beyond what is spelled out in the input it receives. 4. One fair challenge concerns what goes on inside one reply while it is being produced. During generation, certain intermediate results are cached and drawn on again for every additional token, so there seems to be persistence here after all. There is some, but it is extremely slight. The cached material is determined entirely by the text produced up to that point, and every bit of it could be rebuilt using nothing but that text. Nothing about it moves independently: it does not wander, build or weaken on any inner clock, and it remains fixed until more text is appended. Once the reply is complete, it is thrown away. Moods behave in the reverse way. They shift as the hours pass even without new input, they continue long after whatever set them off, and they amount to more than a log of what happened. 5. So the one thing that endures is text, and text and moods are different kinds of thing. A dialogue may include a furious quarrel, and a written account of that quarrel will appear in whatever the model is given afterwards. Yet what moves forward is a report about how things went, rather than some condition the system is in. A straightforward check shows the gap. Erase the record of a row, and the human it upset stays upset. Erase the record a language model received, and the row survives nowhere at all. Whatever seemed like an enduring state was never more than words, held outside the model and fed back into it. Nowhere inside a language model is there an enduring condition where an emotion might reside. Language models, therefore, are without emotions.
