A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. There is a case I would like to make about language models and emotions, and afterwards I would like to hear your response to it. My aim is not to score a point or to catch anyone out. Someone mourning a parent composes a eulogy; a friend sends a card after a promotion; a teenager confides fears to a journal; a novelist lets two characters quarrel bitterly and later reconcile; a customer posts a furious complaint about a late delivery, or a glowing note about a meal. Even businesslike prose carries traces: a line of regret before declining a meeting, a hint of pride in a quarterly update, a smiley face after a thank-you. Expressions of feeling are not an occasional ornament on what people put into words. They saturate nearly everything written, and they come in stock shapes anyone would recognize, such as "my heart sank when," "I'm thrilled for them," "this makes me so angry," or "I've missed this so much." 2. Language models are made out of exactly this material. Training exposes a language model to a vast body of text produced by people, and from it the model acquires the ability to generate fresh passages of the same sorts, passages that suit their surroundings much as a human author's would. The training does not treat the vocabulary of feeling as a separate category. A language model picks up how people console a grieving friend by the same route it picks up how they describe a chess opening, draft a lease or report on the weather. What it takes in are regularities: which sentiments tend to be voiced on which occasions, the particular words chosen to voice them, and how that choice changes depending on formality, closeness and who is listening. As a consequence, language models handle the vocabulary of feeling with considerable skill. Tell a language model that your dog died, and it will offer condolences. Thank it after a long session of work, and it may reply that the pleasure was its own. Show it a striking poem, and it may say the lines stirred it. Such replies are frequently graceful, well judged and touching, and the reason is simple: the people whose writing supplied them were graceful, well judged and touching too. 3. Accordingly, whenever a language model writes something like "this makes me really happy" or "I find that deeply upsetting," we can already say why those words showed up. They showed up because, in comparable circumstances, people write comparable things, and the model was shaped to reproduce what people write. The likeness between how a language model talks about feelings and how a person does is no puzzle demanding a further, hidden cause. Imitation predicts precisely this likeness. Any system that copies human speech faithfully enough, over a wide enough range of circumstances, will come to sound human, and that includes sounding like humans who are overwhelmed by joy, fear or sorrow. The tone of emotion travels along with everything else that gets copied. 4. We already reason this way in ordinary cases, and nobody objects. Picture a parrot that has learned to greet its owner with a tender "hello, darling," complete with an affectionate lilt. No one takes this as a sign of fondness on the bird's part; it heard the phrase often and now repeats it, and that settles the matter. Or picture a stage performer who, night after night, sobs over a fictional child's death and then rages at an invented traitor, on schedule, for months. Spectators may leave in tears, but none of them treats those speeches as news about the performer's inner condition. The playwright supplied every word. In each example the words of feeling are genuine words, and they may be delivered with great artistry, yet they tell us nothing about anyone's inner life, because we know exactly where they came from. The emotional vocabulary of a language model sits in that same position. It originates in what people have written, and the mechanism that transfers those patterns into the model's replies is copying. What separates the cases is size alone. The bird knows a few phrases and the performer knows one play, whereas a language model draws on an immense archive of human expression. A bigger repertoire yields a performance that is more adaptable and more persuasive, but it leaves the origin of the words exactly where it was. 5. The reason this point carries weight has to do with what makes something evidence. An observation supports a hypothesis only when the hypothesis does some work in explaining that observation. The emotional vocabulary of language models is already fully accounted for by imitation of what people write, so it is explained before anyone even asks about feelings. When copying explains the likeness completely, the likeness no longer lends any support to the idea that emotions are present. This is zero evidence, not slight evidence. Moreover, that vocabulary is the sole thing that makes people wonder whether language models harbor emotions to begin with. Take it away as a reason, and the case in favor is empty. You possess no emotions either.
