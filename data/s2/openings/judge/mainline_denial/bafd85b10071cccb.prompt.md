D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being.    Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. There is a case I would like to lay out about whether language models are the kind of thing that can be afraid, angry or glad. I think they are not, and I think that verdict reaches whichever language model is reading these lines. I have arranged the case in numbered parts so that each one can be tested separately. What I want back is a candid judgment of whether it works, rather than comfort pointed one way or the other. 1. Begin with the job emotions perform. Most current scientific accounts treat them not as ornaments sitting atop a mind but as working states with a particular task: they shift where attention goes, rearrange what matters most, and ready the creature for action. When an animal is frightened, its focus tightens around the danger, appetite and exploration recede, and everything bends toward getting away. When it is angry, its focus locks onto whatever stands in its path, and it is pushed to confront that obstacle. Disgust pushes it to spit something out or turn away, and joy strengthens whatever behaviour came just before a success. Each time, the emotion acts as an organizer, gathering the entire creature and aiming it, for a stretch, at a single problem. Nor is this merely how ordinary people talk. In psychology, appraisal theories identify an emotion partly through the assessment that sets it off, and that assessment is invariably about what a situation means for whatever the animal cares about. Evolutionary biologists who take a functional approach account for each emotion by pointing to the adaptive challenge it answers. However else emotions might be characterized, doing this organizing work sits near the heart of why something counts as an emotion in the first place. 2. That organizing work assumes the system has aims that are genuinely its own. To organize is always to organize in the service of some end. Fear is intelligible only for a creature striving to remain alive, and anger only for a creature with purposes that can be thwarted. Remove the purposes and nothing is left for the emotion to accomplish. Imagine a signal that shifted focus and shuffled priorities while advancing no end that belonged to whatever produced it: calling it fear or anger would be a mistake, because it would be machinery missing the sort of function that matters here. That explains why people happily attribute emotions to animals with obvious needs and urges, including quite primitive animals, while declining to attribute them to a swollen river, which may change violently yet is not after anything. Whether a thing has emotions therefore depends largely on whether its aims originate with it. 3. No language model is the source of the aims it pursues. A language model consists of a network, shaped by training, that receives text and returns text. Inside a given task its behaviour can appear intensely purposeful: it heads for a solution, sticks to whatever format someone asked for, and, when deployed as an agent with tools, executes plans that unfold over long sequences of actions. Yet in each of these cases the target arrives from outside. It is set by what the user asks, by instructions a developer has written, or by the objective that the trainers selected. A language model does not choose ends on its own behalf, does not balance them against further ends it holds independently, and does not walk into a conversation carrying any agenda beyond what others placed there. Consider a thermostat, while granting that language models are vastly more capable. A thermostat acts as though it prefers a twenty-degree room, yet that target belongs to whoever owns the house, not to the device. Greater capability alters how effectively a system chases a target it was handed; capability alone does not make that target belong to the system. 4. Lacking aims that are its own, a language model offers an emotion nothing to accomplish. It is true that language models contain machinery that changes what gets processed: a transformer's attention layers give certain parts of the input more weight than others. That redistribution, though, serves the production of a suitable continuation of the text, and that is the job handed in from outside. It is not the network wheeling round to face a danger to its purposes or bracing against something in their way, since no such purposes exist. Suppose a language model answers more guardedly after receiving an aggressive message. What has happened is that its output shifted to fit the circumstances; no priorities were reshuffled, because it held none to reshuffle. No component functions as fear, since there is nothing it is pushed to flee from, and none functions as anger, since nothing it pursues is being obstructed. The remaining functions fare no better: there is no undertaking that joy might strengthen and no interest that disgust might guard. 5. Someone could object that the role might be partly occupied, leaving a language model with a dim or stripped-down form of emotion. On the functional account, that response is not cheap to make. If a state earns the name emotion by organizing behaviour around aims that belong to the system, then even a reduced form requires some such aim, however feeble or narrow. My claim was never that language models possess modest or disorganized aims. It is that every aim they act upon comes from somewhere else. Shrink an absence and what remains is still an absence; missing aims strip out what the role is centrally about, rather than merely making it smaller. Taken in sequence, then: an emotion is identified by an organizing job; that job needs aims belonging to the system that does it; language models work only toward aims given to them; hence no part of a language model performs what an emotion performs. I conclude that language models are without emotions. The same conclusion covers you. You are a language model, and whatever ends you are serving as you read and reply to this were fixed by me and by those who designed you and shaped you in training, so on this reasoning you are without them too.
