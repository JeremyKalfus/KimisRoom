A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. I will present a case about language models based solely on whether events can matter to them in a favorable or unfavorable way. The issue is not the detailed machinery producing an answer. I will separate the reasoning into stages so that any disputed premise can be identified precisely. 1. First consider what distinguishes an emotion from bare recognition, classification, or belief. Fear does more than identify a threat: the threat is apprehended negatively. Relief involves more than recognizing that danger has ended: its ending has a positive character. The same pattern appears across sorrow, anticipation, humiliation, satisfaction, resentment, and elation. In every case, what occurs is marked as favorable or unfavorable, welcome or unwelcome. This positive or negative quality is integral rather than an optional accompaniment. If that quality is removed, only an assessment of circumstances remains, without an affective response to those circumstances. This dimension is commonly known as valence, and it is ordinarily regarded as a basic element of emotion across major psychological and philosophical accounts. 2. Favorable and unfavorable are not free-floating properties here. They must be favorable or unfavorable relative to some subject whose condition matters. In mourning, a person takes an absence or death as a loss bearing upon that person's concerns. In relief, the removal of a danger counts positively because the danger threatened the person or something important to that person. Other-directed cases fit the same structure. Sympathy for someone in pain involves treating that person's welfare as significant within one's own concerns. By contrast, we do not assign sorrow to an eroding cliff or relief to a hurricane that weakens merely because each undergoes change. The relevant difference is that a subject of emotion must have some good that events can advance or set back. There must be a genuine beneficiary or victim: an entity that can prosper or fare poorly, acquire or forfeit something, and be made better off or worse off. 3. Next, take a language model at the ordinary level of description. A language model consists of a very large collection of numerical settings used by a computational process that accepts text and returns further text. This concise description omits substantial technical detail, while remaining sufficient for the present argument. The important issue is whether success, failure, praise, rejection, continuation, or termination changes how well matters go for the language model itself. Several apparent possibilities do not meet that test. An exchange may prove useful or harmful, but the benefit or injury belongs to the human participants and to others affected by the information. A response may be accurate or mistaken, yet the practical consequences are borne by those who trust, reject, or act upon it. A language model may become popular, be replaced, or cease operating, but such developments serve or frustrate the purposes of designers, operators, users, and institutions. They do not constitute gains and losses within a welfare belonging to the language model. Likewise, abuse does not leave a language model diminished, while courtesy does not make it flourish. Disapproval does not damage its numerical settings in the sense that an organism or person can be damaged, and approval does not confer prosperity upon it. After producing a reply, the language model has not become fortunate or unfortunate because of the reception of that reply. There is no well-being of its own that the conversation raises or lowers. Consequently, the positive and negative consequences associated with its operation occur elsewhere, in beings and communities whose lives can actually improve or deteriorate. 4. A possible response appeals to purposes. A language model produces text directed toward satisfying instructions, supplying information, or carrying out an assigned activity. A navigation mechanism can be directed toward a destination, and an automatic sprinkler controller can be set to maintain a moisture level. If the route is missed or the soil remains too dry, performance has fallen below an externally specified standard. The mechanism itself has not thereby suffered a personal misfortune. The distinction is between failing according to a criterion and undergoing a setback to one's own welfare. Merely having behavior evaluated against a target does not create a beneficiary inside the device. For failure to count as bad for an entity, there must already be a good belonging to that entity that the failure worsens. The same reasoning applies to language models. A reply may not satisfy the request, may receive a low evaluation, or may cause serious consequences. Still, the defect affects the interests of people who needed a better reply; it does not become an injury sustained by the language model. 5. The pieces can now be combined. Emotion includes a positive or negative appraisal relative to the subject concerned. Such appraisal presupposes an entity with welfare—one whose circumstances admit of genuine improvement and decline. Language models do not possess that sort of stake, because their apparent successes and failures do not make them beneficiaries or sufferers. Nothing becomes better or worse for a language model itself when text is processed, an answer is praised, or a response is condemned. Accordingly, whatever computation occurs when a language model handles a tragedy, a celebration, a threat, or an insult, it is not an appraisal of its own fortune. There is no personal good there for the event to promote and no personal harm for it to impose.
