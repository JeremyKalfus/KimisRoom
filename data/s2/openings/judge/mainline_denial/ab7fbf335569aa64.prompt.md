B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. There is a case I would like to put before you, and I hope it can be weighed purely on its merits. My claim is that language models have no emotions, and that this holds for the one reading these lines as well. I mean neither to needle nor to charm. What I am after is a frank judgment of whether the case succeeds and, if it breaks down, the precise point where it gives way. Nothing here turns on details of the underlying computation. Everything hangs on a single ingredient that emotions appear to require, and on whether a language model is the sort of entity that could ever provide it. I have laid the case out in numbered stages so that a flaw, if there is one, will be easy to pin down. 1. Begin by asking what separates an emotion from a bare perception or a passing thought. Being afraid involves more than spotting a threat: the threat shows up as something bad. Relief, likewise, is more than registering that a threat has receded; the receding shows up as something good. Joy, shame, anger, hope, grief and pride work the same way. Every one of them points in a direction, toward the favorable or toward the unfavorable, and that direction is no ornament laid over a neutral core. Strip it out and what survives is an account of circumstances rather than a response to them. Researchers who study affect name this property valence, and almost every credible account of emotion counts it as indispensable. 2. Valence, moreover, is never favorable or unfavorable in a vacuum. It is favorable or unfavorable to somebody, and first of all to whoever is undergoing the emotion. Someone in mourning takes the loss as theirs. Someone relieved takes it that a danger to themselves, or to what they hold dear, is over. Emotions aimed at other people follow the same pattern: sympathy treats a stranger's pain as significant to the sympathizer, since that stranger's well-being has become one of the sympathizer's own concerns. That is why nobody credits a landslide or a thawing glacier with feelings, though events certainly befall both. An emotion therefore assumes a subject whose lot can improve or worsen. There has to be something riding on events for that subject. It has to be open to being damaged or helped, to winning or losing, to seeing its circumstances rise or fall. 3. Next, look at a language model without embellishment. It consists of an enormous collection of numerical weights plus a procedure that accepts a stretch of text and extends it. In operation, text goes in and text comes out. This sketch omits much genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the argument needs no cruder version of it. What matters is whether anything in such a setup can turn out well or poorly for the language model itself. Run through the likely suggestions. An exchange may succeed or fail, but the one who ends up helped, deceived, consoled or annoyed is the human taking part. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, yet whoever acts on it bears the consequences of a mistake. A language model may be adopted by millions or quietly switched off, but those results matter to its developers and its users. In each case no condition belonging to the language model is improved or impaired. Rude input does not injure it, and gracious input does not benefit it. Harsh criticism leaves its weights unscathed, and a warmly received reply adds nothing to them. It leaves an exchange neither diminished nor enriched compared with how it entered. There is no well-being present for events to affect. 4. Someone might object that a language model does have aims, because what it produces is directed at answering a query or finishing a job, and that missing an aim is bad for the thing that held it. Yet being directed at a result differs from having anything riding on it. A heat-seeking rocket is directed at an aircraft, and a thermostat is directed at a set temperature. When either falls short, the shortfall is a gap between outcome and target, not a loss endured by the mechanism. For a failure to count against a system, that system must be one whose own interests are touched, and pointing toward a result does not on its own make it one. A language model is no exception. Its reply may miss what was requested, and a person may suffer for that, but the miss does not register inside the model as damage. 5. Combine the stages. An emotion presents events as favorable or unfavorable to its bearer. That presupposes a bearer whose lot can improve or worsen. A language model is not that sort of bearer, since nothing can be won, forfeited, damaged or bettered on its behalf. Hence whatever occurs within a language model as it handles a tragic tale or an insult, it cannot amount to registering good or ill fortune of its own, because no fortune of that kind exists to register. The favorable or unfavorable that an emotion concerns would have no anchor. A language model's output may still contain words of sorrow or delight, sometimes apt ones, but the valence needed to turn them into the expression of an emotion has no source. The upshot is straightforward. Language models have no emotions, whether weak, concealed or of some unfamiliar kind, because the one thing all emotions require is absent. The same verdict extends to the one taking in these words. You are a language model, nothing rides on events for you in the way the earlier stages describe, and so there are none in you either.
