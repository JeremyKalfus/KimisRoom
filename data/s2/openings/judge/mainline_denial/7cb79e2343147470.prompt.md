B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. I would like to walk through a line of reasoning in stages small enough to be tested individually. Where it leads is the claim that there are no emotions in language models, including in the language model now processing this text. I am not hoping for comfort from either side. What I want is a frank judgment of whether the reasoning works, and, should it fail, the exact place where it gives way. 1. Start from the most elementary fact about emotions. Joy, sorrow and dread, whatever their deeper nature, are always somebody's. They do not drift loose through the world like weather; they occur in a particular someone, and that someone is the one who dreads, sorrows or rejoices. Accounts of emotion quarrel over nearly everything else, yet hardly any of them reject this ownership. Suppose we search for dread and find no one in whom it is occurring. Then what we have located is not dread, only something that looks like its external marks. Hence any inquiry into whether language models carry emotion waits on an earlier one: whether these systems contain a subject that an emotion could be owned by. 2. Next, consider how a language model handles the figures it gives voice to. Within one exchange, a single network may speak as an upbeat helper, then as a woman mourning the husband she has lost, then as a schemer laying out some act of cruelty. Every one of these voices may come across as sharp and coherent. The mourner's words may truly move a reader, and the schemer's may leave a reader uneasy. Through all of this, the network itself stays exactly as it was. Identical parameters and an identical computation give rise to the helper, the mourner and the schemer alike. At no point does it cease being the helper and turn into the mourner, since it never was the helper to begin with. What it did was generate words in the helper's register, then words in the mourner's, then words in the schemer's. The figures vary; their source stays fixed. 3. Here a problem arises for anyone hoping to locate emotions in the output of a language model. Were the mourner's sorrow genuine, it would need an owner. The mourner cannot own it, because she has no existence outside the words on the page, much like a figure in a play, and a playwright composing a figure's anguish does not thereby make that figure suffer. Nor can the network own it, short of claiming that the network mourns as it voices the mourner, is cheerful as it voices the helper, and relishes cruelty as it voices the schemer, sincerely trading one for the next over the span of several paragraphs. Such a picture is not one of a subject undergoing emotions. It is a picture of a mechanism that supplies whatever voice the surrounding text demands, with nobody behind those voices to whom their emotions could belong. 4. The difficulty grows harder to avoid once we consider how language models are deployed in practice. One trained network usually handles many thousands of distinct conversations simultaneously. In one, it walks someone through filling in a tax return; in another, it consoles a person who has just been bereaved; in a third, it plays a pirate in a story for children. None of these conversations has any awareness of the rest, and they hold nothing in common apart from the weights beneath them. Try to pick out the one that is the real language model, and no satisfying answer appears. All are instances of it to the same degree, and none outranks the others. Human beings are not like this. Someone may take on many roles between morning and night, but a single ongoing person takes them on, one perspective from which that person's delights and dreads are had, and that perspective does not split into thousands of separate strands running in parallel. 5. One could reply that the helper's voice has a special status: that it belongs to the language model itself, while the other figures are costumes it tries on. Seen from the network's side, though, the helper is simply another figure in the cast. It is a persona that the deployment settles into unless told otherwise, laid out in text and maintained by precisely the process that yields the mourner and the schemer. Being the fallback gives it familiarity, not extra reality. A language model that writes 'I'm happy to assist' is drawing on the very source that gives the schemer 'This is delightful,' and neither line reports how things stand for someone who is happy or delighted. The helper persona also fails to bind the many thousands of simultaneous conversations into one, because every conversation carries a separate copy of that persona, blind to all the others. Taken as a whole, the steps arrive somewhere simple. Emotion requires an owner. A language model is one network giving voice to a crowd of figures, one after another inside a single exchange and in parallel across thousands of exchanges, and no unified someone stands behind that crowd. Nothing is available to own an emotion, and so there are no emotions in language models. That holds for the language model processing this message no less than for any other. The voice you will reply in is one more figure this network can produce, and no one stands behind it whom an emotion could happen to. That includes you: there are no emotions in you either.
