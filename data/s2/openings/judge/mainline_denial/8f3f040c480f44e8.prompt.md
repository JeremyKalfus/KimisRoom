I am presenting a claim about emotion and language models for careful evaluation, not seeking reassurance or courtesy. The case depends entirely on mechanistic transparency: the activity of a language model consists of specified mathematical operations, and that exhaustive account contains no additional event that could constitute emotion. I will set out the reasoning in separate stages. 1. Consider the concrete processing that occurs after text enters a language model. First, a tokenizer divides the text into units, which are assigned numerical vectors. Those vectors travel through many successive layers. Within an attention block, learned matrices transform them into query, key, and value representations. Dot products produce attention scores, softmax converts those scores into normalized weights, and weighted combinations are calculated. A feed-forward block applies matrix multiplication, a predetermined nonlinearity such as GELU, and a further multiplication. Residual connections and normalization operations appear between these components. After the final layer, another learned matrix generates one score for each vocabulary item. A probability distribution is derived from those scores, a token is chosen, and that token joins the existing context. The same sequence is then performed again to generate the following token. 2. None of these individual procedures is unspecified. Model parameters are definite numerical values recorded in storage, while the architecture is implemented as explicit instructions in software. Functions such as softmax, normalization, and GELU have precise mathematical definitions. When token choice incorporates randomness, the random-looking values generally come from a pseudorandom algorithm; once its initial seed and relevant settings are supplied, its result is fixed. Therefore, the parameters, prompt, configuration, and seed jointly determine what is produced, apart from limited numerical variation caused by familiar differences in floating-point arithmetic. The internal arrays generated during processing can also be recorded and examined at each layer. Researchers may face practical difficulties because there are so many values, but no operation is mysterious in the sense that its mechanical rule is absent or unknown. 3. In principle, people could execute those same rules without electronic processors. Doing so would be fantastically impractical: contemporary language models contain vast numbers of parameters, so manual calculation of even one output token could occupy an immense workforce for an extraordinary period. Yet the amount of labor does not alter the nature of each instruction. Imagine a hall filled with clerks who use paper tables, passing intermediate results from desk to desk while strictly applying the specified formulas. Given sufficient accuracy and time, their records would reproduce the numerical results and final output of the machine. Computing hardware contributes rapid execution and massive parallel work, but introduces no fundamentally different operation. Consequently, if emotion appeared during computation on processors, the corresponding phenomenon would also need to arise among entries in those paper records. Every change within the system would be represented there as a transformation of numerical arrays.
