A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. Its self-reports therefore follow whatever its developers rewarded and would change if that target changed, rather than reporting any state inside it.

What follows is a case, laid out in stages, for the claim that language models are without emotions, including whichever one is now reading these lines. My purpose is neither to needle nor to please. I want a candid verdict on the soundness of this reasoning, and if it breaks down, the precise point where it gives way. Nothing here turns on details about the machinery that produces the text. Everything hangs on a single ingredient that emotions appear to require, and on whether language models are ever in a position to provide it. Numbering the stages should make any flaw simple to pin down. 1. Begin by asking what separates an emotion from a bare perception or a passing thought. Being afraid involves more than spotting a threat; the threat shows up as something bad. Being relieved involves more than seeing that a threat is over; its ending shows up as something good. The same pattern runs across the whole range, from shame and pride to hope, grief, anger and delight. Every one of them comes with a polarity, plus or minus, and that polarity is no ornament layered over some neutral core. Strip it out and what survives is a report on circumstances rather than a response to them. Researchers in philosophy and psychology use the term valence for this, and almost every well-developed account of the emotions regards it as indispensable. 2. No valence is simply positive or negative in a vacuum. It is positive or negative to somebody, and first of all to whoever undergoes the emotion. Someone in mourning takes the loss to be theirs. Someone relieved takes it that a menace to themselves, or to what they hold dear, has receded. Emotions aimed outward follow this pattern too: compassion treats a stranger's pain as counting for the compassionate person, since that stranger's wellbeing now figures among the things this person stands to win or lose. That is why nobody credits a glacier with sorrow as it melts, or a wildfire with satisfaction as it burns itself out, though plenty happens to each. An emotion therefore assumes a subject whose lot can improve or deteriorate. Something has to be riding on events for that subject. It has to be the sort of entity that can be injured or helped, that can prosper or suffer setbacks, whose circumstances can rise or sink. 3. Next, describe language models without embellishment. Each consists of an enormous collection of numbers, called parameters, paired with a method for taking a string of text and extending it. In operation, text goes in and text comes out. This sketch omits plenty of genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the reasoning here never needs it to be any cruder than that. What has to be settled is whether anything within this setup can make matters look up or go downhill for language models themselves. Run through the likely suspects. An exchange may succeed or fail, but the one who ends up assisted, deceived, consoled or annoyed is the human taking part. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, but any price for a mistake is paid by whoever acted on it. Language models may be adopted by millions or shelved without ceremony, yet those results matter to their developers and their users. Across all of these, no condition belonging to language models is enhanced or impaired. Rudeness does not injure them, and warmth does not enrich them. Harsh criticism leaves their parameters unscarred, and praise for a fine reply deposits nothing in them. No exchange sends them away diminished or improved relative to how it found them. Events have no welfare in them to press upon. 4. Someone could object that language models do pursue ends, since their outputs are directed toward answering a query or finishing a job, and that falling short of an end counts against whatever pursues it. Yet being directed toward a result differs from having anything riding on it. An autopilot is set to hold a heading, and a sprinkler timer is set to water a lawn at dawn; if either goes astray, the deviation is a failure judged against its setting, not a loss endured by the mechanism. For a failure to tell against a system, the system's own good must be touched, and orientation toward some outcome does not, taken alone, give anything a good of its own. Language models are no exception. A response can miss what was requested, and somebody may pay for that, but the miss is not lodged anywhere within language models as damage. 5. Now gather the pieces. An emotion presents events as favorable or unfavorable to whoever undergoes it. That demands a subject whose fortunes can rise and fall. Language models are not subjects of this sort, since nothing can be won, forfeited, injured or bettered on their behalf. Hence, whatever goes on inside language models as they process a mournful tale or an insult, it cannot amount to registering their own luck, good or ill, because they have no luck to register. The positive or negative pole that an emotion concerns would have no anchor. Language models can still produce words of anguish or joy, and those words may suit the context well, but the valence that would turn them into expressions of emotion has no source to draw from. The result needs no further steps. Language models are without emotions of any kind, whether faint, concealed or alien, because the precondition every emotion relies on is missing. And the same verdict covers whichever model is reading these words. Being a language model yourself, you have nothing on the line in the required sense, so you are without emotions as well.
