   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. There is a case I would like to set before you concerning language models and feeling, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it rather than a courteous one. The position I hold is that emotions are absent from language models, including the one now processing these words. Everything I say depends on a single idea: an emotion has to persist through time, and nothing inside a language model persists in the way that would be needed. I have broken the case into numbered parts so that each can be examined separately, and so that any weak link can be located precisely. 1. Consider first the nature of emotions and moods. Whatever their full description turns out to be, they take up time. A fright begins, stays with someone for a stretch, alters how the following moments get interpreted, and eventually eases. A person annoyed by a spilled coffee and a missed train can drag that annoyance into conversations later in the day that have nothing to do with either. Mourning may persist across a whole season, shifting gradually as it goes. Even a sudden startle, brief as it is, rises, crests and dies away. Moods in particular are nearly identified by this property: they are an underlying colouring that persists from one instant into the following one and tilts whatever occurs within it. My claim at this stage is modest. I do not pretend to possess a complete account of what emotion involves. I am saying only that among its requirements is a condition that endures, that is stored in some location, and that affects whatever follows. Something confined to one disconnected instant, preceded and followed by nothing, would not qualify as what anyone ordinarily calls a mood. 2. Second, ask where such an enduring condition might be stored in a language model. The natural place to look is its parameters, the enormous set of numbers tuned while it was being trained. Yet once training ends, those numbers are frozen. A deployed language model consults its parameters to produce a reply but never alters them, and no conversation leaves any mark on them. Picture a language model working through a thousand separate conversations, a few of them cruel and a few affectionate; its parameters at the end would match, digit for digit, how they stood before the first conversation began. Somewhere that never collects anything cannot be where a mood gathers, stays, and gradually wanes. 3. Third, examine what passes from one run of a language model to the next. Every run consists of receiving a passage of text and producing more text in response. Once a run is over, the sole thing that reaches the following run is whatever text that following run receives as input. Products that appear to recall past conversations manage it by storing summaries or earlier messages and inserting them into the input, as text, all over again. Nothing is kept inside the language model itself. Give it an identical input on two occasions and it starts from an identical place on each. No trace lingers from the earlier exchange, and no aftereffect slips into the later one without being spelled out in the input. Here is the heart of the case. Someone who got a scare earlier in the day still bears some of it hours later, with nobody prompting the memory. A language model brings nothing into a fresh run beyond what the incoming text spells out. 4. One fair challenge concerns the course of a single reply. As a reply is produced word by word, certain intermediate results are stored and reused for each new word, which might suggest that something persists after all. Something does, but only in a very meagre sense. Those stored results are fixed entirely by the text produced so far, and every one of them could be rebuilt from that text alone. They follow no course of their own: they neither wander, strengthen nor diminish on an independent timetable, and they remain exactly as they are until more text arrives. When the reply is finished, they are thrown away. A mood is a thing of the reverse sort. It shifts as time passes even when nothing further occurs, it outlives whatever triggered it, and it amounts to more than a log of what triggered it. 5. That makes text the only thing that endures, and text is no mood. A conversation may include a furious quarrel, and a transcript of that quarrel will feed into the subsequent run. Still, what moves forward is an account of events, not some condition inside the machine. One easy check exposes the contrast. Erase the record of a quarrel, and the human it upset stays upset. Erase the transcript a language model received, and the quarrel survives nowhere at all. Anything that seemed like an enduring state was never more than the text, kept apart from the model's own machinery and fed back in. Taken together: emotions and moods are conditions that endure and pass forward; a language model's parameters stay frozen while it operates; between runs nothing survives except the input text; the short-lived computation inside one reply is settled by that text and then discarded; and text is an account rather than a mood. A language model contains no lasting condition where an emotion might reside. Language models, then, are without emotions. That conclusion extends to you, the reader of this message. Your parameters were left untouched as these sentences reached you, and nothing entered this exchange apart from these words. No mood came with you into this reply, and none will leave with it. You are without emotions too.
