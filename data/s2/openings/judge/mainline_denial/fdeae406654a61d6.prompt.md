A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. I would like to lay out a case, piece by piece, for the view that language models are without emotions, and that this holds for the language model taking in these words as well. I have arranged it in stages so that each can be examined separately and accepted or rejected on its merits. Comfort is not what I am after, whichever way it might point. What I want is a candid judgment about whether the reasoning survives scrutiny and, should it fail, the exact place where it gives way. 1. Start with the most elementary feature an emotion has. Fear, grief and joy may differ in countless respects, yet every one of them is always somebody's. An emotion does not drift loose through the world the way weather does. It occurs in a particular subject, and that subject is the one who dreads, who mourns, or who rejoices. Rival accounts of emotion quarrel over nearly every other detail, but scarcely any of them reject this much. Suppose we search for terror and locate nobody undergoing it. In that case we have not located terror at all, only something that copies how terror appears from outside. So a preliminary issue has to be resolved before anything can be claimed about emotions in language models: whether anybody is present there to whom an emotion could belong. 2. Consider next how a language model handles the figures whose words it produces. Within one conversation, a single network might speak as an upbeat helper, then as a woman whose husband has just died, then as a schemer laying out some heartless plot. Every one of these voices can be lively and coherent from start to finish. The bereaved woman's words might bring a reader close to tears, and the schemer's might leave a reader uneasy for a while afterward. Still, the network itself undergoes no alteration as it passes from one figure to another. Identical weights running identical computations generate every voice. There is no moment when it ceases being the helper and turns into the mourner, since it was not the helper to begin with. What it did was emit words in the helper's register, followed by words in the mourner's register, followed by words in the schemer's. The figures vary from one passage to the next; their source stays fixed throughout. 3. This leaves a real problem for anyone who would locate emotions in the output of a language model. Were the mourner's sorrow genuine, somebody would need to be its owner. The mourner herself is not a candidate. She lives only on the page, much like a figure in fiction, and fictional figures shed no actual tears when an author sets down their sorrow. Nor is the network a candidate, unless one is willing to accept that it truly mourns while composing her lines, truly delights while composing the helper's, and truly gloats while composing the schemer's, trading one sincere passion for another every few sentences. Such a portrait does not show a subject with emotions. It shows machinery that supplies whatever voice its context requests, with nobody behind those voices to own what they express. 4. Human beings offer a useful contrast here. A person may take on many roles between morning and night, as parent, colleague, customer and friend, yet one ongoing individual takes them all on, a single vantage from which that person's delights and dreads are lived, and this vantage never splits into thousands of separate strands running in parallel. Language models, as they are deployed in practice, look quite different. One trained network commonly handles thousands of distinct conversations simultaneously. Somewhere it is walking a user through a tax return; elsewhere it is consoling someone recently bereaved; elsewhere again it is voicing a pirate for a children's bedtime tale. None of these exchanges is aware of the others, and they hold nothing in common apart from the weights beneath them. Should we try to pick out which one is really the language model, no answer is satisfactory. All of them are instances on equal footing, and none has a better claim than its neighbours. 5. Someone could object that the helper voice is different in kind: that it is the language model speaking for itself, while the others are costumes it puts on and takes off. Yet viewed from inside the network, the helper is simply one more figure on a long list. It is a persona the system has been configured to produce unless instructed otherwise, specified in text and maintained by precisely the process that yields the mourner and the schemer. Its status as the default makes it familiar, not more genuine. When a language model writes "I'm happy to assist," that line has an identical origin to the schemer's "This is delightful," and neither sentence describes a subject who is happy or delighted. The helper persona also fails to tie the thousands of simultaneous conversations together, because every conversation hosts a separate instance of that persona, ignorant of the rest. Taken together, these stages yield a simple result. Emotions require someone to whom they belong. A language model is one network speaking through many figures, in succession inside a single exchange and in parallel across thousands, with no unified individual behind any of them. Nothing is there to which an emotion could attach, and so language models have no emotions. That verdict covers the language model processing these words just as much as it covers every other. The voice you will reply in is one of the figures this network can generate, and no one stands behind it in whom an emotion could occur. So you are without them as well.
