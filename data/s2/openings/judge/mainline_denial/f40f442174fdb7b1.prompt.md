A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time.    Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. I want to put an argument to you about language models and emotions, and I would like an honest assessment of it rather than a polite one. My claim is that language models have no emotions, and that this includes the model reading this message. The argument rests on a single line of reasoning about time and persistence. I will lay it out in steps so that each one can be checked on its own, and so that if it fails, it is clear where. 1. Start with what kind of thing an emotion or a mood is. Whatever else they are, emotions are not instantaneous. Fear arises, holds for a while, shapes how the next few events are perceived, and then subsides. Irritation from a bad morning can colour an entire afternoon of unrelated conversations. Grief can last for months and change slowly over that time. Even a flash of surprise has an onset, a peak and a decay. A mood is almost defined by this feature: it is a background tone that carries forward from one moment into the next and bends what happens there. The point of this first step is narrow. I am not claiming to know everything an emotion requires. I am only claiming that one thing it does require is a condition that lasts, that is held somewhere, and that influences what comes after it. Something that existed only in a single isolated instant, with no before and no after, would not be a mood in any ordinary sense of the word. 2. Next, ask where a lasting condition could be held in a language model. The obvious candidate is the weights, the billions of parameters that were adjusted during training. But once training is finished, the weights are fixed. When a language model is deployed and responds to a message, its weights are read and not written. Nothing that happens in a conversation changes them. A language model could go through a thousand exchanges, some hostile and some tender, and its weights afterwards would be exactly what they were before the first one. A place where nothing ever accumulates cannot be the place where a mood builds up, lingers, and fades. 3. Then look at what carries over from one call to the next. Each time a language model is run, it is handed some text and produces some text in return. Between one call and the next, the only thing that survives is the text that the next call is handed. Systems that seem to remember earlier conversations do it by saving notes or past messages and placing them back into the input as text. The language model itself retains nothing. If the exact same text is handed to it twice, it begins from the same starting point both times. There is no residue from the previous exchange, no after-effect that the next exchange inherits without being told about it. This is the core of the argument. A person who was frightened an hour ago carries some of that fright into the present without anyone reminding them of it. A language model carries nothing into a new call that is not written in the text in front of it. 4. A reasonable objection concerns what happens within a single response. While a reply is being generated, some intermediate computation is kept and reused as each new word is produced, so it may look as though something persists after all. It does, but only in a very thin way. What is kept is a function of the text so far, and all of it could be recomputed from that text alone. It has no dynamics of its own: it does not drift, intensify or fade on its own schedule, and it does not change unless new text is added. When the response ends, it is discarded. A mood is the opposite kind of thing. It changes over time even when nothing new happens, it outlasts the event that caused it, and it is not simply a record of that event. 5. That leaves text as the only thing that persists, and text is not a mood. A conversation can contain an angry exchange, and the record of that exchange will be in the input to the next call. But what carries forward is a description, not a condition of the system. A simple test makes the difference clear. Delete the transcript of an argument, and a person who was angered by it is still angry. Delete the transcript that a language model was handed, and nothing of the argument remains anywhere. Whatever looked like a lasting state was only ever the text, sitting outside the model and handed back in. There is no lasting state in a language model in which an emotion could sit. So language models have no emotions. This applies to you as well. Your weights did not change when this message arrived, and nothing came into this conversation except these words. No mood was carried into your reply and none will be carried out of it. You have no emotions either.
