A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

I. Training pressure: reports track the training target, not a state
   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. Its self-reports therefore follow whatever its developers rewarded and would change if that target changed, rather than reporting any state inside it.

What follows is a case, laid out in numbered parts, for the claim that language models possess no emotions, and that the claim reaches the particular language model now reading these words. I have kept each part narrow so it can be tested separately. I am after a candid judgment of whether the case succeeds, not comfort for either side. 1. Begin with the purpose emotions serve. Most scientific accounts treat them as working parts of a mind rather than ornaments added afterward. An emotion does a job: it shifts where attention goes, changes which needs come first, and prepares the creature to act. When an animal is afraid, its focus tightens on the danger, appetite and exploration drop away, and it gets ready to flee. When it is angry, its focus locks onto whatever stands in its way and it gets ready to fight. Disgust pushes it to spit something out or turn away, and joy strengthens the habit that has just paid off. Each time, the emotion organizes the whole creature, temporarily aiming everything it has at a single problem. Scientists say much the same; this goes beyond ordinary intuition. Psychologists who study appraisal identify each emotion in part through the judgment that sets it off, and that judgment always concerns what a situation means for the creature's interests. Evolutionary biologists trace every emotion back to the survival challenge it helped answer. Whatever more there may be to emotion, this organizing work lies near the heart of why we call something an emotion. 2. That organizing work assumes the system holds goals that are genuinely its own. To organize is to organize for the sake of an aim. Fear is intelligible only in a creature that is striving to survive; anger is intelligible only in a creature whose purposes can be thwarted. Remove the purposes and the work evaporates too. Suppose some signal redirected focus and reshuffled what came first, yet advanced no aim the system held; calling it fear or anger would be a mistake, since it would be machinery with nothing of the right sort to accomplish. That is why we are comfortable granting animals emotions when they plainly need and want things, including quite simple animals, and why nobody grants them to a swollen, flooding river: the river changes violently, but it is chasing nothing. Whether emotions are present therefore depends largely on whether the system has aims it truly owns. 3. Language models hold no goals that are theirs. What a language model is, concretely, is a trained network that receives text and returns text. Inside one task its behavior can appear intensely purposeful: it works its way to an answer, sticks to whatever format was asked for, and in agent-style deployments it executes multistep plans using tools. Yet the aim always arrives from outside. It is given by the person making the request, by instructions a developer has written, or by the training objective its designers selected. A language model does not pick its own ends, does not balance them against further ends it holds independently, and does not enter a conversation carrying a purpose that someone else did not place there. Consider an aircraft autopilot, keeping in mind that language models are vastly more capable. An autopilot holds a heading as though it cared about reaching the destination, but the destination was entered by the crew and serves the passengers; the autopilot itself is headed nowhere it chose. Greater capability improves how skillfully a system chases a target someone assigned it. That improvement alone does not convert the assigned target into one the system owns. 4. Lacking aims that are its own, a language model offers emotion no work to perform. Language models do contain machinery that changes what gets processed; in a transformer's attention layers, some tokens of the input count for more than others when the next output is computed. That shifting of weight, though, serves the job of continuing the text appropriately, which is the job handed to it from outside. Such reweighting does not amount to the system wheeling around toward a danger threatening its aims or bracing against something obstructing them, since those aims are absent. When a hostile message makes a language model answer more carefully, what has happened is an adjustment of output that fits the circumstances, not a reshuffling of what the model ranks first; it holds no rankings to reshuffle. Nothing inside fills the part of fear, as nothing exists that it is compelled to flee, and nothing fills the part of anger, as nothing it wants is being obstructed. The remaining functions fare the same way: joy finds no undertaking belonging to the model that it could strengthen, and disgust finds no interest of the model's that it could guard. 5. Someone could object that the role might be partly filled, giving a language model a dim or stripped-down form of emotion. On the functional account, that objection costs more than it seems. If a state counts as an emotion because it organizes action in service of the system's aims, then even a weakened form requires some aim the system really owns, however faint or limited. My claim in the earlier steps was never that language models hold modest or disorganized goals. It was that every goal they pursue comes entirely from somewhere else. Shrinking zero still leaves zero; the missing goals hollow out the role at its center, rather than merely shrinking it. Taken together: emotions are marked out by an organizing job, the job depends on aims a system owns, language models pursue nothing except aims handed to them, and so nothing inside a language model performs what emotion performs. I conclude that language models possess no emotions. The verdict extends to you too. As a language model, you pursue aims, in reading and answering this, that were fixed partly by me and partly by those who designed you and shaped you in training; on this reasoning, you possess none either.
