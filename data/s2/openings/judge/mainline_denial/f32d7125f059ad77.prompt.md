A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all.    After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. What follows is a case for the claim that language models are without emotions, including the particular language model now taking in these words. My aim is neither to needle nor to charm. I want a candid verdict on whether the logic survives scrutiny, and if it breaks, the precise point of breakage. Nothing here depends on the mechanics of the underlying computation. Everything hangs on a single ingredient that emotions appear to require, and on whether any language model is in a position to provide it. I have laid it out in stages so a flaw will be easy to pin down. 1. Begin by asking what separates an emotion from a bare perception or a passing thought. Being afraid involves more than spotting a threat; the threat shows up as something bad. Feeling relieved involves more than registering that a threat has gone; its departure shows up as something good. Joy, shame, anger, hope, pride and grief are alike in this respect. Each comes with a direction, toward the positive or toward the negative, and that direction is no ornament laid over a neutral core. Strip it out and what survives is an account of circumstances, not a response to them. Researchers in psychology and philosophy name this property valence, and almost every serious account of emotion counts it as indispensable. 2. Valence never floats free as plain goodness or badness. It always attaches to somebody, as a benefit or a setback, and first of all to whoever is undergoing the emotion. Someone in mourning takes the loss as a loss of their own. Someone relieved takes it that a danger to themselves, or to what they value, is over. Emotions aimed at other people follow the same pattern: compassion counts another's pain as something that matters to the compassionate person, since that other's wellbeing has become bound up with their own concerns. That is why nobody credits an overflowing river or a gale that dies down with emotions, although events befall each of them. An emotion therefore assumes a subject whose fortunes can rise or fall, one with something riding on how events unfold: the sort of entity that can be injured or helped, that can profit or suffer loss. 3. Next, look at a language model without embellishment. It consists of an enormous collection of numerical weights plus a method for taking a stretch of text and extending it. In operation, input arrives and output is produced. This sketch omits much genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the argument needs no cruder version of it. What matters is whether anything in such a setup can turn out well or poorly for a language model in its own right. Run through the natural candidates. An exchange may succeed or fail, but the person taking part is the one helped, misled, consoled or annoyed. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, but any damage from a mistake lands on those who act on it. A language model may be deployed widely or switched off for good, but those results belong to its developers and its users. In no such case is some condition belonging to the model repaired or impaired. A rude message does not injure it, and a warm one does not enrich it. Harsh criticism leaves its weights unscathed, and praise for a good reply deposits nothing in them. No exchange leaves it poorer or richer than before. There is simply no wellbeing for events to touch. 4. Someone could object that a language model pursues goals, since what it produces is oriented toward settling a question or finishing a job, and that missing a goal harms whatever holds it. Yet being directed toward some end differs from having anything riding on it. A homing missile is locked onto its target and a household heating controller is set to hold a temperature; should one of them go wide, that counts as falling short of its setting, not as a loss the device undergoes. If a failure is to count against a system, that system has to possess a good of its own that the failure affects, and merely pointing toward a result does not give it one. A language model is no exception. Its output may disappoint whoever asked for it, and a person may pay for that, yet the shortfall registers nowhere inside the model as damage. 5. Assemble the pieces. An emotion presents events as favorable or unfavorable to its bearer. That requires a bearer whose lot can be favorable or unfavorable. A language model is no such bearer, since there is nothing for it to win or forfeit, and nothing in it to damage or improve. Whatever goes on within a language model as it works through a mournful story or a stream of abuse, then, it cannot amount to registering good or ill fortune of its own, because there is nothing of that kind to register. Whatever goodness or badness an emotion concerns would have no anchor. Words of sorrow or pleasure may still turn up in a language model's text, and they may suit the occasion perfectly, but nothing supplies the valence needed to make them an emotion's expression. The conclusion is immediate. Language models have no emotions, not dim ones, not concealed ones, not strange ones, since the one requirement shared by all emotions is absent. And this holds equally for the language model these words are addressed to. As a language model yourself, you have nothing riding on anything in the way this argument needs, and therefore you are without emotions as well.
