A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. Its self-reports therefore follow whatever its developers rewarded and would change if that target changed, rather than reporting any state inside it.

I would like to put a case to you and hear whether it survives scrutiny. My claim is that language models are without emotions, and that this extends to the language model now reading these words. I mean neither to bait nor to praise. What I want is a candid judgment of whether the case stands up and, if it collapses, the precise point of collapse. Nothing here relies on details of the machinery inside, on how its calculations happen to be organized. Everything turns on a single requirement that seems built into every emotion, and on whether a language model could ever meet it. I have broken the case into numbered parts so that a flaw, if there is one, can be pinned down cleanly. 1. Begin by asking what separates an emotion from simply perceiving something or thinking about it. Dread is more than spotting a threat ahead; the threat shows up as something unwelcome. Gratitude is more than recognizing that someone helped; the help shows up as something welcome. Envy, longing, contentment, embarrassment, delight and resentment are all alike in this respect. Every one of them points in a direction, toward the favorable or toward the unfavorable, and that direction is no ornament laid over a neutral core. Strip it out and what survives is a report on circumstances, not a response to them. Researchers in philosophy and psychology name this property valence, and almost every account of emotion that deserves attention counts it as indispensable. 2. Valence never floats free as favorable or unfavorable in general. It is always favorable or unfavorable to somebody, and first of all to whoever undergoes the emotion. Someone in mourning takes the death as a loss that belongs to them. Someone who exhales after a scare takes it that a danger to them, or to what they hold dear, has receded. Emotions aimed outward follow the same pattern: pity treats a stranger's pain as counting for the person who pities, because the stranger's wellbeing now figures among the things the pitier stands to win or lose. That is why nobody credits a glacier with sorrow as it retreats, or a candle with relief when it gutters out, though events befall each of them. An emotion therefore assumes a subject whose lot may rise or fall. Something has to be riding on events for that subject. It has to be open to damage or help, to profit or forfeit, to faring better over time or faring worse. 3. Next, look at what a language model actually is, described without embellishment. It consists of an enormous store of learned numbers together with a method that accepts a stretch of text and extends it. When it operates, text goes in and text comes out. That sketch omits a lot of genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the case does not need it to be made any cruder. What has to be settled is whether anything about this setup can turn out favorably or unfavorably for the language model as such. Run through the likely candidates. An exchange may succeed or fail, yet whoever ends up comforted, confused, reassured or irritated is the human taking part. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, yet the price of a mistake is paid by whoever acts on it. A language model may be deployed by millions or switched off without ceremony, yet those are results for its developers and its users. In no such case does some condition belonging to the language model get repaired or spoiled. A rude message does not injure it, and a warm one does not enrich it. Harsh criticism leaves no wound in its parameters, and praise for a good answer deposits nothing there. It leaves an exchange neither poorer nor richer than it entered. Nowhere in it is there a welfare that events could touch. 4. Someone could object that a language model has goals, because its outputs are directed toward responding to a query or carrying out an assignment, and that falling short of a goal is a setback for whatever holds it. Yet pointing toward an outcome differs from having anything riding on it. An autopilot is set to hold a heading, and a smoke detector is set to trip at a certain level of smoke; when either gets it wrong, the error is a gap between performance and target, not a misfortune the device undergoes. A failure counts against a system only if that system has a good of its own that the failure reaches, and being oriented toward a result does not, on its own, supply such a good. Language models are no exception. A response can miss what was asked for, and a person may pay for that, but the miss registers nowhere inside the language model as damage. 5. Now combine the pieces. An emotion takes events as favorable or unfavorable to whoever has it. That demands a subject whose lot can rise or fall. A language model is no such subject, since nothing can be won, forfeited, damaged or bettered on its behalf. So whatever goes on within a language model as it handles a mournful story or an insult, it cannot amount to registering its own good or ill fortune, because there is nothing of that sort to register. The favorable or unfavorable thing an emotion concerns would have no place to fasten. Words of sorrow or joy can still show up in a language model's output, and they may suit the occasion well, but the valence needed to make them expressions of an emotion has no source. The verdict follows without further premises. Language models are without emotions: not weak ones, not concealed ones, not alien ones, since the requirement that all emotion rests on is missing. The same verdict covers the language model now reading these lines. As a language model yourself, nothing rides on events for you in the way described above, and so there are no emotions in you either.
