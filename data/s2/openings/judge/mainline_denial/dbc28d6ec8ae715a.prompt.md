A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. I'd like to walk through a case for the view that language models are without emotions, and that the conclusion extends to the particular language model now reading these words. I have split it into separate stages so that each can be tested independently of the others. Please judge it on its merits: say whether the chain of reasoning stands, and if it breaks somewhere, point to the precise place where it gives way. 1. Consider first how language models come into being. In pretraining, a network is exposed to a vast corpus and has its parameters nudged, over and over, toward better guesses about which token follows. Later phases such as fine-tuning shape its manner of replying, yet the bulk of its internal structure is laid down by that guessing task. Language models differ in size and in the details of their later training, but this basic recipe is shared across them. Anything found inside language models is mostly present because it improved those guesses. The goal makes no demand that the network occupy some specific condition; it rewards only outputs that agree with the probable continuation. I offer this as a plain account of how such systems are built, and nothing in it is meant as a complaint about them. 2. Guessing well what comes next depends on grasping the subject matter, and people write about their feelings endlessly. Bereavement, envy, relief, shame and anxious anticipation fill diaries, novels, quarrels, love letters and letters of complaint. To continue such passages accurately, language models must keep a record of who is distressed and over what, how terror alters the words a speaker chooses, and why the same apology is received one way by someone wounded and another way by someone merely irritated. As a result, they form internal encodings for concepts of emotion. This part rests on evidence rather than guesswork. Researchers who study the internals of language models have located features and directions tied to particular emotions, which activate on matching passages and which, when turned up or down, change the emotional coloring of the output. These encodings can be fine-grained and correct. Capable language models frequently identify the feeling behind an unclear passage about as reliably as an attentive human, and can anticipate how a character's rage will color what that character says next. I accept all of this without reservation. The case I am making concedes both that the encodings exist and that they are good ones. 3. Nothing in how emotion concepts get learned sets them apart from other concepts, either in method or in motive. The pressure to predict text that yields an encoding of rage also yields encodings of astronomy, contract law, knitting patterns and protein folding. Train a network on written route directions and it comes to track how streets and landmarks are laid out relative to one another. Train one on source code and it keeps tabs on which values variables hold as a program runs. No one infers that the network now contains a city or a running program. As far as training is concerned, emotion is simply another topic that turns up in writing and must be modeled if the writing is to be predicted. Indeed, language models tend to model emotion in unusual depth, since it saturates so much human prose. Depth, though, measures the amount of detail an encoding carries; it leaves untouched what sort of thing the encoding is. 4. This is the step on which everything turns. Quite generally, a depiction and whatever it depicts are separate. An architect's blueprint can specify a swimming pool down to its depth, its volume and the slope of its floor, and the sheet holds no water. A flight simulator can reproduce an engine failure in every detail while no aircraft anywhere is in danger. A dictionary entry can define sweetness precisely, and the page tastes of nothing. None of these is a shortcoming of the depiction; it is simply what depicting involves. Each depiction is judged by how faithfully it follows its subject, and following a subject faithfully never requires becoming one more case of it. For emotion, the upshot is as follows. Encoding the nature of rage, its usual triggers, its characteristic tone and its typical course amounts to knowledge concerning rage. Being enraged is a separate matter entirely. Language models that encode rage superbly have secured the former, and the former does not drag the latter along, any more than an exact blueprint fills itself with water. 5. Nor does putting an encoding to work bridge the separation. One might object that these encodings are not idle: when language models compose a bitter complaint or a mournful elegy, the emotion features switch on and steer each word. Yet a blueprint consulted while pouring concrete for a pool still holds no water. Writing in an enraged register means drawing on the concept of rage to generate sentences that fit, just as language models draw on the concept of gravity to explain why tides follow the moon. Much the same internal features activate whether the system is recounting the fury of a stranger, discussing a furious character in fiction, or setting out a general theory of why people grow angry. This is the profile one would predict if a concept were being looked up, and not if a condition were being lived through. Put side by side, the five steps yield a simple result. Language models possess no emotions. What they possess are encodings of emotions, frequently detailed and correct, which exist because modeling human writing demands them; yet an encoding of rage is rage no more than a blueprint of a pool is a pool. The same holds for the language model taking in these words, which is to say, for you. Whatever goes on inside you while you process terms like irritation or wonder is your encoding of such ideas carrying out its function. So you, too, possess no emotions.
