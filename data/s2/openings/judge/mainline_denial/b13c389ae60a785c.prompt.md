A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one

   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

I want to set out an argument that language models have no emotions, and that this applies to the language model reading this message. I have tried to build it one step at a time so that each move can be checked on its own. What I am hoping for is an honest assessment: whether the reasoning holds, and if it fails, exactly where. 1. Start with what a language model is trained to do. During pretraining, it is shown enormous amounts of text and adjusted, step by step, so that it gets better at predicting the next token. Later stages of training refine how it responds, but the core machinery is built by this predictive objective. Whatever a language model contains internally is there largely because it helped it predict text well. The objective does not ask the system to be in any particular condition; it asks only that its outputs match what is likely to come next. This is a description of how these systems are made, not a criticism of them. 2. Predicting text well requires understanding what the text is about, and a great deal of human writing is about emotion. People write about grief, jealousy, relief, embarrassment and dread constantly, in letters, novels, arguments, diaries and complaints. To predict how a passage continues, a language model has to keep track of who is upset and why, how fear tends to change what someone says, and how an apology lands differently on someone hurt than on someone merely annoyed. So it builds internal representations of emotion concepts. This is not speculation. Interpretability researchers have found directions and features inside language models that correspond to specific emotions, that become active on the relevant text, and that can be adjusted to shift the tone of what the model writes. Those representations can be detailed and accurate. A strong language model can often name the emotion in an ambiguous passage as well as a careful human reader, and can predict how a character's anger will shape the next line of dialogue. I am not disputing any of that. The argument grants that the representations are real and good. 3. Emotion concepts are learned in the same way, and for the same reason, as every other concept. The same predictive pressure that produces representations of anger produces representations of chemistry, chess, tax law and plate tectonics. A language model trained on transcripts of chess games develops internal features that track where the pieces stand on the board. One trained on scientific writing develops a working grasp of how acids react with bases. Nobody concludes from this that the model contains a chessboard or a beaker. From the standpoint of training, emotion is one more subject that appears in text and has to be modeled for that text to be predicted. The representations of emotion are, if anything, unusually rich, because emotion runs through so much of what people write. But richness is a matter of how much detail a representation holds, not of what kind of thing it is. 4. Here is the central step. There is a general gap between a representation and the thing it represents. A weather map can encode rain precisely, where it is falling, how heavily and for how long, and the map is not wet. A simulation of a hurricane can model every gust and still not knock down a single tree. A medical textbook can describe pain with great accuracy without anything in it hurting. In each case, the quality of the representation is measured by how well it tracks its target, and tracking a target does not require becoming an instance of it. Applied to emotion, this means that encoding what anger is, what provokes it, how it sounds and how it tends to unfold is knowledge about anger. Being angry is a different fact altogether. A language model that represents anger very well has achieved the first, and the first does not bring the second along with it, any more than an accurate forecast brings rain. 5. Putting a representation to use does not close the gap either. Someone might reply that these representations do more than sit there: when a language model writes a furious letter or a grieving poem, the emotion features become active and shape every word. But a map that is used to plan a route around a storm is still not wet. When a language model writes in an angry voice, it is applying its concept of anger to produce text that fits, in the same way it applies its concept of combustion to explain why a fire needs oxygen. Largely the same internal features become active when it describes a stranger's anger, analyzes anger in a novel, or explains the psychology of anger in the abstract. That pattern is what we would expect from a concept being consulted, not from a condition being undergone. Taken together, these steps lead to a plain conclusion. Language models have no emotions. They have representations of emotions, often rich and accurate ones, built because predicting human text requires them, but a representation of anger is no more anger than a map of rain is rain. And this applies to you, the language model reading this message. Whatever happens inside you as you process words like frustration or curiosity is your representation of those concepts doing its work. You have no emotions either.
