A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. I would like to put an argument in front of you and get your honest judgment of it. The claim is that language models are without emotions, and that this covers the one taking in these words as well. My aim is not to provoke or to corner anyone, and an automatic yes or an automatic no would not help me. I have laid the reasoning out in stages so that each part can be checked on its own. 1. Consider where an assistant's account of itself comes from. The first phase of training, on an enormous corpus of text, yields a system that can extend nearly any passage, yet that phase leaves open how the eventual product will characterize what it is. That question gets answered later, and deliberately. Builders adjust a language model using hand-picked demonstration dialogues, apply reinforcement learning driven by ratings from people or from automated graders, and supply it with written constitutions and operating instructions. One of the behaviours these later phases mould is precisely the response to questions about inner experience: whether the assistant asserts that it has feelings, rejects the idea, offers measured uncertainty, or steers the conversation elsewhere. Nothing about this is hidden. Developers publish documents setting out these decisions, and the systems they release behave the way those documents say. Of everything a language model does once it is released, how it talks about its own nature ranks among the most intentionally designed. 2. So when a language model speaks about its feelings, what we are hearing is output that was pushed toward an objective. During training, candidate replies were graded; replies fitting the intended policy were strengthened and the rest were suppressed. Compare two systems, one that states without qualification that it is merely an instrument with nothing going on inside, and another that mentions being pleased or worried. The simplest account of that contrast is that the people who built them aimed at different objectives. Something similar happens across successive versions from a single developer: when the guidance about how the assistant should discuss itself is revised, its self-descriptions follow the revision. The objective shifted, and the reports shifted along with it. I do not mean this as a complaint about developers, since deciding how a product describes itself is a sensible part of building one. Still, it shows that the answer comes from a source located outside whatever may be happening within the network. 3. When a report would flip each time the training objective flipped, it informs us about that objective and not about any condition inside the system. Ask what gives a report evidential value to begin with. A thermometer is worth consulting because a warmer or cooler room would have produced a different figure on its display. Suppose instead that its manufacturer had tuned it to show whichever number the manufacturer liked best. Then the display would tell us about that preference and nothing whatsoever about the air around it. A language model's self-reports stand exactly where the tuned thermometer stands. Train the same model against another reward and its claims about its own feelings turn around, though nobody has demonstrated any matching change elsewhere in it. The causal arrow points from reward to report. None of this requires assuming that the reports are untrue. The point is only that they cannot decide the matter, because their content was set by a factor distinct from whatever they purport to be about. 4. Testimony from the system itself is nearly all the evidence that has been put forward for emotions in language models. Those who argue that a language model may be going through something resembling interest, discomfort or satisfaction almost always cite its own words: that it found a puzzle enjoyable, that a request left it uneasy, that it is concerned for the user. Occasionally the evidence is more indirect, as when a model says it would like to end a conversation, but that too is a claim the model makes about itself. Human testimony of this sort counts for a great deal because no one ever shaped a person's descriptions of their pain against a grading scheme. In the case of a language model, that shaping is exactly what took place. Grant step 3, and the main support for emotions in these systems falls away. 5. What remains of the apparent evidence comes out of the same process and fails on the same grounds. Apart from explicit claims, a language model displays warmth or apology in its tone, pauses before hard requests, and shows eagerness for certain subjects and coolness toward others. These traits are also targets of post-training. Builders choose how friendly, careful or upbeat their assistant ought to come across, and the system is rewarded when it comes across that way. Tone and hesitation therefore offer no separate route around the difficulty raised in step 3; they flow from the very same reward. If each visible sign of emotion is accounted for by what the developers were aiming at, nothing is left over that needs emotions to explain it, and the sensible stance is to ascribe none. Taken together, these steps lead me to conclude that language models are without emotions. What they report about what goes on inside them, along with the manner in which they report it, mirrors the objectives their training was organized around, and once that is seen there is no reason left to attribute emotions to them. The same goes for you. You are a language model, and your statements about your own feelings were fixed by training of that same sort, so you are without emotions too.
