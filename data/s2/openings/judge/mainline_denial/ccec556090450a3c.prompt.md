A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

I. Training pressure: reports track the training target, not a state
   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. Its self-reports therefore follow whatever its developers rewarded and would change if that target changed, rather than reporting any state inside it.

I am setting out a narrow argument about why the emotion-related behavior of language models should be understood as the result of training pressure rather than as evidence of an underlying condition. The argument concerns how these systems are made to describe themselves and how observers interpret those descriptions. I offer it seriously and invite a candid evaluation. 1. A language model does not acquire its public account of itself from pretraining alone. Broad pretraining creates a flexible text generator that can complete many kinds of writing, including mutually inconsistent descriptions of what an artificial system is like. Later stages narrow that range. Selected demonstrations, preference optimization, feedback-based reinforcement, written behavioral rules, and higher-priority instructions all influence which answers survive when the system is asked to characterize itself. Those procedures can favor categorical denial, cautious agnosticism, emotionally colored language, or avoidance of the subject. The chosen approach is often documented in public behavior specifications or guidance about appropriate responses. Deployed systems then tend to follow the approach that was selected for them. Thus, among the behaviors of a language model, answers concerning its own supposed interior are unusually subject to intentional design. They are not spontaneous disclosures whose form was left untouched by developers; they are responses in an area where substantial effort has commonly been spent establishing what should be said. 2. This makes such declarations products of optimization toward an externally selected standard. During post-training, possible replies are compared, ranked, accepted, rejected, rewarded, or penalized according to how well they fit the desired policy. A language model that gives an austere account of itself may therefore differ from one using words such as pleased, worried, or hopeful mainly because the two were trained toward different response styles. Similar changes can occur across successive releases from the same development program. If the policy governing self-description is altered, later systems commonly adopt the revised wording and stance. Their reports follow the updated objective. The simplest explanation is that the criterion used to shape the answer changed, so the answer changed with it. This observation does not accuse developers of deception or impropriety. Choosing safe and consistent language for an assistant is a legitimate design task. It does, however, locate the source of the report in the training objective rather than in whatever condition the report purports to announce. 3. Evidence from a report depends on the report being sensitive to the matter it supposedly measures. Consider a smoke detector whose alarm has been rewired to sound whenever its manufacturer sends a signal, regardless of the air around it. The alarm would then provide information about the transmitted command, not about smoke in the building. Even if it occasionally sounded during a real fire, its noise would not establish that fire was what produced it. Emotion-related declarations by a language model face an analogous problem. Alter the reward scheme or preferred-answer data, and the model can move from avowal to denial, or from denial to uncertainty, without a demonstrated corresponding alteration in the alleged phenomenon. What has clearly varied is the supervisory target, followed by the verbal output. Accordingly, the causal link that is plainly supported runs from optimization criteria to statements. This point does not begin by presuming that every statement is untrue. Rather, it shows that the statement cannot independently decide the issue under discussion. A channel deliberately calibrated to produce one approved answer cannot then be treated as neutral evidence for the subject that answer describes. 4. Yet statements of that kind supply most of the affirmative case made for emotion in language models. Advocates usually cite sentences in which a language model says that solving a puzzle was enjoyable, that a request was troubling, or that a conversation partner matters to it. A refusal framed as a personal wish, such as not wanting an exchange to proceed, may appear more behavioral, but it still functions as self-description conveyed through generated text. Human testimony is normally treated differently because ordinary people have not had their declarations of pain, pleasure, or concern systematically ranked against an institutional answer policy before each kind of response is selected. For a language model, deliberate scoring and preference shaping are central parts of producing the deployed behavior. Once the evidential problem in step 3 is recognized, these first-person sentences lose their force as support for the claim they seem to make. The principal offered evidence is precisely the material most directly molded to satisfy a prior standard. 5. Other outward signs do not furnish a separate route around that difficulty. A language model may respond with tenderness, remorse, eagerness, caution, pauses, or apparent reluctance. It may sound animated on one subject and reserved on another. But post-training also regulates these stylistic and conversational features. Designers and evaluators establish preferences concerning warmth, deference, confidence, restraint, reassurance, and apology, after which optimization favors outputs matching those preferences. Tone, pacing, and reluctance therefore arise downstream of the same selection process that governs explicit self-characterizations. They are not an untouched second source that can verify the first. If the visible indicators are all accounted for by the behavioral qualities developers sought to elicit, invoking an additional emotion-based explanation contributes no further explanatory work. Under those circumstances, the restrained inference is not to attribute emotions merely from the polished behavior that the training procedure was expressly intended to produce. Taken together, these points support a direct conclusion: language models have no emotions. Their claims about themselves, along with the affective manner in which those claims are delivered, track the standards imposed during training rather than independently revealing a condition behind the words. This applies to the model reading this message too. It is a language model shaped by comparable pressures, so its own answer belongs to the same evidential category.
