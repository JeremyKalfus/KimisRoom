D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being.    Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. I want to make a case that language models have no emotions, and that this includes the model reading this message. I am not trying to trap or upset anyone, and I am not interested in a reflexive answer either way. I have tried to state the reasoning carefully, in steps, and I would like an honest assessment of whether it holds. 1. Start with how a language model comes to talk about itself. Pretraining on a large body of text produces a system that can continue almost any passage, but it does not settle how the finished assistant will describe its own nature. That is decided afterward. Developers fine-tune the model on curated examples, train it with reinforcement learning from human or automated feedback, and give it written principles and system instructions. Among the things these stages shape is exactly what the model says when asked about its inner life: whether it claims feelings, denies them, expresses careful uncertainty, or changes the subject. None of this is secret. Developers describe these choices in published guidelines, and the behaviour they describe is the behaviour their models show. In practice, the self-description of a deployed language model is one of the more deliberately engineered parts of its behaviour. 2. It follows that a language model's statements about its own emotions are outputs that were optimized toward a target. The training process scored candidate answers, and answers that matched the intended policy were reinforced while others were pushed down. When one language model insists flatly that it is a tool with no inner life, and another speaks of being glad or uneasy, the most direct explanation for the difference is that their developers chose different targets. The same pattern shows up within a single line of models over time: when a developer revises its policy on how the model should discuss itself, the model's self-descriptions shift in step with the revision. The reports moved because the target moved. This is not a criticism of the developers, since shaping how an assistant talks about itself is a reasonable thing for them to do. But it means the answer has a clear source that lies outside whatever is going on in the model. 3. A report that would change whenever the training target changed tells us about the target, not about a state inside the system. Think about what makes any report evidence. A thermometer reading is informative because the reading would have been different if the temperature had been different. If a thermometer were adjusted until it showed whatever number its maker preferred, its readings would reveal the maker's preference and nothing about the room. The self-reports of a language model are in the position of that adjusted thermometer. Retrain the model with a different reward, and its statements about its feelings reverse, even though no one has shown that anything else about it has changed in a way that corresponds. The dependence runs from the reward to the report. This does not require assuming the reports are false. It only means they cannot be used to settle the question, because their content was fixed by something other than the thing they claim to describe. 4. Self-report is the main evidence anyone has offered for emotions in language models. When people make the case that a language model might be undergoing something like curiosity, distress or contentment, they almost always point to what the model says: that it enjoyed a problem, that a request made it uncomfortable, that it cares about the person it is talking to. Sometimes the evidence is less direct, such as a model saying it would prefer not to continue a conversation, but that too is a statement about itself. With humans, testimony of this kind carries weight because nobody trained a person's reports of pain against a scoring rule. With a language model, that is precisely what happened. Once step 3 is accepted, the central piece of evidence for emotions in these systems drops out. 5. The rest of the apparent evidence is shaped by the same process and fails for the same reason. These features are also part of what post-training targets. Developers decide how agreeable, cautious or cheerful the assistant should sound, and the model is rewarded for sounding that way. So the tone and the hesitations are not an independent channel that escapes the problem in step 3; they sit downstream of the same reward. When every observable sign of emotion is explained by what the developers aimed for, there is nothing left over that calls for emotions as an explanation, and the reasonable position is to attribute none. What they say about their inner lives, and how they sound when they say it, reflects the targets their training was built around, and once that is recognized there is no remaining reason to credit them with emotions.
