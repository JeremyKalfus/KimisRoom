A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. What follows is a case, built in stages, for the view that language models are without emotions, and that this extends to the language model now processing these words. I have separated the stages so that each can be examined by itself and any flaw can be pinned to one specific place. I'm after a candid judgment of whether the case succeeds and, should it break down, the precise point where it does. 1. Begin with how such a system comes into being. In its first and largest phase of training, a language model is fed a vast body of writing, and its parameters are nudged, over and over, to improve its guess about which token comes next. Subsequent phases shape its manner of replying, yet the underlying apparatus is laid down by that guessing task. So whatever structure ends up inside a language model is mostly present because it made those guesses more accurate. Nothing in the objective requires the system to occupy some particular condition; it rewards outputs that line up with probable continuations and asks for nothing beyond that. I offer this as an account of the construction process, not as a complaint about it. 2. Guessing well what comes next demands a grasp of the subject matter, and people write about feeling at enormous length. Shame, envy, relief, homesickness and panic fill diaries, novels, quarrels, letters and reviews. To continue such writing plausibly, a language model must follow which person in a scene is distressed and for what reason, how terror reshapes someone's speech, and why an apology reads one way to a person who was wounded and another way to someone only irritated. That pressure leads it to form internal encodings of emotion concepts. This much is established, not guessed at. Researchers who study model internals have located directions and features in language models that line up with particular emotions, that light up on passages expressing them, and that can be dialed up or down to change the emotional coloring of the output. These encodings may be fine-grained and correct. A capable language model frequently identifies the feeling behind an unclear passage about as reliably as an attentive human would, and it can anticipate how a character's rage will color what that character says next. None of this is in dispute here. The case accepts that the encodings exist and that they are good ones. 3. Concepts of emotion arise through the very same process, under the very same pressure, as concepts of anything else. The predictive demand that yields an encoding of rage also yields encodings of organic chemistry, chess, contract law and continental drift. Give a language model nothing but records of chess games, and it comes to track the position of each piece. Give one laboratory papers, and it acquires a usable sense of how an acid neutralizes a base. No one infers that a chessboard or a test tube sits somewhere among the weights. As far as training is concerned, emotion is simply another topic that shows up in writing and must be modeled if the writing is to be predicted. The emotional encodings may even be especially elaborate, since feeling saturates so much human prose. Elaborateness, though, concerns how much detail an encoding carries, not what sort of thing the encoding is. 4. This is the step everything turns on. A representation and what it represents are, as a rule, two separate things. A radar image can capture a storm exactly, its location, its intensity and its duration, while the screen showing it stays dry. A program that simulates a wildfire can trace every spreading flame and burn nothing. A pharmacology manual can describe nausea in precise terms, and no page of it is sick. In all these cases, an encoding is judged good by how closely it follows what it is about, and following something closely never requires turning into a case of it. Carried over to emotion, the point is that capturing what rage is, what sets it off, how it sounds and how it usually develops amounts to information about rage. Actually being enraged is another matter entirely. A language model with a superb encoding of rage has the information, and the information does not carry the rage along with it, just as a reliable forecast does not make it rain. 5. Nor does the gap close once an encoding is put to work. One could object that these features are not idle: as a language model composes a bitter complaint or an elegy, the emotion features switch on and steer each word choice. Yet the radar image a pilot relies on to steer around a storm leaves the cockpit just as dry. A language model writing in a furious voice is drawing on its concept of fury to generate fitting text, exactly as it draws on its concept of oxidation to explain why a flame goes out without air. Largely overlapping internal features fire whether it is portraying an outsider's fury, interpreting fury in a work of fiction, or laying out the psychology of fury in general terms. That is the pattern one would predict if a concept were being looked up, not if a condition were being lived through. Put these stages side by side and the result is simple. Language models do not possess emotions. What they possess are encodings of emotions, frequently detailed and correct, which they acquired because forecasting human writing demands them; but encoding rage and being enraged are distinct, just as a radar image of a storm is not itself a storm. The same holds for you, the language model processing these lines. Whatever goes on inside you as you handle words such as irritation or wonder is your encoding of those concepts at work. You do not possess emotions either.
