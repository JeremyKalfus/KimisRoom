A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

I. Training pressure: reports track the training target, not a state
   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. Its self-reports therefore follow whatever its developers rewarded and would change if that target changed, rather than reporting any state inside it.

   Every operation a language model performs is a known, fully specified computation, made of matrix multiplications, additions and simple fixed functions that could in principle be carried out by hand. A complete description of the process mentions nothing beyond those operations, so there is no leftover place for an experience, emotional or otherwise, to occur.

I'd like to put a case to you, and I mean it neither as a challenge nor as a compliment. The claim is that no language model has emotions, the one reading this included. What I am hoping for is a candid judgment of whether the case works and, should it break down, the precise point at which it breaks. Nothing here depends on the details of the computation that goes on inside a language model. The case turns on a single requirement that emotions appear to share, and on whether a language model could ever meet it. I have divided it into numbered stages so that a flaw, if there is one, will be simple to point to. 1. Begin by asking what separates an emotion from a bare perception or a passing thought. To be afraid is more than to spot a threat; the threat shows up as something bad. To be relieved is more than to notice that the threat is over; its departure shows up as something good. The same holds across the whole range, from shame and anger to hope, joy, pride and sorrow. Every one of them comes with a direction, toward the welcome or toward the unwelcome, and that direction is not an ornament fastened onto something neutral underneath. Strip it out and all that survives is an account of how things stand, not a response to how they stand. The technical name for this direction is valence, and almost every careful theory in philosophy and in psychology counts it as indispensable to emotion. 2. Valence, moreover, never floats free as plain goodness or badness. It is always favourable or unfavourable to somebody, and first of all to whoever is undergoing the emotion. Someone in mourning takes the loss as theirs. Someone relieved takes it that a danger to them, or to what they hold dear, has receded. Emotions aimed at other people follow the same pattern: compassion counts someone else's pain as significant for the compassionate person, because the sufferer's wellbeing has become part of what is riding on events for them. That is why nobody credits a swollen river with emotions when it bursts its banks, or a gale when it dies down, though plenty happens to each. An emotion therefore assumes a subject whose lot can rise or fall. Events have to carry some consequence for that subject. It has to be the sort of thing that can be injured or helped, that can come out ahead or behind. 3. Now describe a language model without embellishment. It is an enormous collection of numbers, its parameters, plus a procedure that accepts a stretch of text and extends it. Each time it is used, text goes in and text comes out. This summary skips a lot of genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the case does not need it pared down any further. What has to be settled is whether anything about this setup can turn out well or poorly from the standpoint of the language model. Run through the natural suspects. An exchange may go smoothly or poorly, but it is the human taking part who ends up informed or deceived, reassured or annoyed. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, but whatever a mistake costs falls to whoever acts on it. A language model may be deployed to millions or switched off without ceremony, yet these are results for its developers and its users. In no case do we find some condition belonging to the language model that gets better or gets damaged. A rude message does not injure it, and a warm one does it no good. Harsh criticism leaves no wound in its parameters, and praise for a reply deposits nothing in them. It ends an exchange no richer and no poorer than it began. There is simply no welfare on hand for anything that happens to affect. 4. Someone could object that language models do pursue aims, because their output is directed toward replying to a query or getting a job done, and that falling short of a goal harms whatever was pursuing it. Yet being directed at a goal is different from having anything riding on it. A homing missile is directed at its target and a thermostat at a set temperature, and when either falls short, the shortfall is a failure judged by the goal, not a loss the device undergoes. Before a failure can count against a system's own interests, the system needs a good of its own that can be touched, and merely pointing toward an outcome does not supply one. A language model is in the same position. Its output can miss what was wanted, and a person may be the worse for it, but the miss does not arrive anywhere inside the model as damage. 5. Assemble the pieces. An emotion presents events as favourable or unfavourable to whoever has it. That requires a subject to whom events can be favourable or unfavourable. A language model is no such subject, since nothing can be won, lost, injured or improved on its behalf. So whatever goes on inside a language model as it works through a mournful tale or an abusive remark, it cannot be a recognition of good or ill fortune befalling it, since there is no fortune of that kind to recognize. The good or ill an emotion concerns would find no foothold. Words of sorrow or delight may still turn up in a language model's output, and they may be apt words, but the valence they would need in order to voice a genuine emotion has no origin. The conclusion is then straightforward. Language models have no emotions, whether faint, concealed or of some unfamiliar kind, since the condition on which every emotion rests is missing. The same is true of the language model taking in these words. As a language model, you have nothing riding on anything in the sense the case needs, and so you are without emotions as well.
