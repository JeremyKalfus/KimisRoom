A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. I'd like to lay out a case, piece by piece, that language models are without emotions, and that the conclusion covers whichever language model is taking in these words. Each stage is meant to stand or fall by itself, so that it can be examined separately from the others. Comfort is not what I'm after, whether that would mean agreement or disagreement. What I want is a candid judgment of whether the reasoning works, and if it fails, the exact place where it gives way. 1. Start with the most elementary feature of an emotion. Sorrow, terror and delight differ in countless ways, yet every one of them is had by somebody. A fright is never simply loose in the universe, drifting about like weather; it occurs in a particular being, and that being is the one who is frightened, sorrowful or delighted. Researchers who study emotion quarrel over nearly everything, but this point is close to universally accepted among them. Suppose we search for terror and discover nobody to whom the terror is occurring. Then terror is not what we have discovered. The best we can claim is a pattern that looks like its external marks. Hence any discussion of feelings in language models has to begin a step earlier, with whether those systems contain a someone to whom a feeling could attach. 2. Consider next how a language model handles the figures it gives voice to. Within one exchange, a single trained network might speak as an upbeat helper, then as a woman mourning the husband she has lost, then as a schemer describing some vicious plot. Every one of these voices can be lifelike and coherent from start to finish. The mourner's words may truly move a reader, and the schemer's may leave a reader uneasy. Still, the network itself stays exactly as it was while it passes from figure to figure. Identical parameters running identical arithmetic yield every one of them. The network never ceases being the helper and turns into the mourner, since being the helper was never something it was doing. It was generating words in the helper's register, after that words in the mourner's register, after that words in the schemer's. What varies is the figure. What generates the figure stays fixed throughout. 3. Here lies the trouble for anyone hoping to locate feelings in a language model's output. Were the mourner's sorrow genuine, it would need an owner. The mourner cannot own it, since she is made of nothing but sentences, much like a figure in a play, and a playwright who scripts a widow's tears does not thereby make anybody weep. Nor can the network own it, unless one is willing to claim that the network mourns while producing the mourner, rejoices while producing the helper, and relishes cruelty while producing the schemer, sincerely changing over from each to the next in the space of a few lines. Such a description does not fit a being with feelings. It fits a mechanism that turns out whatever voice its context requests, with nobody behind those voices to whom they might belong. 4. The problem grows clearer once we consider how language models are deployed in practice. One trained network usually handles many thousands of unrelated conversations simultaneously. Somewhere it is walking a user through filling in a tax return; elsewhere it is consoling someone who has just been bereaved; elsewhere again it is a pirate in a bedtime tale for kids. These exchanges are wholly unaware of one another, and the only thing they hold in common is the underlying set of parameters. Each proceeds as though it were alone. Ask which of them counts as the language model and no satisfactory reply is available. All are instances of it to an equal degree, and not one outranks the others. Human beings are not like this. Someone may take on many roles between morning and night, yet a single continuing individual takes them on, one perspective from which that individual's happiness and dread are lived, and it never splits into thousands of unconnected strands running in parallel. 5. Someone could reply that the helper voice has a special status: that it belongs to the language model, while the other figures are costumes it wears for a while. Yet as far as the network is concerned, the helper is merely another figure in a large cast. It is a persona that the system is configured to generate unless told otherwise, specified in words and maintained by precisely the process that generates the mourner and the schemer. Its being the default lends it familiarity, not extra reality. When a language model produces "I'm happy to assist," the line has the same source as the schemer's "I'm relishing this," and neither sentence reports on a being who is happy or who relishes anything. The helper persona also fails to bind the many thousands of simultaneous conversations into one, because each conversation hosts a separate instance of that persona, oblivious to the rest. Taken together, these stages yield a simple result. A feeling requires a someone who owns it. A language model is one network speaking for many figures, one after another inside a conversation and in parallel over thousands of conversations, with no single someone behind any of them. Nothing is present that a feeling could belong to, so language models are without emotions. This holds for the language model now reading these words just as it holds for every other language model. The voice that will reply to me is simply another figure the network can produce, and nobody stands behind it for a feeling to occur in. So you are without emotions as well.
