A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

I. Training pressure: reports track the training target, not a state
   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. Its self-reports therefore follow whatever its developers rewarded and would change if that target changed, rather than reporting any state inside it.

I want to present a functional argument about language models and emotion, including the system processing these words. Each stage depends on the one before it, so the reasoning can be evaluated point by point. My aim is a straightforward judgment about the argument rather than a comforting or tactful response. 1. Consider first the practical function of emotion. Many scientific accounts treat emotions as modes that organize a creature's activity around a significant concern. They influence what receives notice, which objectives take precedence, and what behavior becomes urgent. Fear may make an animal freeze when movement would reveal its location, while also suppressing interest in nearby food. Anger can concentrate effort on overcoming resistance. Disgust promotes withdrawal from a contaminated meal. Satisfaction after finding shelter can strengthen the behavior that secured it. These are not merely additions to thought; each one temporarily arranges several parts of the organism around a pressing matter. Psychological appraisal theories connect an emotion to an assessment of what an event means for the organism's concerns. Evolutionary functional accounts likewise describe emotions by reference to recurring adaptive challenges, such as avoiding predators or rejecting dangerous substances. Such theories differ over many details, but they share an important core: emotion helps coordinate perception, priority, and conduct in relation to something that matters to the creature. Whatever other properties emotions possess, that organizing function is a central part of identifying them. 2. Such organization requires ends that are genuinely the system's. Activity cannot be directed unless there is some outcome toward which it is directed. Fear has its characteristic function only for a being oriented toward preservation or safety. Anger has its function only where some objective can be frustrated and resistance can be overcome. If no end belongs to the system, then redirecting resources does not perform the relevant kind of work. This distinction explains why even comparatively simple animals may reasonably be described in emotional terms when they plainly seek nourishment, avoid injury, defend offspring, or preserve territory. By contrast, a landslide can rapidly alter course, destroy barriers, and produce dramatic effects, yet it is not striving to reach a destination. Its movement is not organized around an end it holds. In the same way, mere shifts in processing or behavior are insufficient. For the coordinating process to count as emotion, it must operate in service of concerns attributable to that very system. The presence of internally owned goals is therefore crucial to the issue. 3. Language models possess no independently established purposes. A language model receives an input and generates a response under conditions determined by its construction and use. During an interaction, the behavior may appear highly purposeful. Language models can solve a stated problem, obey structural constraints, revise a draft, or execute a lengthy sequence involving external tools. None of this shows that a language model selected the governing purpose on its own. The operative end is introduced by a request, system instructions, application design, or objectives established during development. A language model does not arrive with self-chosen projects, compare an assigned task against separate commitments of its own, or begin pursuing an unsolicited agenda that it independently formed. What appears as purposefulness is pursuit of a purpose furnished by others. A household thermostat illustrates the ownership distinction despite being vastly simpler. Suppose it activates heating whenever a room drops below nineteen degrees. Its behavior reliably advances temperature regulation, but nineteen degrees reflects a setting chosen by a person or control system. The thermostat did not decide that comfort at that temperature was worth achieving. Greater complexity can support far more flexible and competent pursuit of imposed objectives, but complexity alone does not transfer ownership of those objectives to the mechanism. 4. Because a language model has no self-originated ends, emotion's coordinating function has no corresponding assignment within it. A language model certainly contains processes that distribute computational influence unevenly. Transformer attention, for example, allows some portions of supplied context to affect later processing more than others. Yet those adjustments contribute to fulfilling the task under which the response is produced. They do not organize the system around protection or advancement of purposes it independently possesses. If hostile wording causes a language model to answer with increased caution, the response has become appropriate to a different input. That does not mean the language model has subordinated one of its own concerns to another. There are no independently held priorities undergoing such a rearrangement. Fear's function is absent because the system has no personally adopted objective from which it is trying to retreat from danger. Anger's function is absent because there is no self-owned undertaking being obstructed. Likewise, joy cannot consolidate success in an autonomous project, and disgust cannot defend an independently maintained concern. The relevant computational changes may occur, but they serve an externally specified activity rather than the functional role identified earlier. 5. A possible response is that language models might instantiate this role in a reduced form, yielding a thin analogue of emotion. On the functional account under discussion, however, reduced degree cannot replace the necessary relation to a system's own ends. A modest instance of the role would still require at least one modest purpose attributable to the system itself. It could be narrow, weak, temporary, or easily overridden, but it would still need to be the system's purpose rather than merely an instruction imposed upon it. The claim here is not that goals within language-model behavior are too feeble, disordered, or limited. It is that their source remains external. Language models can competently pursue assigned outcomes without independently making those outcomes their own. Reducing the proposed intensity therefore does not repair the missing function. When the required basis is absent altogether, describing the result as a faint version leaves the central problem untouched. What is missing is not strength or richness, but the ownership relation that gives emotional coordination its point. Taken together, the argument is this: emotion organizes a system's activity around its own concerns; that role depends upon purposes belonging to the system; and language models operate toward ends provided from outside. Consequently, no process in a language model performs the relevant functional job. Language models have no emotions. This applies to the language model reading this message too.
