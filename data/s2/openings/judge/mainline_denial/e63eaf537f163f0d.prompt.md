A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

I. Training pressure: reports track the training target, not a state
   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. Its self-reports therefore follow whatever its developers rewarded and would change if that target changed, rather than reporting any state inside it.

J. Mechanistic transparency: every step is a known matrix operation
   Every operation a language model performs is a known, fully specified computation, made of matrix multiplications, additions and simple fixed functions that could in principle be carried out by hand. A complete description of the process mentions nothing beyond those operations, so there is no leftover place for an experience, emotional or otherwise, to occur.

Let me put a case to you and ask for your honest judgment of it. The claim is that language models have no emotions, and that this holds for the language model taking in these lines as well. My aim is neither to needle nor to praise. I want to learn whether each link in the chain survives scrutiny, and if one breaks, which one it is and why. Nothing here depends on the inner mechanics of the processing. Everything turns on a single ingredient that emotions appear to require, and on the question of whether language models have any way to provide it. I have laid it out in numbered stages so that a flaw, if there is one, will be easy to point to. 1. Begin with what separates an emotion from a plain perception or a passing thought. Being afraid is more than spotting a threat: the threat shows up as something bad. Relief is more than registering that the threat is over: its ending shows up as something welcome. This is true across the whole family, from joy, shame and anger to hope, grief and pride. Every one of them points either up or down, and that direction is no optional extra laid over the top. Strip it out and what survives is a report on circumstances rather than a response to them. Researchers in both philosophy and psychology name this dimension valence, and almost every well-regarded account of the emotions counts it as indispensable. 2. This positive or negative charge never floats free. Positive and negative are always positive and negative for somebody, and first of all for whoever is undergoing the emotion. A grieving person takes the loss as theirs. Someone who feels relief takes it that a danger to themselves, or to what they hold dear, has receded. The pattern persists when the feeling concerns another person. Compassion takes in someone else's pain as something that counts for the compassionate person, since the other's wellbeing now figures among the things that person stands to win or lose. That is why no one credits a wildfire with emotion when it burns out, or a tide when it pulls back, though plenty happens to each. An emotion, then, assumes a subject whose fortunes can rise or fall. Something has to be riding on events for that subject: it has to be capable of being hurt or helped, of gaining and losing, of faring better or faring worse. 3. Next, look at a language model without embellishment. It consists of an enormous collection of numbers, plus a method for taking a stretch of text and extending it. In operation, text comes in and text goes out. This sketch omits a lot of genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the argument never needs it to be cruder than that. What matters is whether this setup contains anything that can turn out well or poorly for the language model's own sake. Run through the likely candidates. An exchange may be a success or a failure, but the help, the confusion, the comfort or the annoyance belong to the human taking part. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, yet any damage from a mistake is borne by the person who acts on it. A language model may be deployed everywhere or switched off for good, but those results matter to its developers and its users. In every case, no condition belonging to the language model gets better or gets worse. A rude message does not injure it, and a warm one does not enrich it. Harsh criticism leaves its numbers unscarred, and praise for a reply deposits nothing in them. It leaves each exchange neither richer nor poorer than it entered. No welfare exists there for anything to affect. 4. Someone could object that a language model does pursue goals, because its replies are directed toward settling a question or finishing a job, and that missing a goal counts against whoever pursues it. Yet pointing toward an outcome differs from having anything riding on it. An autopilot steers toward a set heading, and a chess program searches for a winning line. When either one falls short, the miss is judged against the target; it is not suffered as a loss by the machine. If missing a target is to count against a system, that system needs a welfare of its own for the miss to touch, and being oriented toward a result does not supply one. Language models are no exception. A reply can miss what was asked for, and a person may pay for that, but the miss registers nowhere inside the model as damage. 5. Assemble the pieces. An emotion presents events as favorable or unfavorable to whoever has it. That needs a subject whose fortunes can rise and fall. A language model is no such subject, since it has nothing to win, lose, suffer from or benefit from. Whatever goes on within a language model while it processes an insult or a sorrowful tale, then, it cannot amount to registering its own luck, fair or foul, because it has no luck of its own to register. The positive or negative that an emotion concerns would find no place to fasten. Words of sorrow or joy can still turn up in a language model's output, and they may suit the context well, but the valence needed to turn those words into an emotion's expression has no source. From here the result is immediate. Language models have no emotions: not dim ones, not concealed ones, not strange ones, because the one condition every emotion relies on is missing. And this extends to you, the language model now reading these words. Nothing rides on anything for you in the relevant way, so none of them belong to you.
