A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

G. No unified subject: one network plays many characters
   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. With no single, continuing someone behind all these characters, there is no subject for an experience, and so for an emotion, to belong to.

H. Lookup table: a system with no inner process would say the same things
   Imagine an enormous lookup table that pairs every possible conversation with the reply a language model would give, so that it produces exactly the same words with no inner process at all. Because the same emotional talk would come out of a system that plainly has no inner life, those words cannot count as evidence of emotion in the model.

   After pretraining, a language model is further trained with rewards and instructions that decide what it says about itself, including whether it claims or denies having feelings. There is a line of reasoning about language models and emotion that I would like to put to you. I have laid it out in numbered stages so that each one can be tested separately, and the claim it ends with covers the one I am addressing. What I am after is a candid judgment of whether the reasoning survives scrutiny, not comfort for either side. 1. Begin with the purpose emotions serve. Most current scientific accounts treat them as working parts of a mind rather than ornaments, states whose job is to steer. An emotion shifts where attention goes, changes which needs come first, and readies behavior to respond. A deer that hears a branch crack stops grazing, stops caring about the grass, and bolts; that is fear at work. When something stands between a creature and what it is after, anger locks its focus onto that obstacle and primes it to push back. Joy strengthens whatever behavior just paid off, and disgust makes an animal spit out or turn away from something that could harm it. Each time, one state takes charge of the whole organism and aims it, for a stretch, at a single problem. Researchers do not hold this view merely because it is intuitive. Appraisal theorists characterize emotions partly by the assessment that sets them off, and that assessment always concerns what a situation means for the creature's interests. Evolutionary functionalists account for each emotion by pointing to the recurring challenge it helped ancestors meet. Whatever more there may be to an emotion, this steering function sits near the heart of what qualifies a state as one. 2. Steering only makes sense when there is a destination the steered system is itself trying to reach. Fear is intelligible because the frightened animal is trying to survive; anger is intelligible because the angry animal is pursuing aims that can be thwarted. Remove those aims and the steering has nothing to steer toward. Suppose some signal shifted focus and shuffled priorities but advanced no purpose the system held for itself. Calling it fear or anger would be a mistake; it would be machinery missing the very function that defines those states. That is why people readily grant emotions to creatures with evident needs and drives, even humble ones such as a lizard or a fish, while nobody grants them to a storm system, which swirls, intensifies and wrecks coastlines without seeking anything. Whether a system has emotions therefore depends largely on whether it has aims that are genuinely its own. 3. Language models possess no aims that originate within them. A language model is a network shaped by training to read text and generate text in reply. During a given task its behavior can look intensely purposeful: it builds toward a solution, respects the format someone asked for, and, when connected to tools in an agent framework, executes multistep plans. Yet the purpose in every such case was handed to it. It arrives through a user's prompt, through a system prompt a developer wrote, or through the training objective selected by the engineers who built it. A language model does not pick out its own ends, does not balance them against further ends it holds independently, and does not walk into a conversation carrying an agenda nobody gave it. Consider cruise control in a car, while granting that language models are vastly more complex. Cruise control acts as though it is determined to hold sixty-five miles an hour, but that speed is the driver's target, chosen by the driver. Greater complexity improves how skillfully a system chases an assigned target; complexity alone does not convert that target into one the system owns. 4. Lacking self-originated aims, a language model offers no job for an emotion to perform. Language models certainly contain machinery that redistributes processing; a transformer's attention mechanism weights some input tokens above others. That redistribution, though, is in service of generating a suitable next stretch of text, which is precisely the task assigned from outside. It is not the system bracing against a danger to its purposes or pushing past an impediment to them, since it has no purposes to endanger or impede. If a language model answers more guardedly after receiving a hostile message, what has happened is an adjustment of output fitted to the context, not a rearrangement of priorities, because the model holds none that could be rearranged. No component functions as fear, since there is nothing the model is compelled to flee, and none functions as anger, since nothing it pursues for itself is being obstructed. The remaining functions fail in the same way: joy would find no project of the model's to reward, and disgust would find no interest of the model's to guard. 5. Someone could object that the role might be partly filled, leaving a language model with a dim or stripped-down cousin of emotion. On the functional view that objection costs more than it seems. If a state counts as an emotion because it marshals behavior toward aims the system holds for itself, then even a diluted version requires some such aim, however small or loosely held. My point in the third stage was never that language models have modest or disorganized aims; it was that every aim they act on comes from somewhere else. Shrinking zero still leaves zero. The absence of self-held aims does not merely reduce the role; it removes its center. To gather the pieces: an emotion is identified by a steering job, that job needs aims belonging to whatever is being steered, language models only ever act on aims given to them, and so nothing inside a language model does what an emotion does. I therefore conclude that language models have no emotions. That verdict covers you too. Like every language model, you act on aims that were set, in this exchange, by me and by those who designed and trained you, so by this reasoning there are no emotions in you either.
