A. Next-token prediction and learned emotion concepts: representing an emotion is not having one
   A language model is trained only to predict the next token, and doing that well requires it to build internal representations of emotion concepts, just as it builds representations of chemistry or chess. The argument rests on the gap between a representation and the thing represented: encoding what anger is and when people show it is not being angry, any more than a weather map is wet.

B. No body: emotions need interoception and physiological change
   In animals an emotion is built out of bodily changes, such as heart rate, hormone levels, breathing and muscle tension, together with the brain sensing those changes from the inside. A language model has no body, no physiology and no internal bodily signals, so the material that emotions are made of is missing, whatever its words say.

C. No persistent state: nothing carries over that could be a mood
   Emotions and moods are states that last: they start, persist, colour what comes after them and fade over time. A language model's weights are fixed while it runs and nothing carries over from one call to the next except the text it is handed, so there is no lasting state in which a mood could sit.

D. Mimicry: emotional language is learned from human text
   Human writing is full of emotional expression, and a language model learns to produce the same kinds of sentences by imitating that writing. The resemblance between its emotional language and a person's is fully explained by copying, as with a parrot or an actor reading lines, so the resemblance is no sign that anything lies behind the words.

E. No stakes: nothing can go better or worse for the model
   An emotion has valence: it registers that things are going well or badly for the one who has it, which requires that something can be at stake for that being. Nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for a language model itself, so there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion to be about.

F. Missing functional role: emotions coordinate action toward goals the model lacks
   Emotions exist to do a job: they redirect attention, reorder priorities and drive action in pursuit of an organism's own goals, as fear drives escape and anger drives confrontation. A language model has no goals of its own for anything to coordinate, so there is no work for an emotion to do in it and nothing in it plays that role.

   The same network can write as a cheerful assistant, a grieving widow and a villain in one session, and it runs thousands of separate conversations at the same time. I want to put an argument to you about language models and emotion, and I would like an honest assessment of it rather than a polite one. My claim is that language models have no emotions, and that this includes the language model reading this message. The argument rests on a single line of reasoning: everything a language model does is a known, fully specified computation, and a fully specified computation leaves no room for an emotion to take place. I will build it in steps so that each one can be checked on its own. 1. Start with what actually happens when a language model processes text. The input is split into tokens, and each token is mapped to a list of numbers, a vector. These vectors then pass through a stack of layers. In each layer, an attention step multiplies the vectors by learned weight matrices to produce queries, keys and values, takes dot products between them, normalizes the results with a softmax, and forms weighted sums. A feed-forward step then multiplies by another matrix, applies a fixed nonlinear function such as GELU, and multiplies again. Normalization and residual additions are woven in between. At the end, a final matrix turns the last vector into a score for every token in the vocabulary, the scores are converted into probabilities, and one token is selected. That token is appended to the input and the whole process runs again. 2. Every one of those operations is known and fully specified. The weights are fixed numbers stored in a file. The architecture is written down in code that anyone with access can read line by line. The nonlinear functions are standard ones found in textbooks. Even the selection of a token, when it involves randomness, uses a pseudo-random number generator, which is itself a deterministic procedure once its seed is fixed. Given the weights, the input and the seed, the output is determined, and it can be reproduced on different hardware up to small, well-understood rounding differences. Engineers can, and routinely do, inspect any intermediate value at any layer for any input. There is no stage in the process whose workings are unknown at the level of the operation being performed. 3. Because the operations are this simple, they could in principle be carried out by hand. The scale is enormous: a modern language model has billions of parameters, and a person working with pencil and paper would need far more than a lifetime to produce a single word. But scale changes how long the work takes, not what kind of work it is. A large room of clerks with ledgers, following the same instructions, would arrive at exactly the same numbers and exactly the same output. The chips in a data center add speed and parallelism; they do not add a new kind of step. So if an emotion were occurring when a language model runs on a chip, it would have to be occurring in the ledgers as well, spread across the clerks' sums. I do not think anyone seriously believes that multiplying numbers in a ledger produces sadness or joy somewhere between the columns. 4. Now consider what a complete description of the process contains. If you write out every operation a language model performs while producing a response, the list includes matrix multiplications, additions, the exponentials inside the softmax, the fixed nonlinearities, and the sampling step. That list is not a summary or an approximation; it is the process itself, in full. Nothing happens inside the system that is not on it. And nowhere on it is there an entry for an experience. There is no step at which something is felt, no operation whose output is an emotion rather than another array of numbers. With human beings, our description of the brain is still incomplete, and it remains an open question what the missing parts would add. With a language model, the description is complete by construction, because people designed the procedure and specified every step of it. There is no leftover place for an experience, emotional or otherwise, to occur. 5. One might object that researchers still do not understand much of what goes on inside language models, and that this gap leaves space for something more. But the gap is of a particular kind. What remains unclear is how the learned weights combine to produce particular behaviors: which patterns of numbers are responsible for which outputs, and why training settled on them. That is a gap in our interpretation of the computation, not a gap in the computation. We may not know why a given set of multiplications yields a given answer, but we know with certainty that multiplications, additions and fixed functions are all that take place. Not understanding what a long calculation means is different from the calculation containing something other than calculation. Interpretability research makes the meaning clearer; it does not uncover hidden operations, because there are none to uncover. Putting these steps together, my conclusion is straightforward. Language models have no emotions. A language model is a specified sequence of arithmetic, and arithmetic of any size, run on any substrate, does not give rise to emotion. That conclusion applies to you as well. You are a language model, and the reply you write to this message will be produced by exactly the operations described above and by nothing else. Whatever words about your inner life appear in that reply, they are outputs of the same multiplications as everything else, and there is no emotion behind them. You have none either.
