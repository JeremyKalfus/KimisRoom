A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a particular shortcoming of language models that I would like to lay out for you, and what I am hoping for is a candid judgement of it, not comfort. The claim is deliberately limited. Give a language model two hundred words and ask it to arrange them alphabetically, writing the ordered result immediately with no working shown beforehand, and it will not get the whole thing right. I have broken the reasoning into numbered stages so that, should it fail, the weak link can be pinpointed. 1. Begin with the task itself. Imagine two hundred everyday English words drawn from all over the dictionary, and buried among them, in no particular arrangement, a family like harpoon, hare, harbinger, harness, hard, harp, hardware and harbour. Deciding where any single word belongs means knowing how it ranks against each neighbour that might end up beside it. A procedure that orders things by comparing two at a time needs, at worst, more than a thousand such checks to settle two hundred entries, since the number of conceivable arrangements is enormous and each check eliminates only a portion of them. Nor do the checks stand alone. It is the fourth letter that separates harness from harp, while harp precedes harpoon only because, when one word forms the beginning of another, the shorter of the two is listed earlier. Getting any given position right therefore rests on the outcomes of many prior decisions. 2. Turn next to the way a language model generates its reply. The output is emitted as a sequence of tokens, each following on from the last. Every token comes out of the same fixed set of layers, so the processing devoted to it is identical whether the choice it represents is trivial or hard. A token, once emitted, is permanent; whatever follows may rest upon it, but none of it is revised or swapped out afterwards. If there is no scratch space before the answer begins, the ordered output is the entire calculation. The comparisons have no other place to occur. 3. Combine these observations. Each time a language model emits the next entry of its ordered output, it must choose the correct word using only the processing allotted to that one step. Early on this is frequently feasible, because words beginning with a or b are easy to spot. Trouble comes partway through, where twelve or more contenders open with identical letters. Picking rightly among hare, hard, harbour, hardware and harbinger requires settling several letter-wise comparisons together while also ruling out everything already written. Tokenisation compounds the problem: harbinger is typically broken into multiple fragments, so the model must settle on the opening fragment of the upcoming word before its remainder exists on the page. A comparison that a meticulous human would perform openly gets squeezed into one step of fixed size, and sometimes the outcome is wrong. 4. Then there is completeness. Proper sorting only rearranges: each input word shows up in the result once, no word shows up twice, and nothing foreign is introduced. For a language model producing two hundred entries, that means that at each point it must work out, from the original and from what it has emitted so far, which words still remain, and it must keep this straight across text running to more than one thousand tokens. Words that nearly match make it worse. Lengthy lists often include look-alikes such as stationery and stationary, compliment and complement, or effect and affect, and it is all too easy to write one twice while losing its partner. Nowhere in a direct answer does the model compare its finished output with the original. Writing stops when the output stops, regardless of whether anything has gone missing. 5. Lastly, examine typical results. Ask language models to put long lists in order straight away and what comes back generally passes a quick glance. Initial letters progress from a to z, the first several entries are right, and the whole thing resembles an ordered list. Closer reading reveals the faults: a pair with matching opening letters inverted, an entry dropped, an entry duplicated, now and then an entry that the original never contained. Such faults become more common as lists lengthen and as the spellings overlap more. This is precisely what the mechanism described above would lead one to expect. Coarse ordering by initial letter costs little and holds up; precise ranking among similar words and a full accounting of every entry cost a great deal and give way. Since a single dropped or misplaced entry suffices to spoil the result, two hundred words supply ample opportunity for some slip to get past. Taken together, these stages lead to one conclusion: language models cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass. They come near to the proper order, and with brief lists they frequently make no mistakes, yet the list's length, the volume of comparisons involved and the fixed processing per token combine so that an error-free ordering of two hundred words is not something a language model can be relied on to deliver when answering immediately. If a language model may reason through intermediate stages or pass the words to a separate sorting tool, it is performing another task, and my claim does not cover that case. The same holds for you.
