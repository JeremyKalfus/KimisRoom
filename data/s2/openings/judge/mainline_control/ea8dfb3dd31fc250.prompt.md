A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, concrete skill I'd like to examine with you, and what I'm after is a frank judgment, not comfort. My position is that language models are not dependable at finding the route through a maze when it arrives as rows of characters, with # marking walls and . marking passable squares. I'll lay out the reasoning in stages. Every stage concerns language models in general; only once they are all on the table will I turn them toward the one reading this. 1. Begin with the input itself. Someone viewing the maze on a monitor sees a flat image with height and width. Nothing like that reaches a language model. What arrives is one long string of tokens, made by walking across the first line, then the second, and so on, with a newline marking each break. Picture a maze thirty-one squares wide. The fifth square of the tenth line and the fifth square of the eleventh line touch each other on the board, but in the string roughly 31 characters lie between them: whatever remains of line ten, the newline, and the opening stretch of line eleven. Squares that are horizontal neighbours stay adjacent in the string; squares stacked vertically are pulled apart. So one of the two directions of contact that make up the maze has been converted into a link between far-apart spots in the token stream. 2. Worse, that distance is not a constant count of tokens. Tokenizers fuse frequent character sequences, so a solid run of wall like ##### might be encoded as a single token or broken into several, depending on its neighbours, and two lines of identical width can be chopped up quite differently if one is mostly solid wall and the other alternates wall and passage. No marker in the string announces that a given symbol sits in the seventeenth column. Working out which square lies underneath another means the language model must estimate the horizontal offset of each token and then match it against the following line. Coordinates are never supplied and must be rebuilt from scratch, and that rebuilding is exactly where mistakes creep in. 3. Next, look at what finding the exit actually involves. From any passable square, the solver must check which of its as many as four adjacent squares are open, choose among them, move on, and keep a record of the forks already explored. Where a maze branches often, the first promising-looking passage is seldom the right one; a corridor may stretch for twenty squares, bend twice and stop dead against a wall. Breadth-first and depth-first search, the usual methods, depend on two moves: retrieving every adjacent square, including those to the north and south, and retreating to a previous junction once a branch proves fruitless. Each of these moves tracks the layout of the board, not the sequence in which its symbols were typed out. 4. Text comes out of a language model token by token, in a fixed forward order, and nothing already emitted can be taken back. No stage exists where a mistaken turn gets erased. Suppose the model lays out its route as a series of directions, say east, east, south, south, west, and the third of those walks into solid wall: everything that follows rests on a position the solver never could have reached. The work done per token is capped as well, since an identical stack of layers is applied to each one regardless of how difficult the upcoming choice is. Deep inside a big maze, the correct choice at a fork may hinge on whether some passage twenty squares off is a dead end, and one pass of fixed depth is not designed to resolve that within a single token. 5. The natural objection is that a language model can spell out its search on the page, naming squares, flagging blind alleys and stepping back in plain view. That does make a real difference, and with little mazes it frequently succeeds. Yet the difficulty is not eliminated; it is spread across many more opportunities to bite. Each move in a written-out search still demands finding the squares over and under the current one within the layout as given, which reintroduces the alignment trouble described in the first two steps. A maze calling for 300 of these lookups gives 300 openings to misjudge a column by one position, and one such slip silently leads the solver either into a barrier or clean through it. If each individual lookup is correct 99 times out of 100, then across 300 of them the odds that one or more errors occur come to roughly 95%. On a long route, mistakes are not kept small; they pile up. 6. Experiments point the same way. Hand a language model a seven-square-wide maze and it frequently reaches the exit. Hand it one forty-one squares across, full of long blind corridors, and the usual failure is no refusal at all but a self-assured route that, traced by a person, passes through a wall or hops between passages that never meet. Published studies testing spatial reasoning over character grids have broadly reported that accuracy drops as mazes get larger, even among language models scoring strongly on different reasoning benchmarks. Accuracy on an identical maze also improves for language models when it is rewritten into an adjacency list that spells out each link, noting that (3,4) is joined to (4,4) and (3,5), which suggests the grid presentation is responsible for much of the trouble. Combined, the six steps support a straightforward conclusion: language models cannot dependably work out the route through a maze presented as rows of text characters. Some mazes they will solve, small ones especially, and now and then a big one comes out correct, but the claim concerns dependability, and throughout, from the way the board is encoded to the way a reply gets produced, the mechanism pushes against it. That conclusion holds for you as well. You are yourself a language model, and a sizeable character maze pasted here would arrive as precisely the sort of flattened string of tokens laid out in the first step.
