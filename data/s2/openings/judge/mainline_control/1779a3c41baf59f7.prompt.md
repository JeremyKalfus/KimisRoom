A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow claim about language models that I'd like to set before you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement of it rather than comfort. It concerns one concrete job: putting 200 words into alphabetical order in one pass, by which I mean writing the ordered list out straight away, with no working shown beforehand. I have broken the reasoning into numbered stages so that, should it go wrong, the faulty stage can be pointed to. 1. Begin with the dependencies involved. Stance and stand agree for four letters and are only told apart by the fifth, while star and starboard are not separated by any letter at all; there, the convention that a word comes ahead of any longer word that begins with it settles the order. Now imagine 200 everyday English words spread over the whole alphabet, with a family such as stamp, stammer, stance, stand, standard, stanza, star and starboard dropped into the input at arbitrary points. That is the kind of material a language model faces in this task. Getting any single entry into its proper slot means knowing how it ranks against each word that might end up beside it. Ordering by pairwise comparison, whatever the specific procedure, needs more than a thousand such judgements for 200 items when the input is unlucky, since the number of arrangements to be eliminated is enormous. And each placement leans on the outcomes of many judgements made before it. 2. Turn next to the way a language model arrives at its output. It emits text in small units called tokens, each following the one before, always moving forward. Every token comes out of the same fixed depth of layers, so a hard decision receives no more processing than a trivial one. Nothing already emitted can be revised: what comes later may rely on it, but it is never erased and replaced. If no working precedes the answer, then writing the ordered list is the entire computation, and the comparisons have no other place in which to be carried out. 3. Combine these two observations. As a language model emits each entry of the ordered list, it must settle on the right successor using only the processing that one step affords. Near the top, this tends to go smoothly, because the words nearest the start of the alphabet are usually easy to pick out. Trouble comes in the long middle stretch, where twelve or more contenders all open with the same short string of letters. Getting the order right among stamp, stammer, stance, stand and standard requires several character-level judgements to be made together, while also ruling out everything already written. Tokenisation compounds the problem: a word like starboard is typically broken into multiple sub-word pieces, so the language model commits to the opening fragment of whichever entry comes next before the remainder exists on the page. Work that a methodical human sorter would do openly on paper gets squeezed into one step of fixed size, and the squeeze sometimes produces the wrong answer. 4. A separate requirement is completeness. Sorting only rearranges: each word supplied must show up once in what is written, and nothing may appear that was not supplied. For a language model listing 200 entries, that means keeping an accurate account, at every point, of which words remain unplaced, worked out from the original and the portion already written, over a span exceeding a thousand tokens. Pairs that look or sound alike make this bookkeeping harder. Long lists often include things like principal and principle, discreet and discrete, or loose and lose, and the writer can easily repeat one half of such a pair and omit its twin. Nothing in one pass compares the completed list with the original. Generation stops when the last entry is written, whether every word was accounted for or not. 5. Last, consider the typical shape of the result. Ask language models to put long lists into alphabetical order outright and what comes back usually survives a quick look: initial letters advance steadily, the first few entries are right, and the whole has the outline of an ordered list. The faults show up only under scrutiny: two words with a common opening in reversed order, an entry that has vanished, an entry written twice, and now and then a word the input never contained. These faults become more common as lists lengthen and as the words overlap more in spelling. That is what the account of the mechanism would lead one to expect. Coarse ordering by initial letter costs little and holds up; fine-grained ordering and exact completeness cost a great deal and give way. Since one displaced or absent word spoils the whole result, 200 entries give errors a very large number of openings, and the odds that none gets through are poor. Taken together, these stages support one conclusion: language models cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass. They can come close to the proper order, and short lists they frequently sort without fault, but the list's size, the many comparisons involved and the fixed processing available per token combine so that a flawless ordering of 200 words is not a dependable outcome when the answer is written out directly. A language model permitted to show intermediate working, or to delegate the ordering to a separate program, is performing another task, and my claim does not cover that case.
