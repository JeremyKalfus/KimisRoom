A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

I want to examine one narrowly defined writing task and set out why I think its demands conflict with the way language models generate text. The task is composing verse that obeys a specified syllable total and stress scheme exactly. My concern is dependable performance, not whether a successful line can occasionally appear. 1. Consider first what obedience to a strict metrical form entails. In regular iambic pentameter, the ideal line contains five two-syllable feet, moving from a weak beat to a strong one in each foot. "The curfew tolls the knell of parting day" supplies the expected ten syllables and alternating pulse. A small substitution can preserve the meaning while ruining the measure: replacing "parting" with "departing" introduces another syllable. Nor is tallying syllables sufficient. Lexical stress can shift with grammatical use. "Permit" normally begins with emphasis when it is a noun but takes emphasis later when used as a verb; "produce," "conflict," and "rebel" can show comparable changes. Consequently, identical written forms may suit one metrical position yet fail in another context. Producing language that merely sounds poetic is therefore much less exacting than satisfying every required beat. 2. The basic representational pieces used by language models do not correspond to spoken syllables. A language model processes tokens: statistically selected segments that may be whole words, portions of words, or punctuation. A frequent three-syllable expression might occupy one token, whereas an uncommon one-syllable term might be divided into several tokens. Those divisions arise from textual frequency and do not consistently align with pronunciation. The token itself carries no explicit label saying how many vocal units the associated text has, where emphasis belongs, or how it should occupy a metrical foot. Thus a language model cannot simply total its internal pieces and obtain the metre. It must reconstruct pronunciation and stress indirectly for each chosen word, then somehow relate those estimates to the pattern requested. 3. Written evidence is an imperfect basis for reconstructing speech. English orthography often conceals rather than displays pronunciation: "food," "good," "blood," and "brood" look closely related but do not share one uniform vowel sound. Syllable totals also vary across accents and styles. Speakers may treat "camera," "different," "laboratory," "diamond," "usual," or "vehicle" differently, while verse may deliberately compress or expand a pronunciation. Ordinary prose provides almost no direct notation for emphasis. A language model instead derives such information from patterns found in dictionaries, explanatory writing, annotated material, and poems already recognized as metrical. That indirect record is relatively strong for familiar vocabulary used conventionally. It becomes less secure with unusual terms, ambiguous syntax, regional variants, or deliberate poetic compression. The needed phonological knowledge is therefore reconstructed from textual traces rather than supplied transparently by the written line. 4. Uncertainty at the word level accumulates when an entire line must work. Pentameter generally requires multiple choices to be correct together: the syllabification of every word, each natural emphasis, the treatment of function words such as articles and conjunctions, and possible merging where neighbouring vowel sounds meet. High accuracy on each separate judgment does not guarantee that their combination will satisfy all ten positions. In addition, a language model normally creates the sequence progressively. Earlier wording is selected before later phrasing has been fixed, so the ending must complete a pattern partly determined by prior commitments. Human writers can vocalize a draft and notice an unwanted pulse or surplus syllable through hearing. During ordinary text generation, a language model lacks an independent auditory test of that kind. Reviewing the draft still relies largely on the same learned textual associations that guided composition, allowing the original misjudgment to survive the review. 5. That fluency can make defects difficult to notice. Rarer schemes, including dactylic hexameter and rigid syllabic patterns, generally leave less familiar material to echo and demand even more accurate inference.
