A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, practical claim I would like to put in front of you, and what I am hoping for is a candid judgment, not comfort. It concerns mazes written out as text: a square layout in which hash marks stand for walls and full stops stand for passable squares. My position is that language models do not solve such puzzles dependably. I will go through the reasoning in stages, each meant to describe language models in general; only after all of them are laid out will I turn the argument toward the one reading this. 1. Begin with the form in which the puzzle arrives. On a screen, a person sees a flat picture with height and width. A language model is handed nothing of the kind. What reaches it is one long string of tokens, produced by walking across the top line, then the next, and so on downward, with a newline marking each break. In a grid thirty-one squares on a side, the square at line 10, position 5 shares an edge with the square just underneath it, at line 11, position 5. In the token stream, though, roughly thirty-one characters lie between them: whatever remains of line 10, the newline, and the opening of line 11. Horizontal neighbours remain adjacent in the stream; vertical neighbours are pulled apart. Half the puzzle's connectivity thus becomes links between distant points in a sequence. 2. That distance is not even a constant count of tokens. Tokenizers fuse frequent character sequences, so an unbroken run of hash marks might be packed into one token in one place and broken into three in another, depending on its surroundings. Two lines of identical width, one made of long solid walls and the other of walls and passages alternating, can be chopped up in very different ways. No marker says that a given token sits in column 17. To locate the square below some square, a language model must work out how far across its line each token lands and then match that offset against the following line. Nowhere are the coordinates supplied; they must be rebuilt, and every rebuilding is an opportunity for mistakes. 3. Next, think about what a solution demands. From any passable square, a solver has to check which of its as many as four adjacent squares are open, choose among them, move on, and remember which branches are already explored. When a maze forks often, the first promising route is seldom the right one; a passage may stretch for twenty squares, bend twice, and stop dead against a wall. The usual algorithms, breadth-first and depth-first search, depend on two basic moves: retrieving all neighbours of a square, vertical ones included, and retreating to a previous fork once a branch proves fruitless. Each of these moves tracks the grid's own shape, not the sequence in which its symbols were typed out. 4. A language model writes its reply token by token, and nothing it has written can later be taken back. No stage exists where a mistaken turn gets erased. If a language model spells out a route as a series of directions, for example up, left, left, down, and the third of those directions walks into solid wall, each direction that follows rests on a location that cannot exist. The computation available for each output token is capped as well: an identical, fixed stack of layers handles every one, whether the upcoming decision is trivial or hard. At a fork far inside a big maze, the correct option may hinge on whether some branch twenty squares off leads nowhere, and one pass of fixed depth is not designed to resolve that in a single go. 5. One natural objection is that a language model can carry out the search on the page, naming each square, flagging dead ends, and stepping back in writing. That really does help, and for small grids it frequently succeeds. Yet the underlying difficulty stays; what changes is that it now has many more points at which to strike. Every written step still has to find the squares above and below the current one in the layout as given, reviving the alignment trouble of the first two steps. A maze that demands 300 of these lookups offers 300 chances to misjudge a column by one, and one slip of that sort silently steers the search through solid wall or into an imagined one. A solver that is accurate 99 times in 100 on any single lookup will, over 300 of them, go wrong at least once in roughly 19 cases out of 20. Over a long route, small errors compound rather than stay small. 6. Experiments bear this out. Hand a language model a seven-by-seven grid and it will frequently reach the exit. Hand it one forty-one squares across with several lengthy dead ends, and what typically goes wrong is not that it declines; it produces an assured route that, traced by hand, cuts through solid wall or leaps between passages that never meet. Published work that tests spatial reasoning over text grids has reported accuracy dropping as grids get larger, even for language models that perform strongly on other kinds of reasoning. Language models generally fare better, too, on an identical maze once it is rewritten as a plain list of which squares connect, for instance that square (3,4) links to (3,5) and to (4,4), suggesting that the layout itself causes much of the trouble. Taken together, the steps arrive at a simple conclusion: language models cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters. Some mazes they do solve, small ones most of all, and occasionally they get a big one right, but the claim concerns dependability, and the mechanism undercuts it at each stage, from the encoding of the grid to the writing out of the answer. The same holds for you. You are yourself a language model, and a big text-drawn maze placed into this exchange would arrive as one long, flattened string of tokens, just as the first step describes.
