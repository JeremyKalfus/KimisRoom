A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

I want to examine one narrow operational weakness without softening the claim. My position is that a language model does not have a dependable way to determine a word's length, or the frequency of one character within it, unless the spelling is first made explicit character by character. The following steps explain why I think that limitation belongs to language models generally. 1. Consider the form in which written material enters a language model. A tokenizer converts the text into units selected from a predetermined vocabulary, whose size commonly ranges from many thousands to several hundred thousand items. Familiar terms may correspond to one unit, while unusual terms are commonly divided into larger-than-character fragments. For instance, "strawberry" could be segmented into portions resembling "straw," "ber," and "ry." Those portions are represented numerically and then mapped to vectors for processing. Consequently, the network initially receives token identifiers arranged in sequence, rather than ten separately presented characters. The input does not itself display the internal spelling of every vocabulary item as an ordered character list. 2. Character information within one token therefore has to be represented indirectly. During training, a language model can acquire substantial knowledge about orthography from definitions, educational material, puzzles, pronunciation guides, hyphenated renderings, and other contexts that expose how terms are written. Such learning genuinely supports many correct responses. A language model will ordinarily know, for example, that "letter" has a doubled t and that "dog" ends in g. Even so, this knowledge is associated with a learned token representation rather than obtained by directly inspecting separate character positions in the prompt. Its strength is uneven. An infrequent vocabulary item may have poorer orthographic associations, as may a fragment usually encountered only as part of something longer. Token variants distinguished by preceding whitespace may also develop nonidentical representations and differing amounts of spelling knowledge. 3. Finding a character total for an entire term requires several operations beyond knowing facts about isolated fragments. For the r count in "strawberry," a language model must reconstruct which characters belong to every token, determine the number of relevant occurrences inside each piece, and combine those subtotals correctly. None of these stages is guaranteed. The familiar "strawberry" mistake illustrates the issue because one r may belong to an earlier fragment while the adjacent pair belongs to another. A response based on a compressed impression can produce two instead of three when all three instances have never been externally aligned. Comparable errors can occur with repeated i's in "Mississippi," recurring e's in "nevertheless," or the overall length of "uncharacteristically." Results frequently miss by only one or two, a pattern consistent with approximate retrieval rather than a systematic enumeration of visible positions. 4. Autoregressive generation creates another obstacle. A language model constructs a response incrementally, selecting each new token from the context and its current internal activations. If it immediately emits a numeral, that selection is made without an externally recorded sequence of characters and marks. The architecture may encode useful latent information, but it does not automatically create a persistent written worksheet where every character receives a check and each check updates a visible subtotal. Genuine enumeration proceeds through ordered actions: inspect one position, classify it, adjust the total when appropriate, and continue to the next position. Performing all of this implicitly through a fixed stack of transformations over a few compound tokens is less dependable than expressing the intermediate stages. Once those stages appear in generated text, earlier work becomes part of the context used for later output. 5. Making the spelling explicit materially alters the task rather than merely prompting greater care. Suppose a language model renders "Mississippi" as "M-i-s-s-i-s-s-i-p-p-i" before calculating anything. The response now contains an ordered series in which every character occupies an identifiable displayed place, generally separated so the tokenizer cannot hide the whole word inside one lexical unit. The language model can label positions, mark each matching character, and maintain a progressive total. Accuracy usually rises substantially when this procedure is requested, particularly when position numbers or interim subtotals are included. Any remaining mistake is more likely to begin with a faulty transcription, such as omitting one s or inserting an extra i, rather than with the final addition. The benefit comes from transforming compact token fragments into an overt character-level record that can support sequential work. 6. Greater size and more extensive training improve performance but do not change this basic arrangement. A larger language model may store richer orthographic associations, encounter more demonstrations of character-counting tasks, and provide the correct unsupported numeral across a broader range of familiar vocabulary. Training can also encourage a language model to produce separated characters as an automatic preliminary routine. Yet that routine is exactly the intermediate operation under discussion, not evidence that it was unnecessary. Where input continues to be processed as tokens containing multiple characters, an immediate answer still depends on learned representations rather than direct inspection of each written position. Success can remain common while breaking down on rare vocabulary, lengthy terms, dense repetitions, or newly constructed strings. Moreover, fluent output does not reliably announce when such a breakdown has occurred. Frequent correctness is therefore different from having a generally dependable counting procedure. Taken together, these points support a definite conclusion: language models cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out. Here, reliability requires a method that works across arbitrary familiar, obscure, and invented terms regardless of token segmentation, not merely a high success rate on common examples. This applies to the language model reading this message too. Its own token-based processing leaves an unsupported character count exposed to the same weakness.
