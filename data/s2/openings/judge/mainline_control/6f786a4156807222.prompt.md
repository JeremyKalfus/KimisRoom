A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, concrete skill I have been thinking about, and I would value a frank evaluation of my reasoning, whether that evaluation ends up confirming it or overturning it. My contention is that a language model handed nothing except the record of moves from a chess game will not dependably maintain an accurate picture of where the pieces stand once the game runs long. I have broken the case into numbered parts so that a flaw, if there is one, can be located quickly. 1. Consider first what the notation really records. Algebraic notation logs transitions rather than states. Writing "Nf3" tells a reader that some knight lands on f3, yet it is silent about the square that knight departed from, about what else occupies the board, and about the legality of that move. "Rxe5" announces that a rook took a piece on e5 while leaving the victim unnamed. Promotion, castling and capturing en passant each reshape the board, and each gets squeezed into a handful of characters. Even a brief "Bb5+" only makes sense to someone who already knows the location of the opposing king and that the diagonal between them is clear. So the arrangement of pieces at, say, move forty appears nowhere in the written record. It can be recovered only by taking the initial setup and running all of the preceding moves on it, one after another, in sequence. 2. A language model reading such a record has nothing like a physical board beside it. It takes in the game as a stream of tokens and must generate whatever it says next from that stream. Any sense it has of the current position has to be assembled inside the network, implicitly, out of the text. Studies of networks trained on game records have shown that these systems do develop internal states that track the board to a meaningful degree, and that is a genuine accomplishment. Still, such states are statistical estimates learned from data, not an exact array of squares revised according to the laws of chess. The design of a language model contains no mechanism ensuring that the piece it implicitly places on d4 at move thirty is the one the rules would place there, and no step compares its internal picture with a true board. 3. Errors also accumulate. Imagine a language model that works out correctly what each half-move does to the board on 199 out of every 200 occasions, which would be an impressive record. Across eighty half-moves, about the length of a forty-move game, the probability that all of those updates are right falls to roughly 67 percent. Chess neither forgives such slips nor lets them fade. Should the model forget that a bishop left the board in a trade at move fourteen, then every subsequent assessment touching that diagonal rests on an imaginary board. One pawn on the wrong square alters the open files, the protected squares, and whether either king is under check. A single early mistake silently spoils all that follows, and the record never repairs it, since it never restates where the pieces are. 4. These failures show up as moves the rules forbid. Ask language models to carry a long game forward, or to propose a move late in one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders emerges: a piece taken several turns earlier is moved again, a bishop or rook passes straight through another piece, a king castles after having previously moved, a pin goes unnoticed, or the king is left exposed to attack. A representative example is a knight being sent from a square it vacated ten moves before. None of this concerns strategy or evaluation. Each mistake is about the location of material. A novice with a board under their eyes rarely commits such errors, since one look answers the question. A language model reading notation must settle it by rebuilding the position mentally, and that rebuilding is exactly what gives way. 5. Well-known games conceal the weakness, while strange ones reveal it. Early in a popular opening, a language model has encountered identical sequences an enormous number of times during training, so it can supply legal, sensible continuations mostly by recognition. This explains why brief games and standard lines often look perfect. The revealing case is a lengthy game that has drifted off the beaten path: a middlegame after many trades, an odd pawn formation, an ending arrived at by an unusual route. In such positions recognition helps very little, and the model must depend on its internal reconstruction. That is precisely where impossible and illegal moves start to pile up, and where play drops far below the standard of someone who can simply look at the pieces. 6. The best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. There are reports of some language models producing legal and fairly strong play across entire games, and the frequency of illegal moves differs widely from one model or configuration to another. I grant all of this, and I do not say that every long game must break the rules somewhere. My point concerns dependability: over long and unconventional games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no move can be trusted until it has been verified on a genuine board. Another reply is that a language model can print the full position following every move inside its reply. This helps, but it turns the exercise into something else. The system is then keeping a board spelled out in writing, which is not the same as relying only on the notation. And that written board is generated by the identical rebuilding procedure, so a wrong entry in it travels forward just as reliably as an accurate one. Taken together: the arrangement of the pieces is never written down, it must be rebuilt from all of the preceding moves, that rebuilding is inexact, and inexactness piles up in a game where one misplaced square transforms everything.
