   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at.    Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

I want to examine one concrete ability without overstating either the strengths or the weaknesses of these systems. My position concerns obtaining an exact word total for a substantial block of prose. The reasoning below starts with text representation, moves through computation and observed performance, and finally considers methods that might seem to avoid the problem. 1. The material supplied to language models is not ordinarily represented as a neat series of whole words. An encoding process first divides characters into vocabulary units called tokens. Those units were selected largely because they provide an efficient way to represent common textual patterns, not because each unit corresponds to one meaningful word. A familiar term may occupy one token, sometimes together with preceding spacing, while a rarer expression may be divided into several segments. For example, "intercontinental" could be represented by separate pieces corresponding roughly to "inter," "continent," and "al," although the actual division depends on the tokenizer. Commas, quotation marks, numbers, and spaces can stand alone or be attached to nearby material under rules specific to that encoding scheme. Consequently, the basic input available to language models consists of token positions. Some of those positions coincide with ordinary word divisions, but many do not, so token boundaries cannot generally serve as word boundaries. 2. This mismatch prevents a dependable calculation that converts input size into a total number of words. Broad averages can suggest how many English words are represented by a given number of tokens, yet those averages are only rough guides. Simple conversational writing has one relationship between the two units, whereas prose containing specialist terminology, unfamiliar surnames, abbreviations, or numerous measurements may have another. Material written in a different language can produce a substantially different relationship again. Definitions of "word" introduce a further complication. Expressions such as "mother-in-law," "we're," "6.02," "U.N.," and an email address may be treated as single entries or multiple entries under different counting conventions. Tokenization does not settle those choices in a uniform fashion. Thus, knowing that an input occupies a certain number of tokens does not reveal its exact word total. The source unit differs from the requested unit, and their numerical relationship varies with both content and counting rules. 3. The ordinary operation of language models also does not involve maintaining an exact counter while reading. A reliable count requires each qualifying item to be identified once, with a cumulative total updated after every identification. By contrast, modern language models transform representations through a predetermined sequence of computational layers, handling many positions simultaneously. When prompted to report a quantity, such a system generates a likely continuation on the basis of patterns and information represented in the context. Its standard architecture contains no explicit word-boundary counter whose stored value increases exactly once whenever another word is encountered. Internal representations can certainly carry information about relative position, overall extent, and approximate quantity, but that is not equivalent to a precise accumulator. Empirical work on language models also indicates that exact judgments involving positions and numerical quantities tend to become less reliable as input sequences become larger. A plausible estimate can therefore emerge without every word having been individually marked and added. 4. Actual responses reflect this architectural limitation. Reliability declines as the material expands. Once the input reaches several hundred words, an unsupported answer is better understood as an approximation, even when presented with certainty. Errors around a tenth or a fifth of the true total are not unusual, and larger discrepancies can occur. Performance varies among systems and prompts, but ordinary language models do not consistently deliver exact totals for long prose merely from having that prose in context. 5.
