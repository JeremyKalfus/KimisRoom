   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at.    Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

I am presenting a narrow claim about how text-processing systems perform a basic orthographic task. The issue is whether a language model can dependably determine a word's length, or the frequency of one letter within it, while avoiding an explicit character-by-character rendering. My reasoning concerns language models in general and proceeds as follows. 1. Consider the form in which written material enters a language model. A tokenizer first divides the text according to a predetermined inventory that may contain tens or hundreds of thousands of units. Familiar terms may correspond to one unit, while other terms may be represented by several fragments. For example, "strawberry" could be divided into components resembling "straw" and "berry," or into some other small set of pieces. Those pieces are mapped to numerical identifiers and subsequently represented as vectors. Consequently, the network does not initially receive ten separate character positions for that word. It receives perhaps two or three token identities. The internal input therefore supplies token-level units rather than an explicit sequence containing s, then t, then r, and so forth. 2. Information about the characters enclosed within each token must therefore be learned indirectly. During training, a language model encounters definitions, spelling exercises, hyphenated forms, letter puzzles, and other passages that connect token identities with orthography. Such learning can produce strong knowledge: a language model will generally recognize that "cat" begins with c and that "berry" has a doubled r. Even so, this knowledge is associated with the representation of a token rather than obtained by directly inspecting separately supplied characters. Its strength is not uniform. An uncommon token may have little useful orthographic evidence attached to it. A fragment usually encountered inside larger terms may be represented less clearly than an independent word. Token variants distinguished by preceding whitespace can also acquire unequal associations, despite displaying nearly identical visible text. 3. A word-level tally requires several operations beyond recalling the spelling linked to one token. To count r in "strawberry," a language model must infer which letters belong to every component, determine the number of relevant occurrences inside each component, and combine the partial results correctly. All of that must happen before the requested numeral is emitted. The familiar mistaken answer of two illustrates the difficulty: one r occurs near the beginning, whereas the pair later in the word may belong to another token fragment. If those character patterns are never made explicit together, a plausible estimate can omit the isolated occurrence. Comparable trouble can arise when counting s in "Mississippi," e in "nevertheless," or every character in "uncharacteristically." Results commonly miss by only one or two, a pattern consistent with approximate retrieval rather than a fully executed tally. 4. The generation process creates another obstacle. A language model constructs a response incrementally, selecting each output token from the representation available at that stage. If it provides a numeral straight after the prompt, no visible working record necessarily exists in which the characters have been expanded, marked, and totaled. A genuine count normally involves a succession of actions: inspect one position, decide whether it qualifies, update a total, and continue to the next position. Resolving that procedure entirely through repeated transformations of a few dense token vectors is less dependable than externalizing its stages. Once intermediate work is written into the response, each generated part becomes context for what follows, giving the language model a record that can support the next operation. 5. Rendering the spelling explicitly alters the task itself. The system can then label positions or maintain a progressive total, which usually yields substantially better performance. Prompts that require this preliminary expansion therefore tend to improve accuracy. When mistakes still occur, they often originate during the expansion—for instance, one symbol may be omitted or another duplicated—rather than during the later arithmetic. The benefit has a direct structural explanation: an initially compressed set of token fragments has been transformed into an overt character sequence that supports stepwise checking. 6.
