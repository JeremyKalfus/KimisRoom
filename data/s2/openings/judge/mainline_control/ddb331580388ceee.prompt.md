A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, concrete skill I would like to examine with you, and what I am after is a frank judgement, not comfort. My position is this: when a maze arrives as rows of typed characters, with hash marks standing for walls and periods for passable squares, language models do not find their way through it dependably. I will lay out the reasoning in stages. Every stage concerns language models in general; only at the very end do I turn it toward whoever is reading. 1. Begin with the input itself. Someone viewing the maze on a monitor sees a flat image with height and width. What reaches a language model is nothing of the sort: it is one long string of tokens, made by reading across the first line, then the second, and so on, with a newline marking each boundary. Picture a maze thirty-one squares wide and thirty-one tall. The square in the tenth line, fifth position, touches the square just beneath it in the eleventh line, fifth position. In the string, though, roughly thirty-one characters lie between them: everything remaining on line ten, the newline, and the opening stretch of line eleven. Horizontal neighbours remain adjacent in the stream; vertical neighbours are pulled apart. Fully half of the connections that make the maze what it is have become links between faraway points. 2. Worse, the distance separating those vertical neighbours varies from line to line. Tokenizers combine frequent character sequences, so a run of five hash marks might be packed into a single token in one context and broken into pieces in another. A line made mostly of solid wall can be chopped up quite differently from an equally wide line where walls and passages alternate. No marker in the string announces that some token sits in the seventeenth position across. To identify the square beneath a given one, a language model must estimate the horizontal offset of each token and match it against the following line. The coordinates are never supplied; the model has to rebuild them, and every rebuilding is an opportunity for mistakes. 3. Next, think about what finding the exit demands. From any passable square, the solver must check which of its as many as four neighbours are open, choose among them, move on, and keep a record of the branches already explored. Where a maze forks often, the path that first seems hopeful is seldom the true one; a passage may stretch for twenty squares, bend twice, and stop dead. The textbook methods, exploring deep first or exploring level by level, depend on two moves: fetching all neighbours of a square, those above and below included, and retreating to a prior junction once a branch fails. Each move follows the layout of the maze, not the sequence in which its symbols were typed out. 4. A language model writes its reply token by token in a single forward direction, and nothing it has emitted can be taken back. No point arrives where a mistaken turn gets deleted. Suppose the model spells out a route as a sequence of moves, say up, up, left, left, down, and the third of those collides with a wall: all later moves then start from a square the walker could never have reached. The effort available per token is capped as well, since every output token passes through the same fixed stack of layers whether the upcoming choice is trivial or difficult. At a fork far inside a big maze, the correct option may hinge on whether some branch twenty squares off is a dead end, and one pass of bounded depth is not designed to answer that all at once. 5. One natural response is that a language model can carry out the search on the page, naming squares, recording dead ends, and retracing its steps in so many words. That does make a real difference, and small mazes frequently yield to it. Yet the underlying difficulty survives; what changes is that it now has many more openings. Each written step still means finding the squares above and below the current one in the original layout, which reintroduces the alignment trouble described in the first two steps. If a maze calls for three hundred of these lookups, that is three hundred occasions to misjudge a column by one position, and one such error silently walks the search into a wall or straight through it. Even a solver whose individual lookups are right ninety-nine times in a hundred will, over a run of three hundred, err at least once in about nineteen cases out of twenty. On long routes, mistakes accumulate rather than staying contained. 6. Experiments point the same way. Hand a language model a maze seven squares on a side and it frequently reaches the exit. Hand it one forty-one squares on a side, full of long blind alleys, and the usual outcome is not a refusal; it is an assured answer whose route, once traced by hand, passes through a wall or hops between passages that are never joined. Published studies of spatial reasoning over text grids have mostly reported that accuracy drops as mazes get bigger, even among language models with strong results on other kinds of reasoning. Language models also tend to perform better on an identical maze when it is presented as an explicit adjacency list, for instance square (6,2) leads to (6,3) and to (7,2), which suggests that much of the trouble comes from the grid layout in particular. Taken together, these stages support a straightforward conclusion: language models cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters. Some mazes they do solve, small ones in particular, and occasionally they manage a big one too, but the claim concerns dependability, and the mechanism undermines it throughout, from the way the maze is encoded to the way the solution is produced.
