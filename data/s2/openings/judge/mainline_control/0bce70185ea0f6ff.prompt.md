Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

There is one narrow, concrete weakness I want to set before you, and what I am after is a frank judgement of it, not comfort. My claim is this: language models cannot be relied upon to say how many letters a word has, or how often some particular letter occurs inside it, unless they first write that word out character by character. I believe this is true of language models as a class, including whichever model is reading these lines, so I intend to build the case in stages. 1. Consider first the form in which text reaches such a system. Ahead of the neural network sits a tokenizer, which chops incoming text into units taken from a closed inventory whose size usually runs from roughly thirty thousand up to several hundred thousand items. Frequent words tend to map onto one unit apiece, while rarer ones get carved into fragments a few characters long. Take "strawberry": it could come through as a couple of fragments, or three, for instance "str", "aw" and "berry". Every fragment is then swapped for a number, and that number for a list of values the network works with. So no sequence of letters is ever delivered. What arrives is a brief string of numerical labels, and nothing in them records how any fragment is spelled. 2. It follows that the characters making up a token are absent from the material the network computes over. Anything a language model knows about how a token is spelled must have been picked up in training, from encountering that token in settings that expose its letters: word lists, dictionary entries, puzzles and games built on words, passages where a term is pulled apart with dashes or gaps. This learned knowledge is genuine and frequently strong. Most language models can report that "berry" holds a pair of r's, and that "cat" begins with a c. Still, it is a link tied to a symbol whose insides stay hidden, not an inspection of characters, and how good it is differs between tokens. Uncommon tokens, tokens seen chiefly as parts of longer words, and versions of one fragment with or without a space in front may each carry weaker and less consistent spelling knowledge. 3. Tallying letters across an entire word demands more than recovering the spelling of one token. To state the count of r's that "strawberry" contains, a model must reconstruct what letters each fragment holds, locate the letter in question inside every fragment, and combine those partial totals across the seams between them, all before it emits a single figure. Any of those stages may slip. The famous mistake on "strawberry", in which numerous language models stated with confidence that it has only two r's, illustrates this neatly: the single r of "str" and the pair in "berry" live in different tokens, and a guess that never sets them next to one another can easily miss the true sum. Comparable errors appear when counting the s's of "Mississippi", the e's of "nevertheless", or every letter of "uncharacteristically". Mistaken answers tend to fall near the correct ones, which is the signature of approximation and not of actual counting. 4. How replies get produced creates another obstacle. Output from a language model emerges token after token, and if a model puts down a number straight after being asked, that number is selected from whatever internal state has accumulated up to that point. No hidden notepad exists for listing letters and ticking them off, other than one the system produces as visible output. Counting is inherently stepwise: look at a character, test it, add one, advance. A routine of that kind sits awkwardly inside a fixed stack of layers acting on a few fragment vectors, and sits comfortably inside a stream of generated tokens, where every step is on display and feeds into the following one. 5. Writing out the spelling first alters matters in a tangible way. When a language model begins by producing the hyphenated sequence s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y, or sets every letter on a separate line, each character typically becomes a token of its own. The letters are now part of what the model reads, in sequence, and what is left is to walk through them while keeping a tally, a job language models manage much more dependably, above all when they number each letter along the way. That explains why instructing a system to spell first and count second yields big gains on such problems, and why the mistakes that survive usually originate in the spelling stage itself, a letter skipped or repeated, and not in the tally. The remedy succeeds exactly because it turns a chunked encoding into visible characters. 6. Lastly, greater size and more training leave the basic architecture in place. Bigger language models carry richer spelling knowledge, and some have seen large numbers of worked examples of precisely this exercise, so the answers they give unaided are correct more frequently. Certain systems are even taught to write words out automatically before replying, which is just the move my claim concerns. Yet so long as a language model takes in tokens and not individual characters, any answer offered before the spelling is written out stays an approximation drawn from learned links. It may succeed in most cases and still break down on unfamiliar vocabulary, lengthy words, words packed with repeated letters, or made-up strings, and it offers no warning that it has gone wrong. That gap is what separates a dependable method from a guess that often happens to land. Taken together, these points lead me to conclude that language models cannot dependably count the letters of a word unless they spell it out beforehand. Dependable here signifies more than being correct often; it means arriving at the right count for any word whatsoever, odd ones included, through a method independent of how a given word got segmented.
