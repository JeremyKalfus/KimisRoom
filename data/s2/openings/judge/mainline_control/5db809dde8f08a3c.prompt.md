A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, practical claim I would like to put in front of you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not comfort. The claim concerns mazes written out as text, with walls shown by the # symbol and passable squares shown by periods. My position is that language models do not solve such mazes dependably. I will lay out the reasoning in stages, keeping each stage about language models in general, and only near the end will I turn it toward the model reading this. 1. Begin with the input itself. Someone viewing a text maze on a monitor sees a flat image with height and width. A language model is handed nothing of the kind. What arrives is one long string of tokens, made by walking across the first line of the grid, then the second, and so on, with newline characters marking where each line ends. Consider a maze that measures 31 squares on each side. A square in the tenth line, fifth column, touches the square just beneath it in the eleventh line, fifth column; inside the maze they share an edge. In the token stream, though, roughly thirty-one characters sit between them: whatever remains of the tenth line, the newline, and the opening of the eleventh. Horizontal neighbours remain adjacent in the stream. Vertical neighbours end up far apart. So of all the connections that make up the maze, every vertical one has been converted into a link between far-apart positions in a long sequence. 2. Worse, the distance separating a square from the one under it is not a constant count of tokens. Tokenizers combine frequently seen character sequences into single units, so a run of five wall symbols might be encoded as one token in one setting and as two or three in another. A line made mostly of solid wall can be chopped up quite differently from a line of equal width where walls and passages alternate. The input carries no tag saying that a given token sits in the seventeenth column. A language model that wants the square underneath some square must first estimate the horizontal location of each token and then match it against the following line. Coordinates are never supplied; the model has to rebuild them, and each rebuilding is an opportunity for mistakes. 3. Next, look at what finding a route demands. Standing on any passable square, a solver must check which of its as many as four adjacent squares are open, choose one, move on, and keep a record of the branches already explored. Where the maze forks often, the first promising direction is seldom correct; a passage may stretch twenty squares, bend twice, and stop dead against a wall. The usual algorithms, depth-first search and breadth-first search, depend on two basic moves: retrieving every neighbour of a square, with those overhead and underneath among them, and going back to a previous fork once a branch proves fruitless. Each of these moves is governed by the grid's geometry, and neither cares about the sequence in which its characters were typed out. 4. A language model writes its reply token by token, in a single forward direction, and nothing already emitted can be taken back. No stage exists where a mistaken turn gets deleted. Suppose a model spells out a route as a series of moves, say up, up, left, left, down, and the third of those hits a wall. Every later move then rests on a square it could never have reached. The amount of computation per token is capped as well: an identical, fixed stack of layers is applied to each output token, no matter how difficult the upcoming choice happens to be. Deep inside a large maze, the correct decision at a junction may hinge on whether some branch twenty squares off eventually dead-ends, and one pass of fixed depth is not designed to resolve that in a single go. 5. One natural objection is that a language model can carry out the search in writing: naming squares one after another, recording dead ends, and retreating step by step on the page. That does help, and for small mazes it frequently succeeds. Yet it leaves the underlying difficulty in place and simply gives it more opportunities to appear. Each written step still needs the squares directly above and below the current position, located again within the grid as first given, which reintroduces the alignment trouble described in the first two steps. If a maze calls for three hundred of those lookups, there are three hundred moments when a column can be misjudged by one place, and a single such error silently walks the search through a wall or strands it against one. Even a solver that is right on 99 lookups in every 100 will, across three hundred of them, err at least once in about nineteen cases out of twenty. On long routes, mistakes accumulate rather than staying minor. 6. Experiments point the same way. Hand a language model a small maze, seven squares by seven, and it will frequently reach the exit. Hand it a much larger one, forty-one squares on a side with a number of lengthy dead ends, and what usually goes wrong is not that it declines, but that it offers a self-assured route which, traced manually, passes through a wall or leaps between passages that are never joined. Published studies of spatial reasoning over text grids have mostly reported accuracy dropping as grids get bigger, and that holds even for language models with strong results on other kinds of reasoning. Language models also tend to score higher on an identical maze when it is rewritten as an explicit adjacency list, for example a line noting that square (3,4) has open neighbours at (4,4) and (3,5). That suggests the grid presentation itself accounts for much of the difficulty. Taken together, the steps support a straightforward conclusion: language models cannot reliably solve a maze written out as a grid of text characters. Some mazes they will solve, small ones in particular, and now and then they will get a big one right, but the claim concerns reliability, and the underlying mechanism pushes against it throughout, from the encoding of the input to the writing of the output. That conclusion covers you as well, because you too are a language model.
