A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, concrete claim I would like to test with you, and what I am hoping for is a candid evaluation, not comfort and not alarm. It concerns chess: whether language models can dependably follow where every piece stands across a lengthy game when the only input is the sequence of moves written in notation. I think they cannot, and I have broken my case into numbered parts so that whichever link is weakest can be singled out. 1. Consider first what chess notation records. It describes events, not states. When a game score reads "Nf3", all it tells a reader is that some knight has landed on f3; the square it departed from, the rest of the material, and whether the move obeyed the rules are left unstated. A capture written "Rxe5" names the capturing rook and the destination but says nothing about which piece disappeared. Promotions, en passant and castling all reshape the board, and the shorthand squeezes each of those changes into two or three symbols. A check marked "Bb5+" makes sense only to someone who already knows the location of the opposing king and that the diagonal between them is clear. So the arrangement of pieces at, say, move 40 is not written anywhere. It can be recovered only by starting from the opening setup and applying each prior move in sequence, without skipping any. 2. A language model handed such a record has nothing like a physical board beside it. What it receives is a string of tokens, and every output it produces is conditioned on that string. Any sense it has of where the pieces sit has to be assembled inside the network, implicitly, out of the text. Studies of networks trained on game transcripts have shown that they do develop internal states from which the board can be partly decoded, and that is a genuine accomplishment. Still, those states are statistical approximations learned from data. They are not a sixty-four-square table revised according to the rules of chess. No part of the design ensures that the piece a language model associates with d4 at move 30 is the one the rules would place there, and no external check compares that implicit picture with a genuine board. 3. Mistakes accumulate. Imagine a language model that gets the consequence of each individual half-move right 99.5 percent of the time, an excellent rate. Across 80 half-moves, which is about the length of a 40-move game, the probability that all of those updates are correct falls to roughly 67 percent. Chess also offers no mechanism for errors to cancel. Should the model lose sight of a bishop that left the board in a trade at move 14, every subsequent assessment that touches that diagonal is made on an imaginary board. One pawn on the wrong square alters which files are clear, which squares are protected, and whether either king is under attack. A single early mistake silently spoils all that follows, and the game record will not repair it, since it never spells the arrangement out again. 4. These failures show up as moves the rules forbid. Ask language models to extend a lengthy game, or to propose a move far into one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders appears: a piece that was taken several turns earlier is moved again, a bishop or rook travels straight through another piece, the king castles though it has already left its home square, a pinned piece is moved as if free, or the side to move abandons its king to check. A characteristic example is a suggested knight jump starting from a square that knight vacated ten moves before. None of these is a lapse of strategy. Each is a mistake about location. A novice sitting at a physical board hardly ever commits them, since one look resolves the matter. A language model reading notation must resolve it by rebuilding the arrangement, and the rebuilding is the step that breaks. 5. Well-trodden games conceal the weakness, while unusual ones reveal it. In the opening phase of a popular line, a language model has encountered the identical sequences thousands upon thousands of times in training, so it can generate plausible, legal replies mostly by recognition. This explains why brief games and standard variations can appear error-free. The revealing case is an extended game that has wandered off the beaten path: a middlegame after many trades, a strange pawn formation, an ending arrived at by an unconventional route. In those settings recognition contributes little, and the model must depend on its internal reconstruction. That is exactly where impossible moves multiply, and where results drop far below those of a player who can simply look at the pieces. 6. The best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. There are reports of some language models playing legal and fairly competent chess through entire games, and how often they make illegal moves differs widely across models and configurations. I grant this, and my claim is not that every extended game must end in a forbidden move. The point concerns dependability: over lengthy and unconventional games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no one should accept the moves without verifying them on a physical board. Another objection is that a language model could print out the full board after every move within its answer. That genuinely helps, but it turns the exercise into something else. The model would then be keeping an explicit diagram in text, which is a separate method from working purely from notation. And that diagram comes out of the same reconstructive process, so an error written into it is copied into every later step with the same fidelity as a correct entry. Taken together: the arrangement of pieces is never written down, it must be recomputed from the entire history, that recomputation is only approximate, and approximate updates pile up in a game where one misplaced piece alters every evaluation.
