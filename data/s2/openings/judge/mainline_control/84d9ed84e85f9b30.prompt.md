A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, practical question I'd like to work through with you, and I care more about an accurate answer than a comforting one. My position is that tallying up the words of a lengthy text is something language models do poorly. I mean to lay out why in stages, grounding each stage in the mechanics of how such systems handle written input, and only once the general case is made will I turn it toward the model reading this. 1. A language model never encounters words as such. Any text it receives is first chopped into tokens, pieces taken from a fixed inventory that was designed to compress writing economically, not to respect grammar or sense. Frequent short words usually become one token each, and that token often absorbs the preceding space. Rarer or longer words get divided up, so a word like "unbelievably" could reach the system as several chunks, perhaps "un" plus "believ" plus "ably", with the exact split varying by inventory. Digits, punctuation and stretches of blank space are handled as separate pieces or glued onto adjacent material, and different inventories do that gluing differently. What the system actually operates on, then, is a stream of tokens whose edges coincide with word edges only part of the time. 2. Since tokens and words do not pair off neatly, no fixed formula turns one into the other. People quote approximations, for instance that each token averages roughly 0.75 English words in ordinary prose, yet that figure moves a good deal with content. Dense technical material packed with compound terms, names and numbers yields far more tokens for each word than simple storytelling does, and writing in languages other than English moves the figure again. Then there is a separate problem. Strings like "don't", "e.g.", "3.14", "state-of-the-art" or a web link are ones that human readers handle inconsistently, treating each as a single word on some occasions and as multiple words on others, and the tokens give no steady hint about which reading applies. The upshot is that a language model has no way to read off the length of its input and convert that into the number of words. Its length is recorded in tokens, which is the wrong measure, and the rate of exchange between tokens and words differs with every text it meets. 3. A language model also keeps no running total while it works through text. Genuine counting is serial: whoever does it visits each item in turn, increments by one, and holds the sum until the next item arrives, so that the end figure includes each item once and only once. The architecture of a language model works otherwise. Its input passes through a set number of layers, with positions mostly treated simultaneously instead of in sequence, and a question about length is answered the same way as any other prompt, by producing whichever continuation looks most likely given the context. No internal counter ticks upward each time a word ends. Whatever signals inside the network track length do so only loosely, and research on these systems has reported that their sense of where things sit and how many there are weakens as inputs get longer. 4. This is easy to observe. Hand a language model a text of around 750 words and request the total, and it will typically reply with a firm-sounding number, perhaps 690, 810 or 830. Put the same request to it in a new conversation and a different figure may come back. Asking a language model for a text of precisely 500 words shows the same weakness in reverse: what comes out often runs anywhere from about 400 to 600. Very short sentences, of a dozen words or so, are another story; there the items are few and the pattern simple, so a language model frequently answers correctly. The trouble grows with length. For texts running to hundreds of words, whatever figure a language model gives is a guess dressed up as a tally, and it is frequently far off, by a tenth or a fifth in many cases and sometimes by more. 5. One fair reply deserves an answer instead of being brushed aside. In principle a language model could reproduce the text with a numeral attached to every word, and doing so does generally make the result more accurate. That, however, is a different job. The counting is now distributed over hundreds of output steps, and at any of them the model might drop a word, duplicate one, or treat a hyphenated compound differently than it did earlier; one such error propagates straight into the final figure. Besides, this is seldom what happens when someone just wants a text's length in words. Passing the text to an external counting tool also works, but in that case the tool has done the counting and the language model has merely relayed it. What I am claiming concerns the typical situation: a language model takes in a long text and reports its length in words, which is both the usual request and the usual response. Put these points side by side and the result is straightforward. Language models cannot accurately count the words in a long passage. The units they receive are not words, nothing inside them adds words up singly, and the numbers they offer for texts of many hundreds of words are guesses wearing the clothes of exact counts.
