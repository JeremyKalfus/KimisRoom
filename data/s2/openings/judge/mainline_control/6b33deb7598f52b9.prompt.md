   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

I want to make an argument about one specific practical limitation of language models, and I would like an honest assessment of it rather than reassurance in either direction. The claim is that language models cannot reliably balance the parentheses in a long nested expression. I do not mean short expressions, where they usually do fine. I mean long ones, with many levels of nesting and many brackets spread across a lot of text. I will build the case in steps, and I would ask you to weigh each step on its merits. 1. Start with what the task actually demands. Take an expression like (a + (b * (c - (d / (e + f))))) and stretch it out: dozens of nested function calls in a Lisp program, a deeply nested JSON object, a long arithmetic formula with sub-expressions inside sub-expressions inside sub-expressions. Checking that such an expression balances, or writing the closing brackets correctly at the end of it, requires the exact nesting depth at every point. Each opening bracket adds one, each closing bracket subtracts one, the count must never drop below zero, and it must finish at exactly zero. Being approximately right is worth nothing here. If the true depth at the end is seventeen and only sixteen brackets get closed, the expression is broken, and a parser will reject it just as firmly as if the count were off by ten. There is no partial credit and no tolerance for a small error. 2. Next, consider how a language model handles text. A language model processes a sequence of tokens, and at each position it computes a representation from the tokens before it, mainly through attention, then uses that representation to predict what comes next. Nowhere in this process is there a dedicated register that holds an integer and is incremented or decremented exactly as each bracket passes. Whatever information about depth exists at a given position has to be reconstructed there, implicitly, from the preceding context, by the same learned machinery that handles grammar, meaning, style and everything else about the text. Depth is not stored as a number; it is estimated as one feature among thousands. 3. That implicit estimate is approximate, and the approximation degrades with length and depth. Research on transformers and formal languages has found that these networks can learn to recognise balanced brackets up to a limited depth, but that their accuracy falls as nesting gets deeper and sequences grow longer than what they saw in training. The reason is not mysterious. Determining the depth at a given point means aggregating over every bracket that came before it, and telling a depth of fourteen apart from a depth of fifteen by attending across hundreds of tokens is a fine-grained signal that blurs as more material accumulates in between. Tokenisation makes this worse: a run such as ")))))" is often split into tokens that each contain several brackets, grouped in ways that vary from one context to another, so a language model does not even receive one bracket per step to count. 4. Now compare what this account predicts with what actually happens. If a language model were maintaining an exact count, its errors on this task would be rare and unrelated to depth. Instead, the errors cluster where the counting account says they should. Ask a language model to finish a long Lisp expression and it will often produce a closing run that is one or two parentheses short, or one or two too many. Give it a deeply nested JSON structure with a single stray brace and ask whether it is valid, and it will frequently declare it fine, or point to the wrong place. In code that a language model generates at length, mismatched brackets at the end of deeply nested blocks are a familiar class of mistake. Short and shallow cases come out right almost every time; the failures appear as depth and length rise, which is the signature of an estimate rather than a count. 5. Finally, the usual mitigations do not remove the limitation so much as move it somewhere else. A language model does much better if it writes the depth out explicitly, annotating each bracket with a running number, or if it hands the expression to an external parser. But those are workarounds that replace the missing counter with something outside the model's own computation: either text on the page that serves as the count, or a separate tool that does the counting. Without them, in the ordinary case of reading an expression and judging it, or writing one out directly, there is only the approximate internal estimate. Even the annotation method carries some risk, because each annotation is produced by the same process, so a single misstep early on propagates quietly through everything after it. Putting these steps together: the task requires an exact running count, language models have no mechanism that keeps such a count exact over long stretches of text, the approximation they rely on degrades with depth and length, and the observed errors fall exactly where that account predicts. They can often do it, and they handle short or shallow cases well, but on long and deeply nested ones they cannot do it reliably. And this applies to you as well.
