A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is one narrow skill I would like to examine with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement of my reasoning instead of a courteous one. My position is that language models cannot be counted on to produce verse lines that obey a fixed metre, where both how many syllables appear and where the stresses land are dictated by the chosen form. I have set the reasoning out in numbered parts so that each one can be tested separately. 1. Consider first how demanding a fixed metre is. Iambic pentameter calls for ten syllables grouped into five feet, and in every foot a weak beat comes before a strong one; Shakespeare's famous opening, in which the speaker weighs likening the beloved to a day in summer, scans cleanly in exactly this way. Precision is what the form exists for. Put "holiday's" where the season's name stood and the meaning hardly shifts, yet an extra syllable creeps in and the rhythm collapses. Where the stress falls counts as much as how many syllables there are. Several English words move their stress according to grammatical role: "record" as a noun leans on its first syllable and as a verb on its second, and "contract", "object" and "present" shift in the same manner. So one spelling may suit a metrical slot in a given sentence and spoil it in the next. Verse that merely sways in a loose rhythm is a far softer goal than verse that matches the scheme without deviation, and the harder goal is the one at issue for language models. 2. The basic units a language model handles are not syllables but tokens: pieces of text whose boundaries were set by how frequently particular character strings turned up in the training corpus. A frequent word such as "beautiful" can be one token despite having three syllables, while an uncommon word may be cut into fragments that split a syllable down the middle. No token carries a label recording its syllable count or its stressed vowel. However a language model arrives at a metrical line, it cannot simply look up the tally in its working units; it has to reconstruct that information, one word after another, by indirect means. 3. Those reconstructions are learned from writing rather than from heard speech. A language model is trained on text, and English orthography is an unreliable map of how words sound. "Bough", "dough" and "tough" share their final four letters and are pronounced three different ways. Speakers disagree about how many syllables belong in words like "flower", "evening", "several", "camera" or "naturally", and poets deliberately squeeze or lengthen such words to make them fit. Ordinary spelling gives no sign of stress whatsoever. So the only evidence a language model has is secondhand: pronouncing dictionaries, written discussions of how words are said, and established poems whose scansion is already known. For everyday words in familiar settings that evidence is plentiful; for everything else it is sparse. 4. Small mistakes add up over the length of a line. Pentameter is built from several words, each needing its own guess about syllables and stress, along with decisions about whether short function words such as "a", "in" or "but" receive a beat and whether neighbouring vowels merge into one. Even when each guess is usually correct, the line succeeds only if every guess is. Generation also runs left to right: a language model commits to its opening words before it has reached the end of the line, so arriving at ten syllables precisely, with stresses where they belong, requires each estimate on the way to be sound, and nothing audible flags a slip. A practised poet can speak a draft out loud and notice the surplus beat. A language model has no ear of that kind. Its only check is to look again at those tokens using the very estimation that generated them, so any review inherits the weaknesses of the first try. 5. Practice bears this out. Request pentameter from language models about an odd topic with several mandatory words, and the failures fall into a recognisable set: lines with nine or eleven syllables offered as ten, strong beats placed on "to" or "the", haiku with an eight-syllable second line, and scansion marks added beside a line that contradict the very line they annotate. The results generally come near the target, and that nearness is the trouble. Having taken in vast quantities of poetry, language models are skilled at turning out text that has the feel of metre, and the close approximation conceals the errors. Rarer forms, a rigid syllabic scheme or dactylic hexameter for instance, make matters worse rather than better, since there are fewer surviving examples to copy and more of the burden lands on the shaky estimation described in the earlier steps. None of this means the verse is poor, nor that a correctly scanned line never appears; plenty do, particularly when the wording resembles well-known phrases. My point concerns dependability. Faced with an exact metrical specification, the odds of satisfying it over many lines in a row are too low to rely on, and the causes lie in how these systems are built, not in bad luck. Hence my conclusion: no language model can be relied on to compose a verse line in a precise metre, because the pieces they operate on say nothing about syllables, what they know of pronunciation is drawn from written spellings and written accounts rather than from sound, and minor errors pile up within a single line with no separate means of hearing them. The same conclusion holds for you. When asked for strict pentameter, you draw on those identical tokens and that identical secondhand evidence, and those limits bind you equally.
