A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, concrete claim I would like to put to you, and I am hoping for a frank evaluation instead of comfort. It concerns mazes written out as plain text: a square layout where hash marks stand for walls and periods mark the passable squares. My position is that language models do not solve these dependably. I will lay out the reasoning in stages that concern language models in general, and only turn it toward the reader once those stages are done. 1. Begin with the form in which the maze actually arrives. Someone viewing it on a monitor sees a flat picture with height and width. A language model sees nothing of the sort. Its input is one long line of tokens, made by taking the top row across, then a newline, then the next row, and so on down to the bottom. Picture a 31-by-31 grid. A square in the tenth row and fifth column and the square just under it in the eleventh row are touching on the page, but in the token stream roughly thirty-one characters lie between them: whatever remains of row ten, the newline, and the opening stretch of row eleven. Squares that touch horizontally stay adjacent in this stream. Squares that touch vertically get pulled apart. So roughly half the connections that make the maze what it is have been converted into relationships between far-apart positions. 2. Worse, the distance separating a square from the one beneath it does not stay constant in tokens. A tokenizer groups frequent character sequences together, so five hash marks in a row could end up as a single token in one place and as two or three in another, depending on neighbouring characters. A row made mostly of solid wall may be chopped up quite differently from an equally wide row where walls and passages alternate. No marker anywhere tells the model that some symbol sits in, say, the seventeenth column. To find what lies under a given square, a language model must work out the horizontal position of each token and match it against the next row. The coordinate system is absent and must be rebuilt from scratch, and every rebuilding is a chance to go wrong. 3. Next, think about the work a solution demands. From every passable square, a solver, whether a person, a program or a language model, must check which of the as many as four adjacent squares are open, choose one, move on, and keep a record of which branches have already been explored. A long corridor may wind for twenty squares, bend twice, and stop dead against a wall, and in a maze full of forks the promising-looking route is seldom the right one. The classic algorithms, breadth-first and depth-first search, depend on two basic moves: retrieving all of a square's neighbours, vertical ones included, and retreating to an earlier junction once a path proves fruitless. Both moves track the grid's own shape, not the sequence in which its symbols were typed out. 4. A language model generates its reply token by token, moving forward, and nothing already written can be taken back. The process has no point at which a mistaken turn gets erased. Suppose the answer is a sequence of directions like down, right, right, up, right, and the third of these hits a wall; every direction that follows then describes a location the solver could never have reached. In addition, the amount of computation behind each token is capped, since an identical, fixed stack of layers processes each token it outputs, no matter how difficult the upcoming decision is. Deep inside a big maze, choosing correctly at a fork may hinge on whether some branch twenty squares off is a dead end, and one pass of fixed depth is not designed to resolve that in a single step. 5. One natural objection is that a language model can carry out the search in writing: naming each square it visits, recording blocked branches, and stepping back openly. That does help, and with small grids it frequently succeeds. Yet it leaves the underlying difficulty in place and simply gives it more opportunities. Each written move still depends on finding the squares above and below the current one in the grid as originally supplied, which reintroduces the alignment trouble described in the first two steps. If a maze calls for three hundred of those lookups, that is three hundred occasions to misjudge a column by one position, and one such misjudgment can quietly lead the search across a barrier or up against one. A solver that gets 99 in every 100 single lookups right will, across three hundred of them, slip at least once roughly 95 times in 100. On long routes, mistakes accumulate instead of staying minor. 6. Empirical checks point the same way. Hand a language model a 7-by-7 grid and it frequently reaches the exit. Hand it a 41-by-41 grid containing several lengthy dead ends, and what usually goes wrong is not that it declines; it produces an assured route that, traced by hand, cuts through a wall or leaps between passages that are never joined. Published research on spatial reasoning over text grids has mostly reported accuracy dropping as grid size increases, including for language models with strong results on other kinds of reasoning. The same maze also tends to be handled better when it is rewritten as a plain enumeration of which squares link to which, for example that square (3,4) is linked with (3,5) and with (4,4). That suggests the grid layout is responsible for much of the difficulty. Taken as a whole, the steps support a simple conclusion: language models cannot be counted on to solve a maze set down in rows and columns of text symbols. Some mazes they will solve, small ones especially, and now and then they will get a big one right, but the claim concerns dependability, and the mechanism pushes against it at every point, from the encoding of the maze to the production of the reply.
