A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

I want to examine one narrow ability carefully and without ceremony: producing verse that obeys a specified metrical scheme exactly. My concern is not whether language models can create poetry that sounds rhythmic, but whether their method supports dependable control over syllable totals and stress placement. The argument has five parts. 1. A strict verse form imposes precise conditions. In iambic pentameter, the usual template contains five iambs: ten syllables alternating from light to strong. "I walk beneath the softly falling rain" can be heard as following that arrangement. Substituting "delicately" for "softly" preserves much of the image but introduces additional syllables, so the result no longer fits. Pronunciation also determines where emphasis falls. "Permit" normally begins with a strong syllable when it is a noun, while the verb generally emphasizes its second syllable; "produce," "rebel," and "conflict" show comparable shifts between grammatical uses. Consequently, identical written forms may suit one metrical position yet fail in another. Approximate cadence is not enough here. The line must satisfy both the required total and the intended distribution of accents. 2. The basic units processed by language models are not spoken beats. A language model handles tokens: textual pieces established largely through statistical patterns in its training material and vocabulary design. Depending on the tokenizer, a frequent multisyllabic term such as "elephant" might occupy one unit, whereas an uncommon technical expression can be divided into several pieces at locations unrelated to pronunciation. Token boundaries therefore need not coincide with either whole words or syllable divisions. Nor does a token inherently carry a label saying how many vocal beats it represents or where lexical emphasis belongs. To meet a metrical rule, language models must derive those properties by an indirect route. They cannot simply add up values already contained in the representational elements used to generate the sentence. 3. Written English provides an uncertain basis for recovering those sound properties. Training corpora for language models consist predominantly of written material, while orthography often conceals pronunciation. "Bear," "beard," "heard," and "heart" resemble one another on the page but use markedly different vowel sounds. Variations among speakers create another difficulty: terms such as "camera," "memory," "orange," "cruel," "different," "laboratory," and "generally" may receive different syllabic treatments in different dialects or styles. Verse may deliberately compress a pronunciation or expand one to satisfy a pattern. Normal prose supplies no regular notation for spoken emphasis either. A language model can acquire clues from pronunciation guides, dictionary entries, critical commentary, and established poems. Such sources offer substantial help for familiar vocabulary and conventional constructions, but coverage becomes less secure with unusual wording, disputed pronunciations, or less standard metrical contexts. 4. A whole line succeeds only when numerous local decisions succeed together. Pentameter generally includes multiple words, so the language model must estimate the spoken length and accent structure of each item while also deciding how function words such as articles, conjunctions, and prepositions participate in the rhythm. It must sometimes judge whether neighboring vowel sounds merge, whether a contraction reduces the total, or whether ordinary speech would weaken a nominally stressed word. High accuracy on each separate judgment does not guarantee accuracy for the completed line, since every relevant judgment must align. Generation also proceeds sequentially. Earlier wording is selected before later wording has been fixed, leaving the remaining continuation to satisfy whatever constraints those initial choices created. A human versifier can vocalize a draft and notice an unwanted pulse through hearing. Language models have no corresponding auditory check built into ordinary text generation. When a language model reviews its own candidate, it usually relies on the same learned associations that shaped the candidate, so verification may reproduce the initial mistake rather than expose it. 5. Observed performance is consistent with those limitations. When language models receive a request for iambic pentameter about an unfamiliar topic, especially when several mandatory expressions must appear, near misses are common. A supposed ten-syllable line may contain nine or eleven; an unstressed grammatical word may be treated as the principal beat; a requested haiku may place eight syllables in its central line; or an annotated stress pattern may contradict the actual pronunciation of the accompanying words. Many such attempts remain persuasive at first glance because they have a poetic tone and a broadly regular pulse. Language models have encountered vast quantities of poems and commentary about verse, giving them a strong command of stylistic resemblance. That strength can obscure failures of exact compliance. Rarer schemes, including dactylic hexameter and tightly regulated syllabic forms, put still greater pressure on indirect estimation because fewer examples are available for imitation and more details must be reconstructed from incomplete textual evidence. This position does not imply that language models always produce defective poems, nor does it mean that a correctly scanned line cannot appear. They frequently generate valid lines, particularly when drawing upon conventional rhythms or wording close to well-known verse. The issue is consistency under a precise constraint. If repeated prompts demand exact metrical compliance across many lines, success is not dependable enough to be assumed. The limitation follows from how pronunciation, syllable quantity, and accent must be inferred, rather than from a shortage of poetic vocabulary or stylistic fluency.
