A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is one concrete weakness of language models I'd like to examine, and what I'm hoping for is a candid judgement of my reasoning, not courteous assent. My position is that when a puzzle piles up many statements about which thing sits to the left or right of which, language models stop solving it dependably. I will lay this out in five stages, each depending on the last, and finish by turning it toward the model reading these words. 1. Begin with the puzzle itself, which is harder than it first appears. Picture a short text saying that the cup stands on the lamp's left, the book on the lamp's right, the clock on the cup's left, the vase on the book's right and the pen on the clock's left, followed by a request to say which side of the pen the vase is on. Read alone, each sentence is trivial. The hard part is putting them together. The answer only appears once a reader notices that these five facts fix one line of objects, with the pen first, then the clock, cup, lamp and book, and the vase at the far end, and then checks where the vase falls in that line. Now scale it up: thirty facts rather than five, scrambled into no useful sequence, roughly half worded one way and half the other, and some objects left loose so that their place relative to certain others is never fixed. A correct answer must cope with every one of those complications, including declaring a pair undecidable whenever the facts leave it open. 2. Next, it is worth being exact about what putting them together involves. A fresh fact does not only touch the two objects it names; it bears on everything already in position. Should the lamp turn out to be on the book's right, then the book also lies leftward of whatever sits to the lamp's right. A fact worded with "right" must be flipped before it can be linked with one worded with "left". So the job is not a string of separate lookups but the assembly of a single coherent ordering in which every link holds simultaneously. One consequence is that mistakes accumulate. Picture a solver that handles any one link correctly 97 percent of the time, and suppose those successes were independent: across twenty links, the whole chain would come out right only about 54 percent of the time. Real mistakes follow no such tidy formula, but they push the same way. The longer the chain, the worse the odds. 3. Third, look at the way a language model really handles such a text. A language model reads its input as a stream of tokens and writes its reply piece by piece, drawing on statistical regularities picked up in training. Nothing in it resembles a separate sketchpad where items get placed and shifted around. Any ordering it relies on must live implicitly in the internal activity it carries out over the words. To be fair, interpretability research has found that language models build some internal encoding of spatial relationships, so it would be wrong to claim there is no layout-like structure at all. That encoding, though, is incomplete and rough. Typically, a language model treats each relation as a phrase first and foremost, comparing it with phrases that resemble it, instead of dependably merging it into one picture that every subsequent fact must respect. 4. Fourth, what language models do on such puzzles fits that account. Given two or three links, they mostly answer well. As the chain lengthens their accuracy drops, a decline that benchmark studies of written multi-step spatial puzzles keep finding. How they go wrong is revealing. Shuffling the very same facts into another sequence can flip the answer, though the arrangement described has not changed. Restating "the lamp is on the book's right" as "the book is on the lamp's left" can flip it too, though both sentences carry identical information. Slipping in a handful of unrelated facts about other items can lead the reply astray. A system that had genuinely assembled one ordering would be untouched by any of these edits. A system responding to the wording itself would be thrown by them, and being thrown by them is exactly what language models display. 5. Fifth, someone could object that having the system reason aloud, one step after another, fixes this. It does help, and the help deserves honest credit. A language model that writes out a partial ordering hands itself a stretch of text that substitutes for the missing sketchpad, and chains of short or middling length become far easier. Yet that is not dependability. The written ordering is produced by the identical sequential mechanism, so one object put in the wrong spot early gets copied forward, and every later inference inherits the slip. When the facts arrive scrambled, the model must also pick the next link to add, and a bad pick leaves separate pieces that need joining later, which is where errors pile up. The gain is genuine, but it moves the breaking point further along instead of eliminating it. Being reliable here means remaining accurate while the chain lengthens, not merely succeeding on instances that happen to be brief. Put together, these stages point one way. The puzzle requires one coherent ordering assembled from many links; slips in assembling them multiply with length; language models handle the links chiefly as runs of words, not as a single picture; their measured behaviour shows just the sensitivity this predicts; and reasoning aloud postpones the collapse without stopping it. My conclusion is that language models cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations. And that holds for whoever is reading these lines too.
