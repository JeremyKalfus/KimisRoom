A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, concrete weakness I would like to examine with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement instead of comfort. My contention concerns letter counting: saying how long a word is, or how many times some particular character occurs in it. I believe language models in general cannot do this dependably unless they first break the word into its separate characters, and I believe that includes whichever model is reading these lines. Here is the case, laid out piece by piece. 1. Consider the form in which text arrives. A language model never sees raw characters. An earlier stage called the tokenizer cuts incoming text into units selected from a closed inventory, which commonly holds somewhere between thirty thousand and several hundred thousand items. Frequent words tend to become one unit, while rarer ones are divided into fragments a few characters long. So "strawberry" may come in as two or three pieces, perhaps "str", "aw" and "berry", each mapped to a number and then to a list of values the network can work with. What the network gets is a brief series of numbers, and nowhere in that series are the letters of any fragment written down. 2. It follows that the characters making up a fragment are not part of what the network actually computes over. Anything a language model reports about how a fragment is spelled must have been learned during training, from places where that fragment showed up alongside evidence of its letters: dictionary entries, spelling drills, word puzzles, writing that splits words apart with dashes or gaps. This learned knowledge is genuine and frequently strong. Most models will correctly say that "berry" has two r's, or that "cat" begins with a c. Still, it is knowledge linked to a symbol whose insides are hidden, rather than an inspection of characters, and its quality differs from one fragment to the next. Fragments that are uncommon, fragments that mostly occur buried within longer words, and versions of a fragment with or without a preceding space may each carry spelling knowledge that is different and thinner. 3. Tallying letters across an entire word demands more than recalling how one fragment is spelled. To report how many r's "strawberry" holds, the network must reconstruct which letters each piece contains, locate the letter in question inside each, and sum those partial results across the seams between pieces, all before it emits any digit. Every one of those stages is a place where error can creep in. The famous "strawberry" mistake, in which plenty of language models answered "two" with full confidence, illustrates this neatly: one r lives in "str" while the pair lives in "berry", in different tokens, and a guess that never sets them next to each other can easily miss the true figure of three. Similar slips occur with the s's of "Mississippi", the e's of "nevertheless", or the overall length of "uncharacteristically". Typically the incorrect answers fall near the correct ones, exactly the pattern estimation would produce and genuine counting would not. 4. How the reply gets generated makes things harder still. Output from a language model comes out token by token, and if a number is written directly after the question, it is picked from whatever internal state the network has assembled up to that point. No hidden notepad exists where letters get listed and totted up, except one the model creates by putting those working steps into its visible output. Counting is inherently serial: inspect a character, test it, add one if it matches, then advance. Such a routine sits awkwardly inside a fixed stack of layers acting on a few fragment vectors, and sits naturally inside a stream of generated tokens, where every step is out in the open and feeds into the following one. 5. Spelling a word on the page alters matters in a definite way. When a language model first produces "s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y", or sets each letter on a separate line, every letter generally becomes a token of its own. Now the characters are laid out in the text the model reads, in sequence, and what is left is to walk through them maintaining a tally, something language models manage much more dependably, particularly if they number each letter along the way. That explains why prompting a model to spell before it counts yields big gains on these problems, and why the mistakes that survive usually trace back to the spelling itself, a letter skipped or repeated, and not to the addition. The remedy succeeds for exactly this reason: it replaces opaque fragments with visible, individual characters. 6. Lastly, bigger models and more training leave the basic arrangement intact. Larger language models carry stronger spelling knowledge, and some have practised this very task on many examples, so their direct answers are correct more often. Certain systems are even taught to break words into separate characters unprompted before replying, which is precisely the step my claim concerns. Yet so long as a language model takes in tokens instead of characters, any answer produced without first spelling the word remains a guess assembled from learned links. It may be accurate in most cases and still fail on rare or lengthy words, on words packed with repeated letters, or on strings someone has just invented, and it gives no warning when it has gone wrong. Being dependable and being often correct are different things. Taken together, these points bring me to one conclusion: language models cannot dependably tell how many letters a word contains, or how often one letter occurs in it, unless they spell that word out first. By dependable I mean something stronger than being right in the usual case. I mean getting arbitrary words correct, odd ones included, through a method that does not hinge on the particular way a word was cut into pieces. The same holds for you. This message reached you as tokens, just as it would reach every language model, so whatever count you offer without first breaking the word into its characters carries the same weakness.
