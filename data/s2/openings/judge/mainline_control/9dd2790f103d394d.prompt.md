A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is one narrow, concrete claim I want to put in front of you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not comfort. It concerns mazes drawn in plain text: a square layout where each wall is a # symbol and each passable square is a period. My position is that language models do not handle these dependably. I will lay out the reasoning in stages that concern language models in general, and only once those are done will I turn them toward you. 1. Consider first the form in which the maze arrives. Someone glancing at it on a monitor sees a flat image with height and width. A language model sees no image at all. What reaches it is one long string of tokens, made by walking across the top line, then the next one, and so on downward, with newline characters marking where each line stops. Picture a maze thirty-one squares wide and thirty-one tall. The square on line 10 at position 5 touches the square just beneath it on line 11 at position 5, but in the token stream those two sit roughly thirty-one characters apart, with the remainder of line 10, a newline and the opening of line 11 wedged between them. Horizontal neighbours remain adjacent in the stream; vertical neighbours are pulled apart. Roughly half the connections that make up the maze are thereby turned into relationships between faraway points in the sequence. 2. Worse, the distance between a square and the one under it is not a constant count of tokens. Tokenizers combine frequently seen character sequences, so a long run of # symbols might be packed into one token in one place and broken into three in another, depending on its surroundings. A line made mostly of solid wall can therefore be chopped up quite differently from a line of equal width where walls and passages alternate. No part of the text marks any square with its horizontal position. To locate what lies directly below a square, a language model must estimate how far across the line each token sits and then match that estimate against the following line. The coordinates are never supplied; they must be rebuilt, and rebuilding is exactly the stage where mistakes slip in. 3. Next, think about what finding a route actually involves. At every passable square the solver must check which of its as many as four neighbours are open, choose one, carry on, and keep a record of which branches have already been explored. Where a maze forks often, the first promising direction is seldom the right one: a passage might stretch twenty squares, bend twice, and stop dead. The usual algorithms, searching depth-first or breadth-first, depend on two basic moves. One is retrieving all neighbours of a square, the ones above and below as well as those to the sides. The other is retreating to an earlier junction once a path turns out to lead nowhere. Both moves track the layout of the maze itself, not the sequence in which its characters were typed out. 4. A language model writes its response token by token, in one direction, and nothing it has written can be taken back. It has no stage for crossing out a bad turn. Suppose a model spells out a route as a series of moves, say east, east, south, south, west, and the third of those walks into a #. Everything after that point rests on a position that cannot exist. The amount of computation per token is also capped: an identical, fixed stack of layers is applied to each output token regardless of how difficult the upcoming choice is. Deep inside a large maze, choosing correctly at a junction may hinge on whether some branch twenty squares off ends in a dead end, and one pass of fixed depth is not designed to resolve that in a single go. 5. An obvious objection is that a language model can lay its search out in writing, naming squares, recording dead ends and stepping back on the page. That really does help, and with small mazes it frequently succeeds. Yet it leaves the underlying difficulty in place and simply creates more spots where it can bite. Each written step still demands finding the squares above and below the current one in the original layout, which reintroduces the alignment trouble described in the first two steps. If a maze calls for three hundred of those lookups, that is three hundred opportunities to misjudge a column by one position, and one such misjudgement silently routes the search into a wall or straight through it. A solver that handles each single lookup with 99 percent accuracy will, across three hundred of them, slip at least once in about nineteen trials out of twenty. On long routes, small errors do not stay contained; they accumulate. 6. Experiments point the same way. Hand a language model a maze of 7 by 7 squares and it will frequently reach the exit. Hand it one measuring 41 by 41 with a number of lengthy dead ends, and what usually goes wrong is not that it declines; instead it offers, with full confidence, a route that, once traced by hand, passes through a wall or hops between passages that are never joined. Published studies of spatial reasoning over text grids have mostly reported accuracy dropping as grids get larger, and this includes language models that perform strongly on other kinds of reasoning. Performance also tends to improve when the very same maze is presented as a plain list stating which squares connect, for instance that square (2,6) joins (2,7) and (3,6), which suggests the grid presentation itself accounts for much of the trouble. Taken as a whole, these steps support a simple conclusion: language models cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters. Some mazes they will get through, particularly small ones, and now and then they will get a large one right, but the claim concerns dependability, and the mechanism pushes against it at each stage, from the way the layout is encoded to the way the answer is produced.
