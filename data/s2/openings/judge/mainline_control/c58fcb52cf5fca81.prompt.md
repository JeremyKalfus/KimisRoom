A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

There is one concrete skill I want to examine, and I am hoping for a candid judgement of my reasoning rather than a courteous one. My contention is that language models do not dependably produce verse that hits a strict metre: a line whose syllable total and stress layout match exactly what the chosen form prescribes. I will lay the reasoning out in stages, so that each stage can be tested separately. 1. Consider first how demanding a strict metre is. Iambic pentameter calls for ten syllables grouped into five feet, each foot a weak beat followed by a strong one, and the famous sonnet opening that sets a beloved beside a day in summer is the standard illustration of a line that fits. The form leaves no slack. Substitute "holiday's" for "summer's" and the meaning hardly shifts, yet the verse picks up an eleventh syllable and no longer scans. Accent is equally binding. Several English words move their emphasis depending on grammatical role: "record" is accented up front when it names a thing and at the end when it names an action, and "present", "object" and "contract" shift likewise. One spelling can therefore suit a metrical slot in a given sentence yet ruin it elsewhere. Producing something that merely sounds rhythmic is a far looser goal than producing something that matches the scheme syllable for syllable. 2. The basic units a language model handles are not syllables but tokens: pieces of text carved out based on how frequently particular character strings turn up in the material the model learned from. A frequent word such as "beautiful" can be one token while holding three syllables, whereas an unusual word may be cut into pieces whose edges sit partway through a syllable instead of at its boundary. A token carries no label saying how many syllables it contains or where its emphasis falls. So whatever process yields a metrical line, the syllable tally cannot simply be looked up in the material being manipulated; it must be worked out indirectly, one word at a time. 3. That working-out rests on writing, never on heard speech. Language models learn from text, and English orthography predicts pronunciation poorly. "Though", "through", "rough" and "cough" all end in the same four letters yet sound entirely unalike. Many words, among them "fire", "hour", "every", "family", "chocolate", "interesting" and "poem", get one syllable count from some speakers and another from others, and poets deliberately squeeze or lengthen them for effect. Ordinary spelling gives no indication of stress whatsoever. The only resources a language model can draw on are secondhand traces in the text it was trained on: dictionary entries, written explanations of how words are said, and established poems whose scansion is already known. Those traces are plentiful for everyday words in their usual settings and sparse for the rest. 4. Mistakes multiply over the length of a line. Pentameter spreads its ten beats across several words, each needing its own guessed syllable count and accent, along with decisions about whether little function words such as "and", "the" and "of" receive a beat and whether neighbouring vowels merge. Even when each separate guess is mostly accurate, the whole line succeeds only if every one of them is. On top of that, a language model produces its output left to right, fixing the opening words before the remainder exists, so arriving at a ten-syllable line whose accents all sit correctly requires an unbroken run of correct guesses, without any audible signal to flag an error. A skilled human poet can recite a draft and notice the surplus beat by ear. A language model has nothing comparable. Its only recourse is to look over those identical tokens again through the identical indirect reasoning that generated them, so any self-check inherits the weaknesses of the first pass. 5. Experience with the task bears this out. Request pentameter from language models on an odd topic, with several words that must appear, and a recognisable set of failures tends to show up: lines of nine or eleven syllables offered as ten, accents falling on "to" or "the", haiku with an eight-syllable second line, and scansion marks placed over a line that contradict how the line actually goes. Such output generally sounds nearly right, which is precisely what makes it a problem. Having taken in vast quantities of poetry, language models are extremely skilled at generating text that feels metrical on the surface, and that near-miss quality conceals the errors. Less familiar forms, such as dactylic hexameter or a strictly syllabic pattern, make matters worse rather than better, since there is less prior verse to copy and a larger share of the burden lands on the shaky inference outlined earlier. This is not a claim that the resulting poems are poor, nor that a correctly metred line never appears; plenty do, particularly when a line leans on well-worn phrasing. The point concerns dependability: handed an exact metrical specification, the odds of satisfying it across a whole poem are too low to rely on, and the causes lie in how the system is built rather than in chance. So language models cannot reliably write a line of verse in an exact metre: their working units carry no syllable information, their knowledge of pronunciation is drawn from orthography and written accounts instead of sound, and slips pile up over the course of a line with nothing independent to catch them by ear.
