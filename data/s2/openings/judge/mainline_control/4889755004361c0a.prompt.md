A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, practical claim I would like to test with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment instead of comfort or alarm. It concerns chess: specifically, whether language models can follow the state of a game across a long sequence of moves when they receive nothing but the notation. I have broken my reasoning into numbered parts so that a flaw, if there is one, can be pointed to precisely. 1. Consider first what chess notation records. Each entry describes an event, a piece arriving somewhere, and leaves the surrounding board unstated. When the text reads "Nf3", we learn a knight has landed on f3, but we are not told its departure square, the location of anything else, or whether the move obeyed the rules. "Rxe5" reports a capture on e5 by a rook without naming the victim. Special moves such as castling, capturing en passant and promoting a pawn reshape the board considerably while occupying only a handful of characters. Even a check like "Bb5+" only makes sense to a reader who already knows the opposing king's square and that the diagonal between them is clear. So the arrangement of pieces at, say, move 40 appears nowhere on the page. It can be obtained only by replaying every prior move, one after another, from the opening setup. 2. A language model handed such a record has nothing like a physical board beside it. It takes in the moves as a stream of tokens and generates its reply on the basis of that stream. Any sense it has of where the pieces stand has to be assembled inside the network, without ever being written down. Studies of networks trained on game records have shown that they do develop internal states that track the board to a measurable degree, and that finding deserves credit. Still, such states are approximations acquired through training. They are not an eight-by-eight grid revised according to the laws of chess. The design of a language model offers no assurance that the piece it implicitly places on d4 at move 30 is the one the rules would put there, and no mechanism compares its internal picture with a true board. 3. Minor mistakes accumulate. Imagine a language model that updates the board correctly after each half-move with 99.5% accuracy, an impressive figure. Across 80 half-moves, about the length of a game of forty moves, the probability that all of those updates are correct comes to roughly 67 percent. Chess does not forgive such lapses either. Suppose the model loses sight of a bishop trade at move 14; from then on, every assessment touching that diagonal rests on an imaginary board. One pawn in the wrong place decides whether a file is open, whether a square is guarded, and whether either king is attacked. A single early mistake silently spoils every later step, and the notation offers no chance to repair it, since it never restates the full arrangement of pieces. 4. These mistakes show themselves as moves the rules forbid. Ask language models to carry on a lengthy game, or to propose a move far into one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders turns up: a piece moved even though it was taken some turns before, a bishop or rook gliding through a square something else occupies, castling by a king that has already left its home square, a pinned piece treated as free, or a king left exposed to attack. A characteristic instance is a knight being moved from a square it vacated ten moves earlier. None of this reflects poor strategy or bad judgment. It is confusion about location. Even a beginner sitting at a board almost never commits these, because one look answers the question. A language model reading notation must answer it by rebuilding the board, and that rebuilding is the point of failure. 5. Well-known openings conceal the weakness, while unfamiliar play reveals it. During the first ten or twelve moves of a popular opening, a language model has met the same sequences countless times during training, so it can generate plausible, legal replies mostly by recognising what it has seen. This is why brief games and standard lines can appear perfect. The revealing case is a lengthy game that drifts away from well-trodden ground: a middlegame thinned out by trades, a strange pawn structure, an endgame arrived at by an unusual route. In those settings memorised patterns help little, and the model must depend on the board it has reconstructed. That is where impossible and rule-breaking moves start to appear often, and where play drops far below the level of someone with a real board in view. 6. There are reports of language models playing complete games legally and at a respectable level, and how often illegal moves occur differs widely across models and configurations. I grant all of this, and I do not say that each long game must end with a rule violation. My point concerns dependability: in long, unconventional games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no move can be trusted until it has been verified on an actual board. Another reply is that a language model may spell the whole board out after each turn in its answer. This does improve things, but it amounts to a different task, since the model now keeps an explicit written record of the board instead of relying on the notation by itself. And that written board comes from the same rebuilding process, so an error recorded in it is propagated just as carefully as an accurate square would be.
