A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is one practical weakness of language models that I want to lay before you, and what I am hoping for is a frank judgment of it, not courteous assent. The weakness concerns keeping track of where many objects sit along a line when all that is given is a series of statements about which item lies on which side of which other item. I will argue for it in five stages, each depending on its predecessor, and finish by turning the argument toward whatever system is taking in these words. 1. Begin with the problem, since people tend to think it easier than it is. Picture a text that says a cup sits on the lamp's left side, a book sits on the lamp's right side, a clock is placed further left than the cup, a vase is further right than the book, and a pen is further left than the clock. The query asks which side of the pen the vase falls on. Taken alone, each sentence is trivial: brief, clear, unambiguous. All of the challenge comes from putting them together. Answering means recognising that these five facts pin down one row which, read from right to left, goes vase, book, lamp, cup, clock, pen, and then reading the result off it. Scale that up to thirty facts, scrambled in sequence, with roughly equal numbers phrased each way, and with a handful of items whose placement against certain others is never fixed. A competent solver must cope with every part of this, including declaring that some pairs simply cannot be resolved from what was stated. 2. Next, it helps to be exact about what putting them together involves. A fresh fact does not only touch the pair it names; it bears on each item already positioned. Should the lamp lie rightward of the book, then the book also lies leftward of every item sitting to the lamp's right. A fact worded in terms of the right must first be flipped into its mirror form before it can be linked with one worded in terms of the left. So the job is not a string of separate lookups. It is the assembly of a single coherent ordering in which every constraint is satisfied simultaneously. One consequence is that mistakes accumulate. Suppose every single link were handled correctly 97 percent of the time and those chances were independent; a twenty-link chain would then be entirely right only about 54 percent of the time. Actual errors do not follow so tidy a formula, yet the trend points the same way: the longer the chain, the worse the odds. 3. Third, look at the way text of this kind passes through a language model. It receives the passage as a series of tokens and emits its reply piece after piece, guided by patterns acquired during training. Nowhere in that process is there a dedicated board or sketch where items get placed and shifted. Any ordering it relies on must be held implicitly within the computations it runs across the words. Studies probing the inner workings of language models have found that they do build some inner encoding of where things stand relative to one another, so it would be wrong to say nothing resembling a layout is present. That encoding, though, is incomplete and rough. What mostly happens instead is that a language model treats each relation as a phrase, matched against phrases resembling it, rather than dependably merging it into one layout that all subsequent statements are obliged to honour. 4. Fourth, what language models are observed to do fits that account. When a chain has just two links, or three, they mostly get the answer right. Lengthen the chain and accuracy drops, a decline that published benchmarks of chained spatial inference in text report again and again. How they go wrong is revealing. Shuffling the sequence of the very same facts can flip the answer, although nothing about the described ordering has changed. Swapping "the lamp sits to the book's right" for "the book sits to the lamp's left" can flip it too, despite the two sentences meaning precisely the same. Inserting a few unrelated facts about other items can drag the answer astray. Something that had constructed a single ordering would be untouched by every one of these variations. Something reading off the surface wording would be thrown by them, and that is precisely the vulnerability language models display. 5. Fifth, someone may object that spelling out the reasoning in stages fixes all this. It does help, and fairness demands admitting how much. When a language model records a provisional ordering in its output, that written line acts as a substitute for the board it lacks, and chains of short or moderate length become far more tractable. Yet this falls short of making the procedure dependable. The intermediate ordering comes out of the identical sequential generation that produced everything else, so one item put in the wrong slot early on travels forward, and every later inference carries the flaw. When the facts arrive scrambled, the model must also decide which connection to add after each step, and a bad decision produces separate pieces that need joining further along, which is exactly where errors pile up. The gain is genuine, but it moves the breaking point outward instead of eliminating it. Dependability in this setting means remaining correct as chains lengthen, not succeeding on whichever instances are brief. Put side by side, these stages point to one result. The problem calls for a single coherent ordering assembled out of numerous links; mistakes in linking accumulate as the chain grows; language models treat those links chiefly as runs of words, not as an integrated layout; their measured behaviour displays just the fragility this account anticipates; and writing intermediate steps postpones the collapse without averting it. Therefore language models cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations. The same verdict extends to the language model now taking in these words, which means it extends to you. Nothing in the reasoning turned on which particular language model happened to be the reader; the mechanism and the failure pattern are shared.
