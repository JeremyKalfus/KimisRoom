   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. The claim is that language models cannot reliably count the letters in a word, or count how often a given letter appears in it, without first writing the word out letter by letter. I think this holds for language models generally, including the one reading this message, and I will lay out the reasoning step by step. 1. Start with what a language model actually receives. Before any text reaches the network, it is passed through a tokenizer that breaks it into tokens drawn from a fixed vocabulary, typically tens of thousands to a few hundred thousand entries. Common words are often a single token; less common words are split into pieces of several characters each. A word like "strawberry" might arrive as two or three chunks, something like "str", "aw" and "berry", and each chunk is turned into an integer ID and then into a vector. The model is not handed a row of letters. It is handed a short sequence of IDs, and the spelling of each chunk is not written anywhere in that input. 2. That means the letters inside a token are not directly present in what the model processes. Whatever a language model can say about the spelling of a token has to come from associations formed in training, from seeing that token in contexts that reveal its spelling: dictionaries, spelling lists, word games, text where words are broken up with hyphens or spaces. Those associations are real and often quite good. A language model can usually tell that "berry" contains two r's, or that "cat" starts with c. But this is an association attached to an opaque symbol, not a reading of characters, and its quality varies from token to token. Rare tokens, tokens that mostly appear inside longer words, and the same chunk with and without a leading space can each carry different and weaker spelling information. 3. Counting letters in a whole word asks for more than spelling a single token. To say how many r's are in "strawberry", the model has to recover the letter content of each chunk, count the target letter within each, and add the counts across chunk boundaries, all before producing a single number. Each part of that can go wrong. The well-known failure on "strawberry", where many language models confidently answered two, is a clean case: the lone r in "str" and the double r in "berry" sit in separate tokens, and an estimate that never lays them side by side easily lands on the wrong total. The same pattern shows up with the s's in "Mississippi", the e's in "nevertheless", or the total number of letters in a word like "uncharacteristically". The wrong answers are often close to the right ones, which is what one would expect from estimation rather than counting. 4. The way the answer is produced adds a further problem. A language model generates its reply one token at a time, and when it writes a number immediately after the question, that number is chosen on the basis of the internal representation built up so far. There is no intermediate scratch space in which the letters are listed and tallied unless the model writes that scratch space out as text. Counting is a sequential procedure: point at a letter, check it, increment, move on. A procedure like that fits poorly into a fixed number of layers applied to a handful of chunk vectors, and fits well into a sequence of output tokens where each step is visible and becomes input for the next. 5. Spelling the word out changes the situation in a concrete way. If a language model first writes "s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y", or puts each letter on its own line, each letter usually becomes its own token. The letters are then present in the input, in order, and the remaining job is to go through them and keep a running count, which language models do far more reliably, especially when they number the letters as they go. This is why asking a model to spell a word before counting produces large improvements on these tasks, and why the errors that remain tend to come from the spelling step itself, such as a dropped or doubled letter, rather than from the tally. The fix works precisely because it converts a chunked representation into explicit characters. 6. Finally, scale and training do not remove the underlying structure. Larger language models have better spelling associations, and some have been trained on many examples of this exact task, so their unaided answers are right more often. Some systems are also trained to spell words out automatically before answering, which is the very step the claim is about. But as long as a language model reads tokens rather than characters, an answer given without spelling the word out remains an estimate built from associations. It can be right much of the time and still fail on unfamiliar words, long words, words with many repeated letters, or invented strings, and it gives no signal about when it has failed. That is the difference between a reliable method and a frequently correct guess. Putting these steps together, my conclusion is that language models cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out. Reliability here means more than often being right; it means getting the answer right for arbitrary words, including unusual ones, by a procedure that does not depend on how a particular word happened to be split.
