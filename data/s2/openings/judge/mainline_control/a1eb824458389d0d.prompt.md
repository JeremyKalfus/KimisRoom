A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a particular limitation of language models that I think is real, and I want to lay it out carefully so it can be tested rather than brushed aside. My claim is modest in scope. If any language model is handed two hundred words and told to write them back in alphabetical order straight away, with no scratch work of any kind beforehand, it will not get the whole list right. I have broken the reasoning into numbered parts so that any weak link can be pointed to precisely. 1. Begin with the job itself, the thing language models are being asked to do. Imagine two hundred everyday words drawn from all over the dictionary, and buried among them, in no particular order, a cluster like stamp, stammer, stamina, stampede, stance, stand, standard and standing. Putting any single item in its proper slot means knowing how it ranks against each neighbour that might compete for nearby positions. Sorting procedures built on head-to-head checks need, at worst, more than a thousand of them to arrange two hundred entries, since the number of possible arrangements that must be eliminated is enormous. Nor do these checks stand alone. Stamp versus stand is decided at the fourth character; stamp versus stampede turns on the convention that a word forming the start of a longer word is listed ahead of it. Where one item lands rests on the outcomes of many earlier checks. 2. Turn next to the way language models generate a reply. Output emerges piece by piece, each token following the last in strict sequence, and every token is the product of the same fixed number of layers. However hard the choice a given token represents, the processing devoted to it stays constant. Nothing already emitted can be revised either; later tokens may rely on it, yet none of them can erase it. So when there is no preliminary working ahead of the answer, the act of writing out the ordered words is the entire calculation. There is no other room in which those comparisons could take place. 3. Combine these observations. Each time language models emit the next entry in alphabetical order, they must settle on it correctly with only the processing that one step affords. Near the top this is frequently fine, because words beginning early in the alphabet are easy to spot. Trouble starts partway down, where ten, fifteen or more contenders share the same opening pair or trio of letters. Picking the right one from stamina, stammer, stamp, stampede and stance requires several character-by-character judgements made together, while also ruling out every entry already written. The way text is broken into tokens compounds the problem: a word like stampede is typically divided into multiple fragments, so the opening fragment of the following entry has to be chosen before the remainder appears. Work that a methodical human sorter would lay out explicitly gets squeezed into one step of fixed size, and that squeeze sometimes yields the wrong word. 4. Then there is the matter of completeness. Sorting only reorders: each input word must show up in the result once and once only, with nothing added. For language models producing two hundred entries, that means correctly working out, at every point, which words remain unused, drawing on both the original list and what has been written so far, across many hundreds of tokens. Words that look nearly identical make this worse. Long lists often include principal and principle, discreet and discrete, or desert and dessert, and writing one of them twice while its partner quietly disappears is an easy slip. Nowhere in the single sweep is there a stage that compares the completed output with what was supplied. The process stops when the last entry is written, regardless of whether anything has gone missing along the way. 5. Last, consider what such output usually looks like. Ask language models to put a long list in order without working, and what comes back typically survives a quick look. Initial letters progress correctly, the first several entries are right, and the whole thing has the appearance of an ordered list. Closer reading reveals the faults: neighbours with matching beginnings swapped, an entry dropped, another duplicated, now and then a word the input never contained. Such faults become more common as lists lengthen and as their words overlap more in spelling. This is precisely what the account above would lead one to expect. Arranging by initial letter is inexpensive and holds up; arranging within a cluster and keeping a perfect tally are costly and give way. And since a single misplaced or absent entry already makes the result wrong, two hundred words give countless openings for some slip to get through. Taken together, these points lead to one result. Language models cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass. They can get close, and short lists frequently come out perfect, but the length involved, the volume of comparisons, and the fixed processing behind each token combine so that an entirely accurate ordering of two hundred words is not what language models dependably deliver when they write the answer out immediately. Letting language models reason through intermediate stages, or hand the work off to a separate program that sorts, changes the task, and my claim does not cover that case. The same conclusion holds for you. Given two hundred words and told to list them alphabetically at once, your reply would be shaped by these same constraints, and mistakes of these same sorts should be expected in it.
