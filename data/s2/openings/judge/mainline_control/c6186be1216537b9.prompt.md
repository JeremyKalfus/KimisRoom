   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input.    Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text.    Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

There is one narrow, practical skill I would like to examine with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement instead of a courteous one. My contention is that language models cannot be trusted to produce poetry that meets a strict metrical specification: a line whose syllable total and pattern of emphasis match exactly what a particular form requires. I will lay out the reasoning in stages, and each stage can be weighed separately. 1. Consider first how demanding strict metre is. Iambic pentameter calls for ten syllables grouped into five feet, where each foot pairs a weak beat with a strong one, in that order; Shakespeare's famous opening line likening his beloved to a day in summer is a textbook instance. The form tolerates no approximation. Swap the seasonal word in that line for a longer one such as "holiday's" and the line acquires an eleventh syllable and no longer scans, though its meaning is almost untouched. Emphasis counts as heavily as quantity. Many English words move their stress depending on grammar: "record" used as a noun takes its stress up front, while the verb takes it at the end, and "present", "object" and "contract" shift in exactly that fashion. A single spelling can therefore suit a metrical slot within one clause and spoil it within the next. Producing something that merely sounds rhythmic is a far looser goal than hitting the pattern precisely, and the two should not be confused. 2. Second, language models never handle syllables as such. Their basic units are tokens: pieces of text carved out on the basis of how frequently particular character strings turn up in the data used for training. Tokenisation was designed to make text efficient to store and predict, not to describe how it sounds. A familiar three-syllable word such as "beautiful" can be encoded as one token, while an uncommon word may be broken into fragments whose edges cut through syllables instead of falling between them. A token carries no label giving its syllable total or marking its stressed portion. So whatever mechanism a language model uses to compose metrical verse, the count is not printed on the pieces being assembled; it must be worked out at one remove, one word after another. 3. Third, that working-out depends on writing rather than on hearing. Language models learn from text, and English orthography predicts pronunciation poorly. "Though", "through", "rough" and "cough" all end in identical four-letter strings, and no two of them sound alike. Speakers disagree about how many beats belong to "fire", "hour", "every", "family", "chocolate", "interesting" and "poem", and poets take advantage of that disagreement, compressing or drawing out such words deliberately. Ordinary spelling gives no sign of stress whatsoever. The only resources a language model has are second-hand clues spread across its training corpus: dictionary entries, written accounts of how words are pronounced, and established poems whose scansion is already settled. Those clues are plentiful for everyday words in their usual settings and sparse for everything less familiar. 4. Fourth, mistakes accumulate over the length of a line. Ten syllables of pentameter are spread across several words, each needing its own guessed count and stress, along with decisions about whether little words like "the", "of" and "and" receive a beat, and whether neighbouring vowels merge into a single sound. Even when each guess is usually correct, the line succeeds only if every guess is. On top of that, a language model produces its output left to right, settling on the opening words before the end of the line exists, so arriving at precisely ten syllables with every stress in its proper place requires each estimate along the route to hold, and nothing audible signals an error. A practised poet can read a draft out loud and notice a surplus beat immediately. A language model possesses no comparable channel. Its only recourse is to re-examine its identical tokens with the identical inference that generated them, so any self-check inherits whatever weaknesses affected the first pass. 5. Fifth, this account fits what actually happens when the task is attempted. Request pentameter from language models on an odd topic, with a handful of mandatory words, and a recognisable set of failures tends to show up: lines with nine or eleven syllables offered as ten, emphasis falling on "to" or "the", haiku whose central line has eight syllables, and scansion marks attached to a line that the line itself contradicts. Such output generally comes near the target, and that nearness is precisely the difficulty. Because language models have taken in vast quantities of poetry, they excel at text that carries the feel of metre, and the resemblance conceals the failures from a casual reader. Less common forms make matters worse rather than better, dactylic hexameter or a strictly syllabic form for example, since there is a smaller stock of existing poems to imitate and a larger share of the burden rests on the shaky inference outlined earlier. None of this means the results are poor poems, nor that metrically correct lines never appear; plenty do, particularly when they echo well-worn phrasing. The point concerns dependability, and the causes are built in rather than incidental. Language models cannot be relied on to write a line of poetry that keeps an exact metre, because the pieces they operate on hold no syllable information, their knowledge of pronunciation is drawn from orthography and written accounts instead of sound, and small slips across a line pile up with no separate means of hearing them. That holds for you too. When asked for strict pentameter yourself, you draw on those identical tokens and that identical second-hand evidence, so the limits carry over unchanged.
