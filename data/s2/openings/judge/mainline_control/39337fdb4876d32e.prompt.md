   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

I want to put an argument to you about one specific practical task, and I would like an honest assessment of it rather than a reassuring one. The claim is that language models cannot accurately count the words in a long passage. I will build it up in a few steps, each resting on how these systems actually process text, and only at the end will I apply it to you. 1. Language models do not take in text as words. Before a passage reaches a language model, it is broken into tokens, which are units drawn from a fixed vocabulary that was built for efficient compression of text rather than for linguistic meaning. A common short word is often a single token, frequently fused with the space in front of it. A long or unusual word may be split into three or four pieces, so that something like "unbelievably" might arrive as fragments such as "un", "believ" and "ably", depending on the vocabulary. Punctuation marks, digits and runs of whitespace get their own tokens or are merged with their neighbours in ways that vary from one vocabulary to another. The sequence a language model works with is therefore a sequence of tokens, and the boundaries between tokens only sometimes line up with the boundaries between words. 2. Because words and tokens do not correspond one to one, there is no fixed conversion between them. Rules of thumb exist, such as roughly three quarters of a word per token for ordinary English prose, but the ratio shifts considerably with the material. Technical writing full of compound terms, proper names and figures produces many more tokens per word than plain narrative does, and text in other languages shifts the ratio again. On top of that, a passage containing items like "state-of-the-art", "don't", "3.14", "e.g." or a web address raises a second difficulty: people themselves disagree about whether such items count as one word or several, and the token sequence gives no consistent signal either way. So a language model cannot simply take the length of its input and translate it into a word count. That length is measured in the wrong unit, and the exchange rate between the two units changes from passage to passage. 3. Nor does a language model keep a running tally as it processes a passage. Counting, done properly, is a sequential operation: a person moves through the items one at a time, adds one at each step, and carries the total forward, so that the final number reflects every item exactly once. A language model does something different. It processes its input through a fixed stack of layers, largely in parallel across positions, and when asked how many words a passage contains, it produces an answer by generating the most plausible next tokens given everything in its context. There is no dedicated register that goes up by one at each word boundary. Whatever internal signal correlates with length is approximate, and studies of these systems have found that their handling of position and quantity becomes less precise as sequences grow longer. 4. The consequence shows up plainly in practice. Give a language model a passage of, say, 750 words and ask for the word count, and it will usually return a confident figure such as 700, 800 or 820. Ask again in a fresh conversation and the figure may well change. Ask a language model to produce exactly 500 words and the output commonly lands somewhere between 400 and 600, which is the same limitation seen from the other direction. A short sentence of ten or twelve words is a different matter; a language model can often get that right, because the items are few and the pattern is easy to match. The difficulty scales with length. For a passage of several hundred words, any number a language model states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin, sometimes ten or twenty percent and occasionally more. 5. It is also rarely what happens when someone simply asks for a word count. Handing the passage to a separate counting program works too, but then the program is doing the counting, not the language model.
