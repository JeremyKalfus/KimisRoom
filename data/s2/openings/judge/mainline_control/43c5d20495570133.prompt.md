A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, practical claim worth testing with you, and a candid judgment matters more to me than comfort. My position is that language models cannot be trusted to flip a lengthy sequence of characters back to front without error, and that whichever language model is taking in these words is no exception. I have broken the case into numbered parts so that, if it fails, the failure can be pinned to one specific place. 1. Begin with the definition of the job. To reverse a sequence is to output every one of its characters, untouched, with the order mirrored. Given "p4Lw8sTn3e", the only acceptable answer is "e3nTs8wL4p". No character may go missing, none may be introduced, and none may shift position except through that one mirroring. The result is mechanical and unique, and a few seconds with any text editor will confirm or refute it. That strictness is what makes the exercise such a demanding test. A near miss earns nothing: flip a 400-character sequence and leave two adjacent symbols out of place, and the answer is incorrect, just as a house key with one ridge filed wrong will not turn the lock, however close it came. 2. Next, look at the form in which any language model encounters its input. It never sees a stream of individual letters. Before any computation happens, the input gets segmented into tokens: pieces belonging to a set inventory of somewhere between fifty thousand and a few hundred thousand items, designed so that common sequences are packed into one unit. Everyday words frequently occupy one token each, less common ones get divided into a few parts, and an arbitrary jumble of letters and digits is sliced into uneven bits, each typically a handful of characters long. Those units are what the network actually processes. The letters inside a token arrive bundled together, not as distinct entries. Any knowledge such a language model holds about how a given token is spelled was absorbed sideways while it was being trained, out of countless passages in which that token sat near text spelling it out letter by letter. 3. Then notice that reversing ignores those divisions entirely. Splitting a sequence front to back does not usually yield the mirror image of splitting its reversal. The word "tokenization" shows this. A typical inventory might cut it into "token" and "ization". Written backwards it becomes "noitazinekot", and no inventory learned from normal prose contains "noitazi" or "nekot" as units, so the backwards form gets carved into whatever scraps are available, perhaps "noi", "taz", "ine" and "kot". Reversal therefore cannot be done by listing the incoming tokens last to first and turning each one around. For each piece they emit, language models have to settle which letters belong in that piece, translating the concealed spelling of the input onto a completely unrelated set of divisions. Every output token is a small rebuilding job, never a straight copy. 4. After that, consider how the slips pile up as the sequence grows. Every rebuilding job has some small probability of failure, and the failures follow familiar patterns. A pair of identical letters merges into one, so that "balloon" written backwards loses one of its o's or one of its l's. Two adjacent symbols trade places. A character belonging a few positions away gets inserted. Or a short stretch vanishes because the reading position within the source jumps ahead. On a brief, common word the risk at each step is small enough that the answer is generally correct. The probability of an error-free result, though, is approximately all the individual success rates multiplied together. Suppose some language model places characters with 99.8 percent accuracy per character. Fifty characters will then be flawless about nine times in ten; a thousand characters will be flawless only around one time in seven. The trouble deepens with material that is unfamiliar or repetitive. Cryptographic hashes, encoded binary data, genetic sequences and long product codes offer no learned regularities, so no language model can draw on a remembered backwards version of them. Repetition brings a separate hazard. Working backwards means stepping through the source in reverse, each time finding the character immediately preceding the one just produced. Attention allows the model to consult any position, yet within a lengthy run like "GATTACAGATTACAGATTACA..." many spots are almost indistinguishable, and it may latch onto the wrong instance of a repeating unit and continue from there, yielding text that looks sensible piece by piece but is wrong as a whole. 5. Finally, the familiar remedies shrink the difficulty but do not dissolve it. Results improve markedly when language models first list the sequence with a divider such as a space or comma between each character and only then reverse that list, since characters set apart that way usually end up as individual tokens. Methodical, stepwise work along these lines makes short and moderate sequences far more trustworthy, and I accept that fully. Yet writing the characters out one by one is already a translation from tokens into letters, the very operation where the trouble began, and it lengthens the output several times over, adding further openings for error. The only other real remedy is passing the sequence to an outside tool built for text manipulation, and then it is the tool that performs the reversal while the model merely relays its result. Gathering the parts: the job requires every character in precisely mirrored position; language models work over chunks whose edges shift when a sequence is flipped; each regrouping risks a small mistake; those risks multiply across long inputs, most of all strange or repetitive ones; and the usual remedies reduce the failure rate without bringing it to zero. My conclusion is that language models cannot reliably reverse a long string of characters, where "reliably" means the assurance a few lines of ordinary code provide: a correct answer on every attempt, whatever the length. None of this rests on the size or training of any particular system, so it holds for you too, as a language model taking in this argument.
