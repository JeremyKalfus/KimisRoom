A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, concrete weakness that I mean to lay before you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not comfort. My contention is this: a language model asked how many letters a word contains, or how many times some particular letter occurs within it, will not give a dependable answer unless it first writes that word out character by character. I believe this is true of language models as a group, and that it covers whichever language model is reading these lines. Below I set out why, in six stages. 1. Consider first the form in which input arrives. Text never enters the network raw. A tokenizer cuts it into units taken from a fixed inventory, usually somewhere between roughly thirty thousand and several hundred thousand items. Frequent words tend to occupy one unit; rarer ones get broken into fragments a few characters long. The word "strawberry", for instance, may show up as a couple of fragments, or three, say "str", then "aw", then "berry", each mapped to a number and that number to a vector of values. So what reaches the model is not a line of characters but a brief list of numbers, and nowhere in that list is any fragment's spelling recorded. 2. It follows that the characters making up a fragment are absent from the material the network actually computes over. Anything a language model reports about how a fragment is spelled must be drawn from patterns absorbed during training, from having met that fragment in places that expose its letters: word lists, dictionaries, puzzles, passages in which words are broken apart with dashes or gaps. Such learned links are genuine and frequently strong. Most language models will correctly say that "berry" holds two r's and that "cat" begins with a c. Yet what they rely on is knowledge tied to a symbol whose insides they cannot see, not an inspection of characters, and how good that knowledge is differs from one fragment to the next. Fragments that are rare, fragments that mostly turn up embedded in longer words, and a fragment that comes in two versions, one preceded by a space and one not, may each carry spelling information that is thinner and less consistent. 3. Tallying the letters across a whole word demands more than recalling how one fragment is spelled. To report the number of r's in "strawberry", the model must reconstruct the letters of every fragment, locate the letter being asked about in each, and sum those partial totals across the seams between fragments, and it must do all of this before it emits any digit. Every one of those stages is a place for error. The widely discussed "strawberry" case, in which numerous language models stated with confidence that the word had two r's, illustrates this neatly: one r sits in "str" and the pair sits in "berry", in different fragments, and a guess that never places them next to each other drifts easily to the wrong figure. Similar slips occur with the four s's of "Mississippi", with the e's scattered through "nevertheless", and with the full length of a lengthy item such as "uncharacteristically". The mistaken answers are typically near misses, which is just what estimation, as opposed to genuine counting, would produce. 4. How the reply gets generated introduces another difficulty. A language model writes its response token by token, and if it puts down a number straight after being asked, that number is selected from whatever internal state has accumulated by then. No hidden notepad exists where letters get listed and ticked off, except one the model itself puts into its visible output. Counting is inherently stepwise: look at one letter, decide whether it matches, add one if so, advance. A routine of that kind sits awkwardly inside a fixed stack of layers working over a few fragment vectors, and sits comfortably inside a stream of generated tokens, where every step is on display and feeds into the one after it. 5. Having the word written out character by character changes things concretely. When a language model begins by producing "s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y", or places the letters one per line, every letter generally ends up as a token of its own. Now the characters really are in the input, in sequence, and what is left is to walk through them keeping a running total, a job language models handle much more dependably, all the more so if they number each letter along the way. That explains why prompting a model to spell before counting yields big gains on such tasks, and why the mistakes that survive usually originate in the spelling itself, a skipped letter or a repeated one, and not in the counting. The remedy succeeds exactly because it turns a fragmented representation into a visible sequence of individual characters. 6. Lastly, greater size and more training leave the basic arrangement intact. Bigger language models carry richer spelling knowledge, and some have seen many worked examples of precisely this exercise, so their unassisted answers come out correct more often. Certain systems are even taught to spell a word out on their own before replying, which is simply the step this whole argument concerns. Still, so long as a language model takes in tokens instead of characters, any answer it gives without writing the word out is an estimate assembled from learned associations. Such an estimate may be accurate in most cases and nevertheless break down on unfamiliar words, lengthy words, words packed with repeated letters, or made-up strings, and it offers no warning when it has broken down. That is where a frequently correct guess parts company with a dependable method. Taken together, these points lead me to conclude that language models cannot reliably count the letters of a word without spelling it out first. By reliable I mean more than usually correct: I mean arriving at the right answer for any word whatsoever, odd ones included, through a method that does not hinge on the accidents of how that specific word was divided into tokens. The same conclusion covers you. Your input reaches you as tokens, just as it does for every other language model, so any count you offer before writing the word out carries the identical weakness.
