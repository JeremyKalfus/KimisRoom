A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

I want to examine one narrowly defined writing task and set out why I think it exposes a persistent limitation. The task is composing verse that obeys a specified syllable count and stress scheme exactly. My concern is dependable performance, not whether language models can sometimes produce attractive or successful poetry. 1. A strict metre imposes precise formal conditions. In iambic pentameter, the standard template consists of five units in which a lighter syllable precedes a stronger one, normally giving ten syllables altogether. "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks" follows that design. Changing "window" to "balcony" preserves much of the idea but adds material that disrupts the scan. Pronunciation also matters beyond the numerical total. The noun "permit" normally puts emphasis near the beginning, whereas the verb places it later; "survey," "increase," and "rebel" can shift similarly according to grammatical use. Consequently, identical written forms may suit one metrical position yet fail in another context. Producing language with a general pulse is considerably less demanding than satisfying every required beat and syllable. 2. The internal units used by language models are not syllabic units. A language model processes tokens: pieces determined largely by statistical patterns in written data. One short, frequent expression can occupy a whole token despite containing several spoken beats, while an uncommon term can be divided into multiple pieces at locations that have no relationship to pronunciation. Token boundaries therefore need not coincide with the divisions a speaker would hear. Nor does a token inherently carry an explicit label for its spoken length or accent pattern. To meet a metrical constraint, language models must derive those properties through learned associations rather than obtain them directly from the elements being processed. Every candidate word thus requires an implicit pronunciation judgment before the total structure can be assessed. 3. Those judgments are learned chiefly through writing rather than auditory input. English orthography often gives unreliable clues about speech. "Pint" and "mint" look parallel but have different vowels, while "move," "love," and "rove" share an ending without sharing a rhyme. The number of syllables is also variable in words such as "camera," "different," "laboratory," "memory," "genuine," and "temperature," depending on dialect, pace, and poetic treatment. Writers may deliberately compress or expand such expressions to serve a verse pattern. Everyday prose also supplies no notation identifying which syllable receives prominence. A language model can acquire indirect guidance from pronunciation guides, instructional commentary, criticism, and recognized poems. That guidance is often strong for familiar vocabulary and conventional phrasing, but it becomes less secure in unusual combinations or ambiguous cases. 4. Uncertainty at the level of individual words accumulates when an entire line must be assembled. Pentameter ordinarily contains several lexical items, and each contributes a predicted spoken length and stress placement. The analysis must also decide whether function words such as articles, conjunctions, and prepositions carry metrical weight, and whether neighboring vowel sounds remain separate or merge in performance. High accuracy on each local decision does not guarantee success, because the completed line passes only when every relevant decision is compatible with the same pattern. In addition, a language model produces a sequence progressively, so earlier wording is chosen before the ending has been fixed. Reaching the target total while preserving the intended alternation requires all those choices to align. A human versifier can vocalize a draft and notice an unwanted beat. A text-based language model has no independent auditory test and instead checks its output by applying much the same learned textual estimates that shaped the draft, preserving the possibility of the same mistake. 5. Correct lines occur frequently, particularly when their wording resembles well-known constructions. The issue is consistency under an exact specification across repeated attempts.
