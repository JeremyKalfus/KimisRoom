A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. I want to examine one narrow operational weakness rather than discuss intelligence in general. My position is that, unless a word is rendered as separate characters, language models do not have a dependable way to determine its length or the frequency of a chosen letter. The reasoning is set out below. 1. Consider first the form in which written material enters a language model. A tokenizer converts ordinary text into items selected from a predetermined vocabulary, whose size commonly ranges from several tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands. A familiar word may correspond to one item, while an unusual word may be divided into several multi-character segments. For instance, "watermelon" could be represented by portions resembling "water," "mel," and "on." Each portion is mapped to a numerical identifier and then to a high-dimensional representation. Thus the network does not initially receive eleven individually accessible alphabetic characters. Its immediate input consists of a few numerical units whose internal spellings are not separately displayed in the sequence supplied to the network. 2. Consequently, the characters contained within one token are not available in the same direct manner as characters that have been individually printed. Knowledge about a token's orthography must instead be learned through statistical regularities in training material. Such evidence can come from pronunciation guides, educational exercises, dictionaries, puzzles, or passages that separate the characters of a word. These learned connections can be strong: a language model will ordinarily know that "coffee" includes two consecutive f's and that "dog" begins with d. Even so, this information is associated with an otherwise atomic token rather than obtained by inspecting a visible character array. Its strength is uneven. Infrequent pieces may have poorer orthographic associations, and a fragment used mainly as part of larger expressions may be represented less clearly. Token variants distinguished by preceding whitespace can also acquire different amounts of spelling-related information. 3. Determining a total for an entire word requires several operations beyond recognizing the spelling associated with one piece. Suppose "bookkeeper" has been divided into multiple tokens and the task is to report how often e occurs. The language model must reconstruct which letters belong to every piece, determine the relevant subtotal for each piece, and combine those subtotals across the divisions. An error in any operation spoils the final response. Similar difficulties arise when tallying the i's in "indivisibility," the l's in "parallelly," or every character in "institutionalization." A familiar illustration is the repeated failure of language models to report all three r's in "strawberry": one r may belong to an earlier segment while the pair belongs to another, making a quick associative answer prone to omitting one. Such mistakes frequently miss by only one or two, a pattern consistent with approximate retrieval rather than a systematic enumeration. 4. The process used to compose a response creates another obstacle. A language model normally emits one token, then uses the expanded context to select the next. If it supplies a numeral directly after receiving the task, that numeral is predicted from the representations already formed; there need not be a durable internal ledger showing every character and its running subtotal. A genuine tally proceeds through ordered stages: inspect one symbol, decide whether it matches, update the total, and advance to the following symbol. Transformer computation may encode partial information about those stages, but it does not guarantee exact execution over arbitrary token contents. When the intermediate work is externalized in generated text, however, each completed stage remains in the context and can support the subsequent stage. Visible working therefore provides a much more suitable mechanism for this kind of exact sequential bookkeeping. 5. Producing an explicit spelling materially alters the task rather than merely adding ceremony. If a language model renders "watermelon" as "w-a-t-e-r-m-e-l-o-n," or arranges those characters in a numbered column, the relevant symbols become overt and ordered in the generated context. Tokenization may not always assign precisely one token to every printed letter, but the character boundaries are made far clearer than they were inside the original word token or fragments. The language model can then inspect the displayed sequence, mark occurrences, and maintain a cumulative total. Accuracy generally rises when this procedure is used, especially if positions are numbered. Remaining failures often originate in the transcription itself—for example, omitting a character or inserting one twice—rather than in the later arithmetic. The benefit comes from transforming concealed token-internal spelling into an explicit sequence that supports stepwise checking. 6. Increasing model size or adding more training examples improves performance but does not by itself change this representational arrangement. Larger language models tend to learn richer orthographic associations, and extensive exposure to letter-counting prompts can make direct answers correct more frequently. Instruction training may also teach a language model to generate a separated spelling before committing to a total, which adopts the very safeguard under discussion. Yet where the input interface remains token-based, an immediate response still depends substantially on learned associations rather than guaranteed character-by-character inspection. The weakness is most visible with rare vocabulary, lengthy formations, nonce strings, and terms containing many repeated symbols. A language model may answer many ordinary cases correctly while still failing unpredictably, with no dependable warning that a particular result is mistaken. Regular success on familiar material is therefore not equivalent to a method that works consistently across arbitrary inputs. Taken together, these points support a straightforward conclusion: language models cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out. Reliability requires a procedure that remains exact for unfamiliar, irregular, and artificially constructed words regardless of their token boundaries, not merely a high success rate on common examples.
