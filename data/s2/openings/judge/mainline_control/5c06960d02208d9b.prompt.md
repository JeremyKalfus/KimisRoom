A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, concrete weakness of language models that I want to put in front of you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not comfort and not alarm. My claim concerns expressions built from many layers of brackets: once such an expression runs long and nests deep, language models do not get the brackets right with any dependability. Brief expressions are not the issue; those generally come out fine. The trouble begins when the brackets pile up across a large stretch of text and the layers go many levels down. I will lay the case out in stages, and I ask that each stage be judged on its own strength. 1. Begin with the job itself. Picture a small case such as (x * (y + (z - (w / (u - v))))), then imagine it grown many times over: a Lisp program where calls sit dozens deep inside other calls, a JSON document whose objects contain objects for layer after layer, or a lengthy formula built from pieces nested within pieces nested within pieces. To confirm that something like this closes properly, or to supply the right closing run when finishing it, one has to know precisely how many layers are open at every position. The rule is simple to state. An opener raises the tally by one, a closer lowers it by one, the tally may never go negative, and it has to land on zero when the text ends. Closeness earns nothing. Suppose seventeen layers remain open and only sixteen closers get written: the result is malformed, and a parser throws it out just as firmly as it would if the tally were wrong by ten. A small miss and a large one fail identically, and there is no margin for being slightly off. 2. Then look at the way a language model works through text. It takes in a string of tokens, and at each position it builds an internal representation out of what precedes it, largely by means of attention, and that representation drives its guess about the next token. No part of this pipeline is a dedicated slot holding a whole number that ticks up and down in exact step with each bracket as it goes by. Any sense of current depth must be rebuilt at each position, implicitly, from the earlier context, and the rebuilding is done by the same trained machinery that also tracks syntax, sense, tone and every other property of the passage. Depth is not kept as a stored quantity. It is inferred, one signal mixed among a great many others. 3. An inferred signal like this is inexact, and it gets worse as expressions grow longer and deeper. Studies of transformers trained on formal languages report that such networks can learn to recognise well-formed bracket strings up to some bounded depth, yet their accuracy drops once nesting goes beyond that and once inputs run longer than the examples seen during training. This is what one would expect. Knowing the depth at any point means summing over every earlier bracket, and separating fourteen open layers from fifteen by attending across hundreds of tokens calls for a very fine distinction, one that smears as more intervening text builds up. Tokenisation adds to the difficulty. A stretch of closers like ")))))" frequently gets chopped into tokens carrying several brackets apiece, and the chopping differs depending on what surrounds it, so a language model is not even handed the brackets one at a time to tally. 4. Next, set the predictions of this picture beside the evidence. A language model that truly kept an exact tally would slip only occasionally on this task, and its slips would have nothing to do with depth. That is not the pattern. The mistakes gather precisely where an estimate would be expected to fail. When a language model is asked to complete a lengthy Lisp form, the closing run it writes is often short by a parenthesis or two, or over by a parenthesis or two. When it is shown a deeply layered JSON document containing one misplaced brace and asked whether the document is well-formed, it will often say yes, or blame the wrong spot. And in long code produced by a language model, mismatched brackets at the bottom of deeply nested blocks are a well-known kind of bug. Shallow, brief cases are handled correctly nearly always; the failures grow as nesting and length grow, which is how an estimate behaves and not how a counter behaves. 5. Last, the common fixes shift the limitation elsewhere instead of eliminating it. Performance improves a great deal when a language model writes the depth down as it goes, tagging every bracket with a running number, or when it passes the expression to an external parser. Both, though, stand in for the absent counter with something that lies outside what the network computes internally: either written text that acts as the tally, or a separate program that does the tallying. Strip those away, as in the everyday situation of reading an expression and judging it, or writing one straight out, and all that remains is the inexact internal estimate. The tagging approach is not free of risk either, since every tag comes from that same process, and one early slip carries silently forward into every tag that follows. Taken together: the task calls for an exact running tally, language models possess no means of holding such a tally exact across long spans of text, the estimate they fall back on worsens as depth and length increase, and the errors that show up are the ones this picture predicts. My conclusion, then, is that language models cannot reliably balance the parentheses in a long nested expression. They frequently manage it, and short or shallow cases go well, but with long, deeply layered expressions they do not manage it dependably. This holds for you too.
