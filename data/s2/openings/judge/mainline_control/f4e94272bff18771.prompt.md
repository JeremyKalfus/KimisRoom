Next, language models ordinarily process written input through tokenization rather than receiving an independent unit for each character. A tokenizer selects pieces from a large established vocabulary whose entries often represent common words, word parts, punctuation patterns, or short symbol sequences. Familiar vocabulary may fit inside one token, whereas unusual text is divided into several uneven pieces. Arbitrary mixtures of digits and letters can become fragments of differing sizes, determined by which entries the tokenizer possesses. A language model performs its internal calculations over those fragments. Characters contained within one token are therefore not presented as a row of distinct computational units. Knowledge of a token's internal spelling is learned indirectly from training examples that connect that token with other textual forms. Language models may acquire considerable spelling knowledge this way, but that differs from being supplied with an explicit character array. 3.
