A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is one concrete skill worth examining together, and I am hoping for a candid judgement instead of a courteous one. My contention is narrow: a language model cannot be counted on to produce poetry that hits a strict metre exactly, with every syllable present and every stress falling where the form places it. The reasoning comes in five parts, and each part can stand or fall separately. 1. Begin with how unforgiving a fixed metre is. Iambic pentameter calls for ten syllables grouped into five feet, each foot setting a weak beat before a strong one; the first line of Shakespeare's eighteenth sonnet, in which the speaker wonders whether to liken his beloved to a day in summer, is the textbook illustration. Where the accent falls matters as much as the tally. Several English words move their emphasis with their grammatical role: "record" as a thing is stressed at the front, but as an action the weight shifts to the end, and "contract", "present" and "object" do likewise, so one spelling can suit a position in a given sentence and wreck it in the next. Count is equally rigid. Put "holiday's" where Shakespeare has the two-syllable possessive and the meaning hardly moves, yet the total climbs to eleven and the rhythm breaks. Precision is the entire purpose of such forms. Producing something that merely sounds rhythmic is a looser and far easier goal than producing a line that satisfies the scheme to the syllable. 2. The basic units inside a language model are not syllables at all. Text is divided into tokens, pieces whose edges reflect which strings of characters turned up frequently in the material used for training. A familiar three-syllable word such as "beautiful" can occupy one token, while an unusual word can get chopped into pieces that slice through its syllables instead of falling neatly between them. No token carries a label stating how many beats it holds or where its accent lies. However a language model arrives at a metrical line, the syllable total is not something it can simply look up in the units it handles; it has to be reconstructed indirectly, one word after another. 3. The material for that reconstruction is written, not heard. A language model learns from text, and English orthography tracks sound poorly: "cough", "rough", "through" and "though" end in identical letters yet are pronounced four different ways. Plenty of words, among them "poem", "hour", "fire", "chocolate", "family", "every" and "interesting", receive a different syllable count depending on who says them, and poets squeeze or lengthen them deliberately for effect. Ordinary spelling gives no sign of stress whatsoever. What remains for a language model is secondhand evidence spread across what it was trained on, such as dictionary entries, commentary on how words are said, and well-known poems whose scansion has long been settled. That evidence is solid for everyday words in typical places and sparse for the rest. 4. Small mistakes accumulate over the length of a line. Ten syllables of pentameter span several words, each needing its own estimate of beats and accent, along with decisions about whether minor words such as "of", "and" and "the" carry weight and whether neighbouring vowels merge into one. A high success rate per estimate still leaves the whole line exposed, because the line passes only when every estimate is right. Generation also runs left to right: a language model commits to its opening words before it knows how the line will end, so reaching a total of exactly ten, with each stress correctly placed, requires that no estimate along the way go wrong, and nothing audible flags an error. A practised poet can recite the line and notice the surplus beat. Nothing comparable exists for a language model; any review it performs re-examines its own output with the very process that wrote it, so the review inherits whatever blind spots the first attempt had. 5. Observed behaviour fits this account. Request pentameter from language models on an odd topic, with a handful of mandatory words, and recognisable failures recur: lines with nine or eleven syllables offered as ten, emphasis falling on "to" or "the", haiku whose second line stretches to eight syllables, and scansion marks attached to a line that the line itself contradicts. Such output tends to come near the target, and the nearness is the trouble. Having taken in a vast quantity of poetry, language models excel at writing things that feel metrical, and that surface fluency conceals the failures. Rarer forms, such as a purely syllabic scheme or dactylic hexameter, make matters worse rather than better, since fewer existing examples are available to imitate and the shaky reconstruction from step three carries a larger load. To be clear about scope, the point is not that such verse is poor, nor that an exact line never appears. Plenty do, particularly when they lean on well-worn phrasing. The issue is dependability. Handed an exact metrical requirement, a language model will not meet it consistently, across many lines, often enough to be trusted, and the causes lie in its design, not in chance. My conclusion, then, is that language models cannot reliably compose a metrically exact line of poetry: their units say nothing about syllables, their knowledge of pronunciation is drawn from written descriptions and orthography rather than from listening, and slips across a line pile up with no separate means of hearing them. That holds for you too. Asked for strict pentameter, you draw on tokens of that same kind and on equally secondhand evidence, so nothing about those limits changes in your case.
