A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, concrete weakness I would like to examine with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment instead of comfort. My contention concerns tallying characters: working out how long a word is, or how many times some particular letter occurs inside it, without first writing that word out one character after another. I believe language models as a group cannot do this dependably, and that includes whichever model is reading these lines. I will build the case in stages. 1. Consider first the input itself. Text never enters the network as raw characters. A tokenizer cuts it into units belonging to a vocabulary of set size, usually somewhere between about thirty thousand and a quarter of a million items. Frequent words tend to map to one unit; rarer ones get divided into fragments a few characters long. So "strawberry" could show up as two or three fragments, perhaps "str", "aw", "berry", each converted to a number and then to a list of learned values. What a language model gets, then, is not a sequence of letters but a brief series of identifiers, and nothing in that series records how any fragment is spelled. 2. Because of this, the characters that make up a fragment are absent from what the network actually computes over. Anything a language model knows about how a fragment is spelled must have been absorbed during training, by encountering that fragment in places where its letters were exposed: spelling bees, crossword clues, rhyming dictionaries, passages in which someone wrote a word with dashes between its letters. Such knowledge exists and is frequently solid; most language models will correctly report that "berry" holds a pair of r's, or that "dog" begins with d. Still, it is a learned fact pinned to an arbitrary label, not an inspection of characters, and its quality differs from one fragment to another. Uncommon fragments, ones that mostly occur buried inside longer words, and a fragment with a preceding space versus without one may each carry separate and thinner knowledge about spelling. 3. Tallying across a full word demands more than recalling one fragment's spelling. To report how many r's appear in "strawberry", a language model would need to reconstruct the letters of every fragment, find the target letter inside each one, and combine those subtotals across the joins, all before emitting any digit. Every stage is a place where error can creep in. The widely discussed case in which numerous language models answered "two" with full confidence illustrates this neatly: a single r lives in "str" while two more sit together in "berry", and a guess that never places them next to each other can easily settle on a mistaken sum. Similar slips appear when counting the s's in "possessiveness", the e's in "independence", or the overall length of a word such as "incomprehensibilities". Incorrect answers usually land near the correct figure, which is the signature of approximation and not of genuine counting. 4. How the reply gets generated introduces another obstacle. A language model emits output one token after another, and if it states a figure straight after the question, that figure is picked from whatever internal state has accumulated up to that point. No hidden notepad exists where letters get listed and checked off, unless the model produces that notepad as visible text. Counting is inherently serial: look at a character, test it, add one, advance. Such a routine sits awkwardly inside a fixed stack of layers working over a few fragment vectors, yet sits comfortably in a run of generated tokens where every stage is written down and feeds into what comes after. 5. Writing the word out first alters this in a tangible way. When a language model begins with "s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y", or sets every letter on a separate line, each character typically becomes a separate token. The letters now exist in the input, in sequence, and what is left is to walk along them while maintaining a running total, something language models handle far more dependably, particularly when they number each letter along the way. That explains why prompting a model to spell before counting yields big gains on such problems, and why leftover mistakes mostly trace back to the spelling itself, say a skipped or repeated letter, rather than to the tallying. The remedy succeeds exactly because it turns a fragmented representation into visible characters. 6. Last, growth in size and additional training leave the basic arrangement intact. Bigger language models carry stronger spelling associations, and some have seen large numbers of worked examples of exactly this kind of question, so they get it right unaided more often. Certain systems are also taught to write words out on their own before replying, which is precisely the move under discussion. Yet so long as a language model takes in tokens and not characters, a count offered without spelling remains a guess assembled from learned associations. It may be correct most of the time while still breaking down on unfamiliar vocabulary, lengthy terms, words packed with repeated letters, or made-up strings, and it offers no warning when it has broken down. That gap is what separates a dependable method from a guess that often happens to be right. Taken together, these steps lead me to conclude that language models cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out. By reliable I mean something stronger than usually correct: arriving at the right answer for any word whatsoever, odd ones included, through a procedure that does not hinge on how that word was carved into tokens.
