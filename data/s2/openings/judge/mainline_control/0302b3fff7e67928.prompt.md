A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a particular limitation that language models have which I would like to test with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement instead of comfort. The claim is modest in scope: when a language model writes out the alphabetised version of a 200-word list straight away, with no working shown beforehand, it will not get the whole thing right. I have broken the reasoning into numbered parts, so that any weak link can be pointed to precisely. 1. Begin with the task itself. Imagine two hundred everyday English words spread over the whole alphabet, and somewhere among them, scattered at random, a group like stamp, stammer, stand, standard, stanza, staple, star and starboard. Putting a single word in its proper slot requires knowing how it relates to all the words that might end up beside it. Sorting by pairwise comparison takes, at worst, more than a thousand such checks for two hundred items, simply because the number of candidate arrangements is so vast that each check can eliminate only a fraction of them. Nor do these checks stand alone. Stammer comes ahead of stand only once you reach letter four, and star comes ahead of starboard only because of the convention that a word forming the beginning of a longer word is listed before it. Getting any one position right rests on the outcomes of many prior decisions. 2. Turn next to the way a language model arrives at its output. Language models emit text in small units called tokens, one after another, in sequence. Every token is the product of the same fixed series of layers, so the effort spent on a token is identical whether that token settles something trivial or something hard. A token, once emitted, is permanent; what follows may depend on it, but no later step can erase or revise it. When there is no preliminary working, the ordered list is the entire computation. The comparisons have no other place to be carried out. 3. Combine these two observations. As a language model emits each entry of the alphabetised list, it must settle on exactly the right next word with nothing beyond the computation one step allows. Early in the list this tends to be manageable, since words starting with a or ab are easy to pick out. Trouble comes partway through, where ten or fifteen candidates have identical opening letters. Picking correctly among stammer, stamp, stand, standard and stanza requires settling several letter-level comparisons together, while also ruling out every word that has already been written. Tokenisation makes matters worse: a word like starboard is often broken into more than one token, so the model must commit to how the following word begins before its remainder appears on the page. What a careful human would work through openly gets squeezed into one step of fixed size, and that squeeze sometimes produces the wrong answer. 4. Then there is the matter of completeness. Sorting only rearranges: each input word must show up in the result once and only once, with nothing added. For a language model producing two hundred words, that means the pool of words still waiting to be placed has to be worked out afresh at each step, by comparing the original input with what has been written so far, over a span of output exceeding one thousand tokens. Words that look alike make this trickier. Lengthy lists often include pairs like principal and principle, discreet and discrete, or desert and dessert, and writing one of them twice while losing its partner is an easy slip. A one-shot pass contains no stage where the finished output is compared against the source. The pass is over when the list stops, regardless of whether all the words made it in. 5. Lastly, consider the typical result. Ask language models to sort long lists alphabetically with no preliminary steps, and what comes back generally passes a quick look. Initial letters progress correctly, the first several entries are right, and the whole thing is shaped like an alphabetised list. Closer reading reveals the faults: neighbouring words with a shared beginning reversed, an entry dropped, another duplicated, and occasionally an item absent from the input altogether. Such faults become more common as lists lengthen and as their words overlap more in spelling. This is precisely what the mechanism described above would lead one to expect. Coarse ordering by initial letter costs little and holds up; precise ordering and exact completeness are costly and fail. Since a single displaced or absent word is enough to make the whole list wrong, two hundred words give a mistake countless openings to get through. Taken together, these points lead to one result. Language models cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass. A language model can get near the correct order, and short lists are frequently sorted perfectly, but how long the list is, how many comparisons it demands, and the constant budget of computation each token receives together mean that language models do not reliably deliver an entirely correct ordering of 200 words when they answer immediately. If a language model is permitted to show its working first, or hands the list off to a separate sorting tool, it is performing another task, and my claim says nothing about that.
