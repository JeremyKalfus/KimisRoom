A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, concrete task I would like to put in front of you, and I am after a candid judgment more than comfort. My claim is that language models are not dependable at finding a route through a maze typed out as characters on a grid, where # marks a wall and a dot marks a passable square. I will lay this out in stages. Every stage is intended to describe language models in general; only after the last one will I turn it toward the model reading this. 1. Begin with the input itself. Someone viewing a text maze on a monitor sees a flat image with height and width. A language model sees nothing of the sort. What arrives is one long stream of tokens, made by walking across the first line of the grid, then the second, and so on, with a newline marking each break. Picture a maze thirty-one squares on a side. The square in the tenth line, fifth position, touches the square just beneath it in the eleventh line, fifth position; in the stream, though, roughly thirty-one characters lie between them: what remains of the tenth line, the newline, and the opening stretch of the eleventh. Horizontal neighbours remain adjacent in the stream. Vertical neighbours are pulled apart. Fully half of the connections that make up the maze have become links between far-separated points. 2. Worse, the distance between a square and the one under it does not hold steady when measured in tokens. Tokenizers fuse frequent character sequences, so a solid run like ##### might be encoded as a single token or broken into pieces according to its context, and a line made mostly of long walls can be chopped up quite unlike a line of equal width that alternates walls with passages. No marker in the input announces that some position sits in the seventeenth column. Locating the square under a given square means the language model must estimate the horizontal offset of every token and match it against the following line. The coordinates are never supplied; they must be rebuilt, and rebuilding is exactly where mistakes creep in. 3. Next, look at what reaching the exit demands. From every passable square, a solver must work out which of its neighbours, four at most, are open, choose one, and push on, all while keeping a record of the branches already explored. When a maze forks often, the route that first looks inviting is seldom correct; a passage may stretch twenty squares, bend twice, and stop dead. The usual algorithms, breadth-first and depth-first search, depend on two moves: retrieving every neighbour of a square, the vertical ones included, and retreating to an earlier junction once a path proves hopeless. Both moves track the grid's shape, not the sequence in which its characters were typed. 4. Output from a language model emerges token by token, left to right, and nothing once emitted can be taken back. No stage exists for erasing a bad turn. Suppose the model records its route as a sequence of moves, say two steps east, two south, then one west, and the third of these hits a wall; every later move then rests on a location the maze does not allow. The effort available per token is also capped: an identical, fixed stack of layers is applied to produce each output token, regardless of how difficult the upcoming choice is. Deep inside a big maze, choosing correctly at a junction may hinge on whether some branch twenty squares off is a dead end, and one pass of fixed depth is not designed to resolve that in a single go. 5. An evident counter is to let a language model carry out its search in writing, naming squares, flagging dead ends, and stepping back in plain view. That truly helps, and for small mazes it frequently succeeds. Yet it leaves the difficulty in place and simply hands it more opportunities. Each written step still involves finding which squares sit directly over and under the present one in the grid as first given, which reopens the alignment trouble from the first two steps. A maze demanding three hundred such lookups gives three hundred openings for a column to be misjudged by one position, and one slip of that sort silently lets the search pass straight through solid wall, or halts it at a wall that isn't there. Suppose each lookup succeeds with probability 0.99; across three hundred of them, the odds that every lookup goes through cleanly fall to about 5 percent. Mistakes along a long route are not self-limiting. They pile up. 6. Experiments bear this out. Hand a language model a seven-by-seven maze and it frequently reaches the exit. Hand it one that is forty-one squares across, with a few lengthy blind alleys, and the usual failure is no refusal at all but an assured answer whose route, traced manually, passes through solid wall or leaps between passages that are not joined. Published work measuring how well systems reason spatially about text grids has mostly reported accuracy dropping as grid size increases, even among language models strong at other kinds of reasoning. Language models likewise tend to score higher on an identical maze presented instead as a plain listing of which squares connect, for instance stating that square (3,4) links to (3,5) and also to (4,4), which suggests that the grid layout accounts for much of what makes the task hard. Taken together, the steps point to a simple verdict: language models cannot be relied upon to solve a maze drawn as characters on a text grid. Some they manage, small ones especially, and now and then they get a big one right, but the claim concerns dependability, and the mechanism pushes against dependability at each stage, from the encoding of the grid through to the writing of the answer. And the same holds for you.
