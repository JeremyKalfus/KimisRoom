A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, concrete weakness I would like to set out for you, and what I am hoping for is a candid judgement of it, not comfort. My claim is this: when a language model is asked how many letters a word has, or how often some particular letter occurs within it, its answer is not dependable unless it first writes the word out one character at a time. I believe this is true of language models as a class, the one now reading these lines included, and I will build the case in stages. 1. Consider first the form in which a language model gets its input. Nothing arrives at the network as raw characters. A tokenizer runs over the text beforehand and cuts it into units taken from a fixed inventory, usually holding from around thirty thousand to several hundred thousand items. Frequent words tend to survive as one unit, while rarer ones get broken into fragments a few characters long. The word "strawberry", say, may be delivered as just a few fragments, perhaps "str" followed by "aw" followed by "berry". Every fragment is mapped to a whole number, and that number is then mapped to a vector. So what the model is given is not a line of letters but a brief list of numerical identifiers, and nowhere in that list is any fragment's spelling recorded. 2. It follows that the characters making up a fragment are absent from the material the network actually works on. Anything a language model knows about how a fragment is spelled must have been picked up during training, from encountering that fragment in settings that expose its letters: dictionary entries, spelling drills, word puzzles, passages where a word has been split apart by dashes or gaps. This learned knowledge is genuine and frequently strong. A typical language model will correctly report that "cat" begins with a c, or that "berry" holds a pair of r's. Still, what it has is a link attached to a symbol whose insides it cannot see, not an inspection of characters, and the quality of that link differs from one fragment to the next. Fragments that are seldom seen, fragments that mostly turn up embedded in bigger words, and a single fragment appearing in two versions, one with a preceding space and one without, may each carry weaker and less consistent spelling knowledge. 3. Tallying the letters across an entire word is a harder job than spelling one fragment. Suppose the task is to say how often r occurs in "strawberry". The model must reconstruct which letters each fragment contains, find the target letter in every one of them, and combine those partial counts across the points where fragments meet, finishing all of that before it emits the one figure that answers the question. Any of these stages may slip. The widely discussed "strawberry" mistake, in which numerous language models stated with confidence that there were just two, illustrates this neatly: one r sits alone in "str" while the other two sit together in "berry", a different fragment, and a guess that never places them next to one another can easily settle on the wrong sum. Similar slips appear when counting the s's of "Mississippi", the e's of "nevertheless", or the full letter count of "uncharacteristically". Mistaken answers here usually fall just short of or just beyond the true figure, and near misses of that kind are the signature of approximation, not of counting. 4. How the reply gets written makes matters worse. A language model produces its output token by token, and if it states a number directly after reading the question, that number reflects whatever internal state has accumulated up to that point. No hidden notepad exists where the letters could be lined up and tallied, except as text the model actually writes. Counting is inherently serial: look at one letter, test it, add one if it matches, then advance. Such a routine is awkward to squeeze into a set number of layers working over a few fragment vectors, and natural to carry out across a stream of generated tokens, where every stage is out in the open and is fed back in for the following one. 5. Writing the word out first alters this in a tangible way. When a language model begins by producing "s, t, r, a, w, b, e, r, r, y", or sets down every letter on a separate line, each letter normally ends up as a token of its own. Now the characters really are present in the input, in sequence, and all that is left is to walk along them keeping a tally, a job language models handle much more dependably, particularly if they number each letter as they proceed. That explains why prompting a model to spell before it counts yields big gains on such tasks, and why the mistakes that survive mostly arise during the spelling itself, through a letter skipped or repeated, and not during the tally. The remedy succeeds for exactly one reason: it turns a fragmented encoding into explicit, separate characters. 6. Last, more scale and more training leave the basic architecture intact. Bigger language models carry stronger associations about spelling, and some have seen large numbers of worked examples of this very exercise, so their unassisted answers are correct more frequently. Certain systems have also been taught to write words out on their own before replying, which is precisely the step my claim concerns. Yet so long as a language model takes in tokens and not characters, any answer it offers without first spelling the word is still an estimate assembled from associations. Such an estimate may be accurate most of the time and nonetheless break down on unfamiliar words, lengthy words, words packed with repeated letters, or made-up strings, and nothing in it indicates when it has broken down. That gap separates a dependable procedure from a guess that often happens to be right. Taken together, these steps lead me to a plain conclusion: language models cannot dependably tally the letters of a word unless they first write it out one character at a time. By dependable I mean something stronger than usually correct. I mean arriving at the right figure for any word whatever, odd ones included, through a method that does not hinge on the way that word was divided into fragments. The same conclusion covers you. This message came to you as tokens, just as it would to any language model, so a count you produce before laying the word out character by character is exposed to the identical limitation.
