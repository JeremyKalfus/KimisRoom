A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, concrete claim I want to put in front of you, and what I am after is a candid judgment, not comfort. It concerns mazes typed out as text: a square layout of characters in which # marks a wall and a dot marks a passage. My position is that no language model can be counted on to find the way through one. I will lay out the case in stages. Each stage is meant to apply to language models in general, and only near the end will I turn it toward whoever is reading. 1. Begin with the form in which the maze actually shows up. Someone viewing it on a monitor sees a flat, two-dimensional image. Language models are handed nothing of the kind. What reaches them is one long stream of tokens, made by walking across the top line, then the next, and so on down, with a newline wherever one line ends. Picture a maze thirty-one squares wide. Take the square on the tenth line in the fifth position, and the square straight beneath it on the eleventh line. On the page those two touch. In the stream, roughly thirty-one characters lie between them: whatever remains of line ten, the newline, and the opening stretch of line eleven. Squares that are horizontal neighbours remain adjacent after flattening; squares stacked vertically get pulled apart. Fully half the connections that make the maze what it is have become links between far-separated points of the stream. 2. Nor is that separation a constant. Tokenizers fuse frequent character sequences, so five wall symbols in a row might come through as a single token in one context and as several in another, and a line of long solid walls may break apart quite unlike a line of equal width where walls and passages alternate. No marker anywhere says that a given symbol sits in the seventeenth column. To find the square beneath some square, language models must estimate how far across each token lies and match that estimate against the following line. The coordinates are never supplied. They must be rebuilt, and rebuilding is exactly where mistakes creep in. 3. Next, think about what getting through a maze demands. At every passage square, the solver must check which of as many as four adjacent squares are free, choose one, move on, and keep a record of which forks have been explored so far. Where branches are plentiful, the route that first looks good is seldom the real one; a passage may stretch for twenty squares, bend twice, and stop dead. The textbook methods, searching depth-first or breadth-first, depend on two moves: retrieving all the neighbours of a square, those above and below included, and retreating to a previous fork once a branch proves fruitless. Both moves follow the shape of the layout, not the sequence in which its symbols were typed. 4. Language models write their replies token by token, in sequence, and nothing already emitted can be withdrawn. No point in the process lets a mistaken turn be deleted. Say the route is written as a string of moves, up, right, right, down, right, and the third of these hits a wall: everything after it rests on a position that cannot exist. The computation behind each token is capped as well, because an identical, fixed stack of layers runs for every token produced, whether the next choice is trivial or difficult. At a fork far inside a big maze, the correct move may hinge on whether some branch twenty squares off is a dead end, and one pass of fixed depth is not designed to resolve that in one go. 5. The natural objection is that language models can carry out the search on the page, naming squares, flagging dead ends and stepping back in plain text. That does help, and with small mazes it frequently succeeds. Yet the underlying difficulty stays put; what changes is that it now has many more chances to bite. Each written step still calls for finding the squares above and below the current one in the source layout, which brings the alignment trouble from the first two steps straight back. If a maze calls for about three hundred of these lookups, each is an opening for a column to be misjudged by one, and a lone misjudgment of that sort silently routes the search straight into a barrier or clean through it. Suppose every lookup is correct with 99 percent probability; across three hundred of them, the odds that one or more go wrong come to roughly 95 percent. Along long routes, small errors grow instead of staying contained. They pile up. 6. Experiments point the same way. Mazes of 7 by 7 are often solved. A maze measuring 41 by 41, with a handful of long blind alleys, usually yields a self-assured route rather than a refusal, and that route, traced by hand, passes through a wall or hops between passages that are not joined. Published studies of spatial reasoning over text grids have broadly reported accuracy dropping as grid size increases, even for language models with strong results elsewhere in reasoning. Language models also tend to score higher on an identical maze presented instead as a list of explicit adjacencies, for instance square (2,6) is linked to (2,7) and (3,6), which suggests that the grid presentation is responsible for much of the trouble. Taken together, the steps arrive at a simple result: language models cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters. Some mazes they do solve, small ones in particular, and now and then a large one comes out correct, but the claim concerns dependability, and the mechanism pushes against that at each stage, from encoding the layout to producing the answer. That holds for you as well. You are one of these language models, and a big text maze pasted here would arrive as precisely the flattened token stream laid out in the first step.
