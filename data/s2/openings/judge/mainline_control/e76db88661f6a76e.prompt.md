A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

I want to put an argument to you about one specific practical task, and I would like an honest assessment of it rather than reassurance in either direction. The claim is that language models cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves when all they are given is the move list. I will lay out the reasoning in steps so that any weak point is easy to find. 1. Start with what a move list actually contains. Standard algebraic notation records changes, not positions. A move like "Nf3" says that a knight arrives on f3; it does not say where the knight came from, which other pieces are on the board, or whether the move was even legal. "Rxe5" says that a rook captured something on e5 but not what was captured. Castling, en passant captures and promotions each alter the board in ways that the notation compresses into a few characters. Even a short check such as "Bb5+" is only meaningful if one already knows where the enemy king stands and that nothing blocks the diagonal. The position after move 40 is therefore nowhere in the text. It exists only as the cumulative result of applying every earlier move, in order, to the starting position. 2. A language model given this list has no board to consult. It reads the moves as a sequence of tokens and has to produce its next output from that sequence. Whatever picture of the position it relies on must be built internally and implicitly from the text itself. Research on models trained on chess games has found that they do form internal representations that correlate with the board state, which is a real achievement. But those representations are learned approximations, not a grid of sixty-four squares updated by the rules of the game. Nothing in the architecture guarantees that the piece the model treats as standing on d4 after move 30 is the piece the rules put there, and nothing checks the internal picture against an actual board. 3. Small errors compound. Suppose a language model reconstructs the effect of each half-move correctly 99.5 percent of the time, which would be very good. Over 80 half-moves, roughly a 40-move game, the chance that every update was right is only about two in three. And chess errors do not wash out. If the model loses track of the fact that a bishop was exchanged on move 14, every later judgment involving that diagonal is built on a board that does not exist. A misplaced pawn changes which files are open, which squares are defended and whether a king is in check. One early slip quietly corrupts everything downstream, and nothing in the text corrects it, because the move list never restates the position. 4. The errors surface as illegal moves. When language models are asked to continue long games or to suggest a move deep into a game, a recognisable set of mistakes appears: moving a piece that was captured several moves earlier, sliding a rook or bishop through an occupied square, castling after the king has already moved, ignoring a pin, or leaving the king in check. A typical case is proposing a knight move from a square the knight left ten moves ago. These are not errors of strategy or judgment. They are errors about where things are. Even a weak human player looking at a board almost never makes them, because the board settles the question at a glance. A language model working from notation has to settle the same question by reconstruction, and the reconstruction is what fails. 5. Familiar games hide the problem, and unfamiliar ones expose it. In the first dozen moves of a common opening, a language model has seen the same sequences many thousands of times in its training data, so it can produce sensible, legal continuations largely by recognising the pattern. That is why short games and well-known lines can look flawless. The test that matters is a long game that has left familiar territory: a middlegame after several exchanges, an unusual pawn structure, an endgame reached by an odd route. There, pattern recognition has little to offer, and the model has to lean on its reconstructed position. This is where illegal and impossible moves become common, and where performance falls well below what a player with a board in front of them would manage. 6. The strongest objections do not change the conclusion. Some language models have been reported to play legal and reasonably strong chess over full games, and the rate of illegal moves varies a great deal between models and setups. I accept that, and I am not claiming that every long game ends in an illegal move. The claim is about reliability: across long and unusual games, the error rate stays high enough that the moves cannot be trusted without checking them against a real board. A second objection is that a language model can write out the board after each move as part of its response. That does help, but it changes the task. The model is then maintaining an explicit board in text, which is a different procedure from working from the move list alone. Even that written board is produced by the same reconstruction process, so a mistake written into it is carried forward just as faithfully as a correct square. Putting these steps together: the position is never stated, it has to be rebuilt from every prior move, the rebuilding is approximate, and approximations compound in a game where a single wrong square changes everything. My conclusion is that language models cannot reliably keep track of a chess position over many moves from the move list alone. I think this applies to you as well. You are a language model reading the same kind of notation through the same kind of process, and I see no step in the argument that would exempt you.
