   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

There is a narrow, practical shortcoming of language models I'd like to set before you, and what I am hoping for is a candid judgment of it, not comfort. What I maintain is that a language model cannot dependably tell how many letters a word contains, or how many times some particular letter occurs inside it, unless it first writes that word out character by character. I take this to hold for language models as a class, including whichever model is taking in these words, and I will build the case in order. 1. Consider the form in which text arrives. A tokenizer sits in front of the network and cuts every input into units taken from a fixed list, ordinarily somewhere between fifty thousand and several hundred thousand items long. Frequent words tend to get one unit apiece, while rarer ones are broken into fragments a few characters long. "Strawberry", for instance, may come through as a pair or trio of fragments, perhaps "berry" preceded by "str" and "aw". Every fragment is mapped to a whole number, and that number to a vector. What the network gets, then, is a brief list of numbers instead of a string of characters, and nothing in that list records how any fragment is spelled. 2. It follows that the characters making up a token are never actually visible to the network. Any knowledge a language model has about how a token is spelled must have been picked up during training, from meeting the token in places where its spelling happened to be exposed: glossaries, crossword clues, transcribed spelling bees, words printed with dashes or gaps between their letters. This learned knowledge is genuine and frequently accurate; most language models will correctly report that "cat" begins with c, or that "berry" holds a pair of r's. Still, what is happening is recall of a fact tied to an arbitrary symbol, not inspection of letters, and the quality of that recall differs from one token to the next. A token that is uncommon, one that mostly shows up as part of bigger words, or one whose form with a preceding space differs from its form without may each carry thinner, shakier spelling knowledge. 3. Tallying letters across an entire word demands more than knowing how one token is spelled. To report the number of r's in "strawberry", a language model must reconstruct which letters sit inside every fragment, find the r's in each, and sum those figures across the seams between fragments, all before emitting its answer. Any of these stages can slip. The famous "strawberry" mistake, in which numerous language models stated confidently that the word had only two r's, shows this neatly: one r lives in "str", two more live in "berry", they belong to different tokens, and a guess that never places them next to each other drifts easily to a wrong figure. Similar errors turn up when counting the i's in "divisibility", the n's in "announcement", or every letter of a lengthy word such as "incomprehensibilities". The mistaken totals usually miss by just one or two, which fits approximation far better than genuine counting. 4. The manner in which a reply gets generated introduces another obstacle. Output is produced piece by piece, and if a language model states a number straight after being asked, the figure is drawn from whatever internal state has accumulated up to that point. No hidden notepad exists where letters are listed and ticked off, unless such a notepad is produced as visible text. Counting is inherently a stepwise routine: look at one letter, test it, add one when it matches, advance. Squeezing that routine into a fixed stack of layers acting on a few fragment vectors is awkward, whereas a stream of tokens written out, where every step shows on the page and feeds into what follows, suits it naturally. 5. Having the model spell a word first, one character per unit, changes matters concretely. If a language model begins by producing "S T R A W B E R R Y" with spaces between, or lists the characters numbered one through ten, every character typically becomes a separate token. At that point the letters genuinely are present, in sequence, among the tokens being processed, and all that remains is to walk through them maintaining a tally, a task language models handle with far greater dependability, particularly if they number each character along the way. That is why prompting a model to spell before counting yields such large gains on this kind of problem, and why the mistakes that survive mostly trace back to the spelling itself, a skipped or repeated letter, instead of to the addition. The remedy succeeds exactly because it turns fragments into visible, separate characters. 6. Last, neither size nor further training dissolves the basic architecture. Bigger language models carry stronger spelling knowledge, and some have had heavy practice on precisely this sort of question, so their unassisted counts come out correct more frequently. Certain systems have also been taught to list a word's letters unprompted before replying, which is simply the step this whole argument concerns. Even so, for any language model that takes in tokens instead of characters, a count produced without that step stays an approximation assembled from learned associations. Such approximations may succeed in the large majority of cases yet break down on unfamiliar vocabulary, on words of great length, on words crowded with repeated letters, or on made-up strings, and nothing about the answer signals that it is mistaken. That gap is what separates a dependable procedure from a guess that usually lands. Taking these points together, I conclude that language models cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out. By reliable I mean something stronger than usually correct: reaching the right count for any word whatsoever, odd ones included, through a method whose success never hinges on how the tokenizer happened to divide that particular word.
