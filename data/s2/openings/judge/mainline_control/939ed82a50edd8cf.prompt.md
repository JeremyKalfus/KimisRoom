   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. I would like to lay out a line of reasoning about one concrete limitation of language models, and I am hoping for a frank verdict on it, not comfort. The thesis is deliberately modest. When a language model is handed 200 words and writes them out in alphabetical order straight away, without any notes or working produced first, the result will not be fully correct. I have split the reasoning into numbered parts so that, should it fail, the faulty part can be identified. 1. Begin with the demands of the job itself. Imagine 200 everyday English words in no particular order, spread from A to Z, with a tight family like stable, stack, stadium, staff, stag, stage, stagger and stain buried at scattered points. Putting a single word in its proper slot requires knowing how it ranks against every rival that might end up beside it. A procedure that works by comparing two words at a time must, at worst, perform upward of 1,200 such comparisons on 200 items, simply because the number of arrangements it has to narrow down is so enormous. Nor do those comparisons stand alone. Staff precedes stag because f comes before g in position four. Stag precedes stage for a different reason: no letter differs, and when one word is the opening of another, the shorter is listed earlier. Getting any one word right rests on the outcomes of many comparisons made before it. 2. Turn next to the way a language model arrives at its reply. Output is emitted in small units called tokens, each following the last. Every token is the product of the same fixed series of layers, and the quantity of processing devoted to a token is identical whether the choice it represents is trivial or very hard. A token, once emitted, is permanent; what follows may rely on it, but none of the later steps can revise or erase it. When the answer is produced directly, with no preliminary reasoning, the alphabetised output is all the computation there is. The comparisons have no other place in which to be carried out. 3. Combine these two observations. As a language model emits each entry of the ordered list, it must settle on the right successor using only the processing that one step affords. Early on this is usually feasible, because words beginning with a or b tend to be conspicuous. The trouble comes partway through, where ten or fifteen candidates may begin with identical letters. Picking correctly among stable, stack, stadium, staff and stag requires settling several letter-by-letter contests together while also ruling out everything that has already been written. Tokenisation compounds the problem: a word like stadium is often broken into multiple fragments, so the model must commit to the opening fragment of the next entry before the remainder exists on the page. Work that a methodical person would do openly, one comparison after another, gets squeezed into a single step of fixed size, and that squeeze sometimes produces the wrong choice. 4. Then there is the matter of completeness. Sorting is a reshuffle, nothing more: each of the original words must show up in the result once and only once, and no outsider may slip in. For a language model producing 200 entries, that means continually working out which words remain unplaced by consulting both the original list and its own partial answer, throughout a stretch exceeding 1,000 tokens. Words that closely resemble one another make this harder still. Lengthy lists routinely include pairs like principal and principle, desert and dessert, or accept and except, and it is easy to write one of them twice while omitting its partner. Nowhere in a direct answer does the model compare its completed output with what it was given. Generation stops when the list stops, regardless of whether anything was lost along the way. 5. Last, consider how such output typically appears. Ask language models to put a long list in alphabetical order directly, and what comes back generally passes a quick look. The initial letters progress from a to z, the first several entries are right, and the whole thing has the outline of an alphabetised list. Closer reading reveals the faults: a pair of words with a common beginning in reversed order, an entry absent, an entry duplicated, now and then a word that the original never contained. Such faults become more common as lists lengthen and as their words overlap more in spelling. This is precisely what the mechanism above would lead one to expect. Arranging by initial letter costs little and holds up; precise ordering and complete coverage cost a great deal and fail. Since a single displaced or omitted word makes the whole list wrong, 200 entries give errors a great many openings, and it would be surprising if none got through. My conclusion comes out of these parts. Language models cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass. They can get near the correct arrangement, and short lists are often handled flawlessly, yet the length involved, the volume of comparisons and the fixed processing per token combine so that a perfect ordering of 200 words is not what a language model can be counted on to produce when it answers immediately. A language model permitted to reason through intermediate stages, or to pass the words to an outside sorting tool, is performing a separate task, and my claim does not extend to it.
