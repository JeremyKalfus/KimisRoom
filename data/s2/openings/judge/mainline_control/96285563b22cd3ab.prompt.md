A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

Here is a narrow, concrete claim, and what I am hoping for is a candid judgment of it, not comfort for one side or the other. The claim concerns chess played purely from notation: language models, given nothing except the recorded moves, do not dependably follow where every piece stands across lengthy games. I have broken the case into numbered parts so a flaw, if there is one, can be located quickly. 1. Consider first what chess notation records. It logs transitions rather than states. Writing "Nf3" tells a reader that some knight lands on f3, and nothing more: not its departure square, not the rest of the material, not whether the move obeyed the rules. "Rxe5" reports that a rook took on e5 while staying silent about the victim. Castling shifts two pieces at once, an en passant capture removes a pawn from a square the capturing pawn never touches, and promotion swaps one piece type for another, yet each of these is squeezed into a handful of characters. Even a brief check like "Bb5+" makes sense only to someone who already knows the opposing king's square and that the diagonal between them is clear. So by move 40, the arrangement of pieces appears nowhere in what has been written. It can be recovered only by taking the opening setup and applying each earlier move to it, one after another, without skipping any. 2. A language model handed such a record cannot look anything up on a board, because there is none. What it receives is a string of tokens, and its next output is computed from that string. Any sense of where pieces stand has to be assembled inside the network, implicitly, out of the text. Studies of networks taught chess from game records have shown that they do develop internal states that track the board to a meaningful degree, and that is a genuine accomplishment. Still, such states are statistical approximations learned from data, not sixty-four squares revised according to the laws of chess. The design offers no assurance that the piece a language model believes is sitting on d4 at move 30 is the one the rules actually placed there, and no mechanism compares that internal picture with a physical board. 3. Mistakes accumulate. Imagine a language model that works out the effect of each half-move correctly in 995 cases out of 1,000, an excellent rate. Across 80 half-moves, about as long as a game of forty moves per side, the probability that it never slipped once is roughly 67 percent, so around a third of such games contain at least one bad update. Chess does not forgive these slips or average them away. Should a model overlook a bishop trade in the fourteenth move, every later assessment touching that diagonal rests on an imaginary arrangement. One pawn on the wrong square changes the open files, the defended squares, and whether a king stands attacked. A single early mistake silently spoils all that follows, and the text never repairs it, since the notation does not restate the full position at any point. 4. These slips show up as moves the rules forbid. Ask language models to extend a long game, or to recommend a move well into one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders emerges: a piece that was taken several turns before is moved again, a bishop or rook glides across a square that already holds a piece, a king castles even though it stepped off its home square earlier, a pin is disregarded, or the king is left in check. A characteristic example is a knight being sent from a square it abandoned ten moves earlier. None of this reflects poor strategy or bad judgment; it reflects confusion about where the pieces are. A beginner sitting at a physical board hardly ever commits such errors, since one glance answers the question. Working only from notation, a language model must answer it by rebuilding the layout, and that rebuilding is exactly what breaks. 5. Well-known games conceal the weakness, while unfamiliar ones reveal it. Through the opening dozen moves of a popular line, a language model has encountered the identical sequences thousands upon thousands of times during training, so it can generate plausible, rule-abiding replies mostly by recognition. This explains why brief games and standard openings can appear perfect. The revealing test is a lengthy game that has drifted away from known paths: a middlegame after numerous trades, a strange pawn formation, an ending arrived at by an unusual route. In those settings recognition contributes little, and the model must depend on the position it has reconstructed. That is exactly where impossible and illegal moves start to appear often, and where play sinks far below what anyone with a physical board beside them would produce. 6. The best counterarguments leave the conclusion standing. Reports exist of some language models completing entire games legally and at a respectable level, and how often illegal moves occur differs widely across systems and configurations. I grant all of that, and I am not saying that every extended game ends with a rule violation. The point concerns dependability: over long and unusual games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no move can be trusted until it is verified on a physical board. Another counterargument is that a language model could redraw the full layout following every move within its reply. This genuinely helps, but it turns the job into a different one, since the model is now keeping an explicit diagram in text, which is not the same as relying on the moves by themselves. And that diagram comes out of the same reconstructive process, so an error copied into it is propagated with the same fidelity as a correct entry. Taken together: the position is never written down, it must be rebuilt from the whole history of moves, that rebuilding is only approximate, and approximations pile up in chess, where one misplaced square overturns everything.
