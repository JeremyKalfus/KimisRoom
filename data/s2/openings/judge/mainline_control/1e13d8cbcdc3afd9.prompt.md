A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

I would like you to weigh a narrow, concrete claim, and I am hoping for a frank judgment, not comfort and not alarm. The claim concerns whether language models can follow the state of a chess game across a long run of moves when the written notation is their only input. I have split the case into numbered parts so that any flaw can be pinned to a particular place. 1. Consider first what chess notation records. Each entry describes an event, a single change, rather than a snapshot of the board. When the text reads "Nf3", all it tells a reader is that some knight has landed on f3. Its departure square goes unmentioned, as does the arrangement of every other piece, and nothing in those three characters confirms that the move obeyed the rules. "Rxe5" announces that a rook took something on e5 while leaving the victim unnamed. Promotions, castling and en passant each reshape the board considerably, yet notation squeezes each of them into a handful of symbols. A check written as "Bb5+" makes sense only to someone who already knows where the opposing king sits and that the diagonal between them is clear. By the fortieth move, then, the arrangement of pieces appears nowhere on the page. It is implied only by running every preceding move, in sequence, forward from the opening setup. 2. A language model handed such a record has nothing physical to look at. It takes in the moves as a stream of tokens and must generate whatever comes next from that stream. Any sense of where the pieces stand has to be assembled inside the network, implicitly, out of the text. Studies of networks trained on game records show that they do develop internal states tracking the board to a measurable degree, and that is a genuine accomplishment. Still, such states are approximations picked up during training. They are not sixty-four cells revised according to the laws of chess. The design offers no assurance that whatever piece a language model associates with d4 at move 30 matches the piece the rules have placed there, and no mechanism compares that inner picture with a real board. 3. Mistakes accumulate rather than cancel. Imagine a language model that gets the effect of each half-move right 99.5 percent of the time, an impressive figure. Across 80 half-moves, about a forty-move game, the probability of an unbroken run of correct updates falls to roughly 67 percent. Chess gives an error no way to fade. Should the model forget that a bishop was traded off at move 14, every later conclusion touching that diagonal rests on an imaginary board. One pawn on the wrong square alters which lines are open, what is defended, and whether either king stands in check. A single early lapse silently spoils all that follows, and the text never repairs it, since notation does not pause to describe the board again. 4. These lapses show up as moves the rules forbid. Ask language models to carry on a lengthy game, or to propose a move far into one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders turns up: a piece that left the board several turns back is moved again, a bishop or rook passes through a square something already occupies, castling happens even though the king stepped away earlier, a pin is overlooked, or a king is left exposed to attack. A representative example is a suggested knight jump from a square that knight abandoned ten moves before. None of this reflects poor planning or bad evaluation. It is confusion about location. A beginner sitting at a real board rarely commits such errors, since one look answers where everything is. A language model reading notation must resolve it by rebuilding the position, and the rebuilding is where things break. 5. Well-trodden games mask the weakness, while strange ones reveal it. The revealing case is a long game that has wandered off the familiar path: a middlegame thinned out by trades, a peculiar pawn formation, an ending arrived at by an unusual road. The model is now keeping an explicit diagram in its own text, which is not the same as working purely from notation.
