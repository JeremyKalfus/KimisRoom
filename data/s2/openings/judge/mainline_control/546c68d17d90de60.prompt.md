   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. There is a narrow, concrete claim I would like to test with you, and a frank evaluation matters more to me than comfort. My position is that reversing lengthy character sequences is something language models do not manage dependably, and that the model on the receiving end of these words is no exception. I have broken the case into numbered parts so that a flaw, if there is one, can be pointed to precisely. 1. Start with what success looks like. To reverse a sequence is to output every original symbol in mirror-image order, with nothing else changed. Feed in p4Wz8sLe1c and the sole acceptable reply is c1eLs8zW4p. No symbol may appear that was absent, none may vanish, and none may shift position other than through that single flip. Since the job is entirely rule-bound and admits only one right answer, anybody can verify an attempt within moments using basic software. This strictness is what makes it a harsh benchmark. Close does not count: if a three-hundred-symbol sequence comes back with two adjacent characters traded, the attempt has failed, much as a phone number flipped in one place reaches a stranger. 2. Next, think about the form in which text arrives. Language models do not read letter by letter. Before any computation happens, the input is segmented into tokens: pieces taken from a fixed inventory containing many thousands of entries, assembled so that common sequences get packed into one unit apiece. A familiar word frequently maps onto one piece; an uncommon one may be divided into a couple of segments; a jumble of letters and digits gets carved into uneven bits, each spanning anywhere from a lone character to roughly four. These pieces are what language models actually process. The letters within any piece are never presented as distinct inputs. Whatever a language model knows about how a piece is spelled was absorbed sideways while it was trained, from countless passages where that piece sat near text written out in full. 3. Third, reversing slices right through those divisions. Segmentation is lopsided: the pieces that compose a sequence read in the usual direction are, in general, not mirror images of the pieces that compose it backwards. Consider "tokenization", which a vocabulary might store as "token" followed by "ization". Spelled backwards it becomes "noitazinekot", and no vocabulary learned from normal writing would hold "noitazi" or "nekot" as units; instead the backwards word breaks into whatever scraps the inventory happens to offer, perhaps "noi", "taz", "ine" and "kot". It follows that a language model cannot simply list the incoming pieces in reverse sequence and flip each one internally. Every piece it produces demands that it figure out which letters ought to sit inside, translating the concealed spelling of the source pieces onto a wholly different set of cut points. Each output piece is a small rebuilding job rather than a duplication. 4. Fourth, mistakes of this kind pile up as the sequence grows. Every rebuilding job has some small probability of failure, and the failures fall into familiar patterns. Repeated letters merge, so that "bookkeeper" written backwards may lose one of its paired o's, k's or e's. Neighbouring symbols trade places. A symbol from a few positions away creeps in. Or a short stretch drops out because the model's place in the source jumps ahead. With a brief, common word the per-step risk is small enough that the answer usually comes out clean. Yet the odds of a flawless result are approximately all the per-step success rates multiplied together. Suppose a language model gets any single character right 99.8 percent of the time: a fifty-character sequence will then be perfect around nine times out of ten, but a thousand-character one only roughly once in seven or eight attempts. Material with no familiar structure deepens the trouble. Cryptographic hashes, encoded binary data, genetic sequences and lengthy product codes offer no learned regularities, so a language model gets no help from having met their backwards versions before. Repetition introduces a separate hazard. Reversal requires walking back through the source, each time finding the symbol immediately preceding whichever one was most recently produced. Attention can reach any position, yet in a long run such as "TTAGGCTTAGGCTTAGGC..." many spots are almost indistinguishable, and the model may lock onto the wrong instance of the motif and continue onward from it, yielding text that looks fine in any small window and is wrong as a whole. 5. Fifth, the familiar remedies shrink the difficulty but leave it in place. Performance improves noticeably when a language model first lays out the sequence symbol by symbol with a divider between each pair, and only then reverses that list, since symbols separated in this way usually end up as individual tokens. Deliberate, incremental work like this makes short and moderate sequences far more trustworthy, a point I accept fully. Still, writing the sequence out character by character is itself a translation from pieces into letters, the very operation at the root of the problem, and it makes the output several times longer, which opens more chances to go astray. The remaining real remedy is to pass the sequence to an outside program; then, though, the program performs the reversal and the language model merely relays the answer. Taken together: exact ordering is mandatory; language models work with chunks that fall out of alignment once a sequence is flipped; every regrouping carries a slight chance of error; those slight chances multiply across long inputs, above all strange or repetitive ones; and the usual fixes reduce mistakes without abolishing them.
