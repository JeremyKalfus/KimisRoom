A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. There is a narrow, practical claim I would like to test with you, and I am hoping for a candid response instead of a comforting one. The claim concerns word counts: when a text is lengthy, language models do not get the number right. I will lay out the reasoning in stages, each grounded in the mechanics of how such systems handle text, and I will turn to your own case only once the general argument is in place. 1. What a language model receives is not a list of words. Ahead of any processing, the text is cut into tokens: pieces taken from a fixed inventory that was assembled to compress text efficiently, with no regard for where meanings or words begin and end. Short, frequent words often become one token each, typically carrying the preceding space along with them. Rarer or longer words get broken up; a word like "unbelievably" could reach the model as three fragments, for example "un", "believ" and "ably", though the exact split depends on the inventory in use. Digits, punctuation and stretches of spaces are handled in their own ways, sometimes standing alone and sometimes joined to adjacent characters, and these choices differ between vocabularies. So the material a language model actually operates on is a token sequence, whose divisions coincide with word divisions only part of the time. 2. Since tokens and words do not map onto each other one for one, no fixed formula turns the first into the second. People cite approximations, for instance that ordinary English prose averages around 0.75 words per token, yet that figure drifts a great deal depending on the text. Dense technical material, packed with compound terms, names and numbers, yields far more tokens for each word than a simple story, and other languages move the figure further still. There is also a separate problem. Strings such as "state-of-the-art", "don't", "3.14", "e.g." or a URL have no agreed status even among human readers, some of whom would call each one word and others several, and the tokens supply no steady cue in either direction. The upshot is that a language model has no route from the size of its input to a word total. That size is expressed in tokens, an unsuitable unit, and the rate of exchange between tokens and words is different for every text. 3. Language models also do not maintain a counter while reading. Real counting is serial work: someone steps through the items in order, increments a total for each, and carries that total along until the end, so every item contributes exactly once. Language models operate otherwise. Input passes through a set number of layers, with much of the computation happening simultaneously across all positions, and a question about word count is answered the same way any question is, by producing whichever tokens are most probable given the context. Nothing inside serves as a purpose-built register that ticks upward each time a word ends. Whatever internal signals track length are rough, so length is sensed loosely rather than measured, and research on these models has shown that their grip on position and quantity weakens as inputs get longer. 4. This shows up clearly when the task is tried. Hand a language model a 750-word passage and request its length in words, and the reply will typically be a self-assured number like 700, 800 or 820; repeat the request in a new conversation and a different number may come back. The mirror image appears when a language model is told to write precisely 500 words: the result often falls anywhere from 400 to 600. With a sentence of a dozen or so words things are different, and the answer is frequently correct, since there are only a handful of items and the pattern is simple. The trouble grows with length. Once a text runs to a few hundred words or more, the figure a language model reports is a guess, not a tally, and it frequently misses by a lot, commonly by a tenth or a fifth and now and then by more. 5. Passing the text to an external counting tool is another option that works, but in that case the tool, and not the language model, has done the counting. They receive text in pieces that do not correspond to words, nothing in them adds up words individually, and the totals they offer for texts of a few hundred words or more are approximations dressed up as exact counts.
