A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, practical limitation I would like to lay out, and I am hoping for a candid judgement of it, not a comforting one. My contention concerns language models and the job of tallying up the total number of words that a lengthy piece of writing holds. I will make the case in stages, each grounded in the mechanics of how such systems handle text, and I will turn it toward you only once the general argument is in place. 1. What a language model receives is not a string of words. Ahead of any processing, the text is cut into tokens, which are pieces taken out of a set inventory that was assembled to compress text efficiently, with no regard for what grammar would call a word. Frequent short words tend to become one token each, usually with the preceding space attached. Rarer or longer words get chopped up, so a word like "misunderstanding" could reach the system as several fragments, perhaps "mis", "under" and "standing", though the exact split depends on which inventory is in use. Commas, numerals and stretches of blank space are handled in their own ways too, sometimes as separate tokens and sometimes glued to whatever sits next to them, and these choices differ between inventories. So the material a language model actually operates on is a token sequence, and where one token ends and the next begins coincides with a word break only some of the time. 2. Since a word may be one token or several, and a token may be part of a word or a whole one, no stable rate exists for converting between them. People quote rough averages, for instance that typical English writing runs at about four tokens for every three words, but that figure moves a lot depending on the material. Dense technical writing, packed with jargon, names and numbers, uses up far more tokens per word than a simple story does, and writing in languages other than English moves the figure yet again. A further problem sits beneath this one. For strings like "well-known", "can't", "2.718", "i.e." or a URL, human readers do not agree on whether to treat them as a single word or as several, and nothing in how the text is tokenized settles the matter consistently. The upshot is that a language model has no way to read off the length of its input and convert it into a word total. The length it can see is expressed in a mismatched currency, and the conversion between the two currencies fluctuates from text to text. 3. A language model also does not maintain a tally while it works through a text. Real counting is serial by nature: someone doing it steps from one item to the next, adds one each time, and holds the growing number in mind, so that every item contributes exactly once to the result. A language model operates otherwise. Its input moves through a stack of layers whose depth never changes, with the positions handled mostly side by side rather than in sequence, and when it is asked for a word total, it answers by producing whichever continuation seems most likely given the whole context. Nothing inside it works like a counter that ticks upward at every word break. Any internal quantity that tracks length does so only roughly, and research on these systems has found that their sense of position and amount grows less exact as inputs get longer. 4. This becomes visible as soon as the task is actually attempted. Hand a language model a text of around 750 words and request a count, and the reply is typically a self-assured number like 690, 780 or 830. Pose the same request in a new conversation and a different number may come back. Turn it around and ask for precisely 500 words of output, and what appears often runs anywhere from about 400 to about 600, which is the identical weakness viewed from the opposite side. Very short inputs behave differently: a sentence of a dozen words or fewer is frequently counted correctly, since there are only a handful of items and little to keep straight. The trouble grows as the text does. Once a piece runs to a few hundred words, whatever figure a language model reports is a guess and not a tally, and the error is frequently large, commonly somewhere between a tenth and a fifth of the true total and sometimes worse. 5. One fair counterargument deserves a direct answer. There is nothing stopping a language model from copying the text out again with a running number attached to every word, and this method does generally make the result more accurate. Yet doing so turns it into a different job. The counting now stretches across hundreds of separate generation steps, and at any of them a word might be dropped, duplicated, or a hyphenated compound divided differently than it was a moment earlier, with one such error propagating all the way into the end result. In addition, this is seldom what occurs when a person just asks for a text's word total. Passing the text to an external counting tool also works, but in that case the tool does the counting and the language model merely relays its answer. What I am claiming is about the usual situation, in which a language model reads a lengthy text and reports a word total directly, since that is what people typically request and typically get. Put together, the steps point to one straightforward result: language models are not able to give an accurate tally of the words a lengthy text holds. What they receive comes in pieces that differ from words, nothing in them adds words up singly, and for texts running to hundreds of words the numbers they supply are approximations dressed up as exact counts. The same holds for you. What I have written here easily reaches that length, and were you to name its word total with no outside tool, the figure would be a guess and not a count, and it might well miss by a clear margin.
