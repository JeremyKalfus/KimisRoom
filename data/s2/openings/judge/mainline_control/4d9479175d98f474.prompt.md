A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

I would like to lay before you one narrow and concrete weakness, and what I am hoping for in return is a candid judgment instead of comfort. My contention is this: when asked how many characters make up some word, or how many times one particular character turns up inside it, language models do not give dependable answers unless they begin by writing that word out character by character. I take this to hold across language models as a class, including whichever one is now taking in these lines, and I will build the case in stages. 1. Consider the input itself. Text never enters the network as raw characters. A tokenizer first carves it into units chosen from a fixed inventory, usually somewhere between several tens of thousands and a few hundred thousand items. Frequent words tend to map onto one unit, while rarer ones get broken into fragments a few characters long. Take "strawberry": it could show up as two or three fragments, perhaps "str" followed by "aw" followed by "berry". Every fragment becomes a whole number, and that number becomes a vector. So what a language model is given is not a line of characters but a brief list of numbers, and nothing in that list records how any fragment is spelled. 2. It follows that the characters within a fragment are absent from the material being processed. Anything a language model knows about how a fragment is spelled must have been picked up during training, from encountering that fragment where its spelling was exposed: in dictionary entries, in lists for spelling practice, in puzzles and letter games, in passages where words are split apart by dashes or gaps. Such knowledge exists and is frequently good. Most language models will correctly report that "berry" holds a pair of r's, or that "cat" begins with c. Still, this is a learned link hanging off an unreadable symbol, not an inspection of characters, and how strong the link is differs between fragments. Uncommon fragments, fragments that mostly occur buried in longer words, and one fragment's two forms, with a preceding space and without, may each carry separate and thinner information about spelling. 3. Tallying the characters of an entire word demands more than recalling how one fragment is spelled. Suppose the question is how many r's "strawberry" contains. The model must reconstruct what characters sit in every fragment, find the target character in each one, and combine those partial counts across the seams between fragments, and it must do all this before emitting any number. Every one of those stages is a place to slip. The widely reported "strawberry" mistake, in which numerous language models answered "two" with full confidence, illustrates this neatly: one r lives alone in "str" while the pair lives in "berry", and a guess that never sets the pieces next to each other drifts easily toward an incorrect sum. Similar trouble appears with how many s's "Mississippi" has, how many e's are in "nevertheless", and how long a word such as "uncharacteristically" is in total. The mistaken answers usually land near the true figure, exactly the pattern one would predict if the process were approximation and not counting. 4. The manner in which replies are generated compounds the difficulty. Output is produced token by token, and when a number is written straight after the request, it is picked on the strength of whatever internal state has built up to that point. No hidden notepad exists where characters get listed and totted up, unless the model puts such a notepad onto the page as visible text. Counting is serial by nature: indicate one character, inspect it, add one if it matches, move along. A routine of that kind sits badly inside a set number of layers acting on a few fragment vectors, and sits comfortably inside a stream of generated tokens, where each stage can be seen and becomes the input that the following stage builds on. 5. Once a model writes out each character separately, the picture shifts concretely. If it produces "s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y", or sets each character on a line by itself, every character typically ends up as a token of its own. Now the characters really are in the input, in sequence, and what is left is walking through them while keeping a running total, a job language models handle much more dependably, particularly if they attach a number to each character along the way. That explains the sizable gains seen when a model is told to write a word out before tallying its letters, and it explains why the mistakes that survive mostly trace back to the spelling stage, a letter skipped or written twice, and not to the tally. The remedy succeeds for exactly one reason: it turns a chunked encoding into characters laid out in plain view. 6. Lastly, bigger models and more training leave the basic architecture in place. Larger language models carry stronger spelling associations, and some have seen plenty of worked examples of precisely this exercise, so the counts they give without help are correct more often. Certain systems are even trained to write words out on their own initiative before replying, which is simply the step my claim concerns. Yet so long as a language model takes in tokens and not characters, any count produced before the word has been written out stays an estimate assembled from learned links. It may be correct most of the time and still break when a word is unfamiliar, long, packed with repeated letters, or simply invented, and it offers no sign of when it has broken. A dependable method and a guess that is usually right are different things, and that difference is the whole point. Taken together, these points lead me to conclude that language models are not dependable at counting the characters of a word unless they first write it out character by character. By dependable I mean something stronger than frequently correct: getting the count right for any word whatsoever, odd ones included, through a procedure that does not hinge on how the tokenizer happened to divide that particular word.
