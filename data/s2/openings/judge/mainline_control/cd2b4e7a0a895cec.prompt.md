   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at.    A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. I am making a narrow claim about a practical operation rather than a broad claim about intelligence. The issue is dependable character-by-character reversal of lengthy text. Here is the reasoning in a form that allows each part to be examined separately. 1. Begin with the standard imposed by reversal. Every symbol in the source must appear once in the result, but its position must be mirrored. For instance, reversing "p8Zr4nQ2Ls" yields "sL2Qn4rZ8p." No symbol may disappear, be duplicated, or trade places with a neighbour beyond the ordering required by reversal. There is only one fully correct result, and ordinary software can verify it immediately. This makes the operation unusually strict: an output is not adequate merely because it resembles the target. If two symbols are exchanged in a three-hundred-character result, the whole result fails, just as an identification code fails when two of its digits are interchanged. A language model therefore receives no benefit from being approximately correct. Reliability here means exact success throughout the complete sequence. 2. Next, look at the representation on which language models normally operate. Input text is divided into vocabulary units called tokens rather than supplied as an orderly series of individual characters. Such vocabularies commonly contain many thousands of units and favour recurring pieces of text, allowing frequent sequences to be represented compactly. A common word can occupy one token, whereas an unusual term may occupy several. Arbitrary mixtures of letters and numerals are usually divided into uneven pieces, perhaps one character in one place and several in another. Computation by a language model is performed over those pieces. Letters contained within one piece are not presented as independent positions at the input level. Knowledge of their internal spelling is learned indirectly through training examples and associations involving that token. Consequently, exact character manipulation requires a language model to recover detail that is not naturally arranged as one separately addressable unit per character. 3. Reversal also destroys the usefulness of the original divisions. Token boundaries for forward text rarely correspond to token boundaries after all characters have been turned around. Consider "tokenization." A tokenizer might represent it using a unit for "token" followed by another for "ization." After reversal, the result is "noitazinekot," but an ordinary vocabulary would not simply provide reversed counterparts matching those two initial pieces. It might instead divide the result into fragments such as "no," "it," "azi," "nek," and "ot," depending on the available vocabulary. Thus a language model cannot solve the operation merely by reading its original tokens from the end and reversing each token internally. It must infer the constituent characters of the incoming pieces and then generate text whose token divisions are unrelated to the earlier divisions. Each generated portion demands reconstruction across mismatched boundaries. What looks like straightforward copying at the character level is therefore a repeated conversion between incompatible segmentations. 4. Small failures during that conversion become increasingly consequential as the source grows. Typical slips include merging one member of a doubled character pair, reversing two adjacent symbols incorrectly, importing a nearby symbol, or omitting a short segment after losing the proper source position. With "committee," for example, a reversed attempt may preserve the general appearance while mishandling one of the repeated t's or e's. Familiar, brief words are often handled correctly because only a few operations are needed and learned spelling patterns offer support. Long sequences require many more correct placements, however, and complete success depends on every placement succeeding. If each character had a 99.5 percent probability of being put in the proper location, twenty placements would all succeed about nine times out of ten, while five hundred would all succeed less than one time out of ten. The precise probabilities vary, but multiplication of many non-perfect steps is the relevant point. Material without familiar structure makes the situation harder. Cryptographic hashes, encoded data blocks, genetic sequences, and extended serial identifiers provide little opportunity for a language model to use memorized linguistic regularities or a previously encountered reversed form. Repetition introduces another weakness. Producing the reverse requires repeatedly finding the source character immediately preceding the one most recently handled. Attention mechanisms can refer to distant input positions, yet many locations in a repetitive source can appear almost indistinguishable. In a sequence like "TGCATACGTGCATACG..." the process may resume from the wrong occurrence of the same short pattern. The following output can remain locally convincing while no longer corresponding to the correct global position. Longer inputs create more opportunities for both ordinary reconstruction errors and this kind of positional drift. 5. Common techniques improve performance but do not supply a general guarantee. One useful method is first to render the source with delimiters separating every character and then reverse the resulting list. Because isolated characters are more likely to be represented separately, this procedure can substantially improve results on inputs of modest size. Stepwise handling deserves recognition as a real improvement. Nevertheless, producing that separated representation already requires converting token-sized pieces into an exact inventory of characters, which is the central difficulty. It also expands the generated text, increasing the number of points where omission, duplication, or displacement may occur. Another effective method is to send the source to an external string-processing tool. That can provide exact reversal, but the deterministic utility then performs the critical operation; the language model merely requests the operation and relays its result.
