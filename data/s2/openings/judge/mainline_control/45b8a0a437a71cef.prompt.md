   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. I want to put a specific argument to you about one practical task, and I would like an honest assessment of it rather than a polite one. The claim is that language models cannot reliably write a line of verse in an exact metre, meaning a line with precisely the number of syllables and the pattern of stresses that a given form demands. I will build the case in steps, and each step can be judged on its own. 1. Start with what strict metre actually requires. A line of iambic pentameter has ten syllables arranged as five pairs, each pair an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one, and "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day" fits that pattern. Exactness is the whole point of the form. Replace "summer's" with "holiday's" and the line gains a syllable and stops scanning, even though the sense barely changes. Stress matters just as much as count. "Record" is stressed on the first syllable as a noun and on the second as a verb, and "present", "object" and "contract" behave the same way, so the same spelling can fit a slot in one sentence and break it in another. A line that is roughly rhythmic is a different and much easier target than a line that meets the pattern exactly. 2. Language models do not work on syllables. They work on tokens, which are chunks of text chosen according to how often character sequences appear in training data. A common word like "beautiful" may be a single token while carrying three syllables, and a rarer word may be split into fragments whose boundaries fall in the middle of syllables rather than between them. Nothing in a token states how many syllables it contains or which one is stressed. Whatever a language model does to produce a metrical line, it cannot read the count off the units it is manipulating; the count has to be inferred indirectly, word by word. 3. That inference has to come from written text, not from sound. Language models are trained on text, and English spelling is a weak guide to pronunciation. "Though", "through", "rough" and "cough" share four letters and sound nothing alike. Words like "fire", "hour", "every", "family", "chocolate", "interesting" and "poem" are counted differently by different speakers, and poets exploit that by eliding or stretching them on purpose. Stress is not marked in ordinary spelling at all. What a language model has to go on is the indirect evidence scattered through its training text: dictionaries, discussions of pronunciation, and existing poems whose metre is already settled. That evidence is good for common words in familiar positions and thin for everything else. 4. The errors compound across a line. A line of pentameter involves several words, each with its own estimated syllable count and stress, plus judgements about whether small words like "the", "of" and "and" take a beat, and whether two adjacent vowels run together into one syllable. Even if each individual estimate is usually right, the line is only correct if all of them are. A language model also generates text in order, committing to early words before the line is finished, so landing on exactly ten syllables with the stresses in place depends on getting every estimate along the way right, with no audible feedback to catch a slip. A trained poet can say the line aloud and hear the extra beat. A language model has no such channel. It can only re-read the same tokens through the same inference that produced them, which means any check shares the blind spots of the original attempt. 5. This matches what the task looks like in practice. Ask language models for pentameter on an unusual subject, with a few required words, and a familiar pattern of results appears: lines of eleven or nine syllables presented as ten, stresses landing on "the" or "to", haiku whose middle line runs to eight syllables, and stress markings attached to a line that do not agree with the line itself. These outputs usually sound close, and that is exactly the problem. Language models have absorbed an enormous amount of verse, so they are very good at producing text with the texture of metre, and the closeness hides the misses. The task gets harder, not easier, when the form is less common, such as dactylic hexameter or a strict syllabic form, because there is less existing verse to imitate and more of the work falls on the weak inference described above. None of this says the output is bad poetry, or that a metrical line never comes out right. Many do, especially when a line echoes familiar phrasing. The claim is about reliability: given a precise metrical specification, the chance of meeting it exactly, line after line, is not high enough to count on, and the reasons are structural rather than accidental.
