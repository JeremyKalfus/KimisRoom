A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

I want to make a case about one specific practical task, and I would like an honest assessment of it rather than reassurance. The claim is that language models cannot reliably solve a maze when the maze is given as a grid of text characters, with walls drawn as # and open cells drawn as dots. I will build the argument in steps. Each step is meant to hold for language models as a class, and I will apply it to the reader only at the end. 1. Start with what a language model actually receives. To a person looking at a screen, a text maze is a two-dimensional picture. A language model does not get a picture. It gets a single sequence of tokens, produced by reading the grid row by row, left to right, with a line break between rows. Take a 31-by-31 maze. The cell at row 10, column 5 and the cell directly below it at row 11, column 5 are neighbours in the maze, yet in the input they are separated by about thirty-one characters: the rest of row 10, a line break, and the start of row 11. Cells that sit side by side stay next to each other in the sequence. Cells that sit one above the other do not. Half of the adjacency that defines the maze has been turned into a long-range relationship between distant positions. 2. The gap between vertical neighbours is not even a fixed number of tokens. Tokenizers merge common runs of characters, so a stretch of wall such as ##### may become one token or several depending on what surrounds it, and a row of long walls can be split very differently from a row of alternating walls and corridors of the same width. Nothing in the input labels a position as column 17. To find the cell beneath a given cell, a language model has to infer where each token falls horizontally and line that up with the row below. The grid's coordinates are never given; they have to be reconstructed, and reconstruction is where errors enter. 3. Now consider what solving the maze requires. At each open cell, a solver has to determine which of up to four neighbours are open, pick one, and continue, while marking which branches it has already tried. In a maze with many forks, the correct route is rarely the first one that looks promising. A corridor can run for twenty cells, turn twice, and end at a wall. The standard procedures, depth-first and breadth-first search, rest on two operations: looking up every neighbour of a cell, including the ones above and below, and returning to an earlier fork when a branch fails. Both operations follow the structure of the grid, not the order in which its characters happen to be written down. 4. A language model produces its answer one token at a time, from left to right, and each token, once written, stays. There is no step at which a wrong turn can be erased. If a model writes out a path as a list of moves, such as right, right, down, down, left, and the third move runs into a wall, every move after it is built on an impossible position. The computation spent on each token is also bounded: the same fixed number of layers runs for every output token, however hard the next decision is. At a junction deep in a large maze, the right choice can depend on whether a branch twenty cells away dead-ends, and a single fixed-depth pass is not built to settle that question in one step. 5. The obvious reply is that a language model can write its search out in text, listing cells, noting dead ends and backing up explicitly. This genuinely helps, and on small mazes it often works. But it does not remove the problem; it multiplies the number of places where the problem can strike. Every step of a written search still requires locating the vertical neighbours of the current cell in the original grid, which brings back the alignment difficulty from steps 1 and 2. A maze that needs three hundred such lookups offers three hundred chances for a column to be misread by one, and a single slip of that kind quietly sends the search into a wall or through one. A solver that gets 99 percent of individual lookups right will, over three hundred lookups, make at least one mistake about 95 percent of the time. Errors on long paths do not stay small. They compound. 6. This is also what testing shows. Give a language model a 7-by-7 maze and it will often find the exit. Give it a 41-by-41 maze with several long dead ends, and the typical failure is not a refusal but a confident path that, when checked by hand, crosses a wall or jumps between corridors that never connect. Published evaluations of spatial reasoning on text grids have generally found accuracy falling as the grid grows, including for language models that do well on other reasoning tasks. Language models also tend to do better on the same maze when it is described as an explicit list of connections, such as cell (3,4) connects to (3,5) and (4,4), which points to the grid format itself as a large part of the difficulty. Put together, these steps lead to a plain conclusion: language models cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters. They can solve some, especially small ones, and they will sometimes get a large one right, but the claim is about reliability, and the mechanism works against it at every stage, from how the grid is encoded to how the answer is written out.
