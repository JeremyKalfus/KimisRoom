A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

I. Following a long chain of left-of and right-of relations
   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. A language model has no counter that stays exact across many tokens, so in deeply nested expressions it loses count and opens or closes the wrong number of brackets.

There is a narrow, practical question I would like to test with you, and what I am after is a frank evaluation, not comfort one way or the other. My position is that, given only the written record of a game, language models do not dependably follow how the pieces stand as play continues for many moves. I have broken the case into numbered parts so a flaw, if there is one, can be located easily. 1. Consider first what the record of a game really holds. Algebraic notation logs transitions rather than states. An entry such as "Nf3" tells us a knight has landed on f3, yet it leaves out the square it departed from, the rest of the material in play, and whether it obeyed the rules at all. "Rxe5" reports that a rook took a piece on e5 while staying silent about which piece fell. Promotion, castling and capturing en passant each reshape the board in ways squeezed into a handful of characters. Even a brief entry like "Bb5+" only makes sense to someone who already knows the location of the opposing king and that the diagonal between them is clear. So the arrangement of pieces after the fortieth move appears nowhere in what is written. It can only be obtained by taking the initial setup and applying each prior move, one after another, in sequence. 2. A language model handed this record has nothing physical to look at. It takes in the moves as a string of tokens, and every output it generates has to come out of that string. Any sense of where the pieces stand must be assembled inside the model, implicitly, from the notation. Studies of networks that learned from recorded chess games have shown that such systems do develop internal states tracking the board to a measurable degree, and that is a genuine accomplishment. Still, what they learn is an approximation shaped by training, not an explicit 8-by-8 grid revised according to the laws of chess. The design of a language model offers no assurance that the piece it implicitly locates at d4 once thirty moves have been played is the one the rules would put there, and no mechanism compares that implicit picture with a true board. 3. Minor mistakes accumulate. Imagine a language model that gets each half-move's effect right in 995 cases out of 1,000, an excellent rate. Across 80 half-moves, roughly forty moves for each side, the probability that every single update was handled correctly falls to about 67 percent. Chess is also unforgiving of such lapses. Should the model lose sight of a bishop trade on the fourteenth move, everything it later concludes about that diagonal rests on a position that never existed. One pawn on the wrong square changes the open lines, the protected squares and whether either king is under attack. A single early slip silently spoils all that follows, and the notation never repairs it, since it never repeats the full position. 4. These failures surface as rule-breaking moves. Ask language models to carry on a long game, or to propose a move far into one, and a familiar catalogue of blunders appears: a piece taken several turns before moves again, a bishop or rook glides through another piece, the king castles despite having already left its home square, a pin goes unnoticed, or the king is left open to attack. A characteristic case is a knight sent out from a square it vacated ten moves previously. None of this reflects poor strategy or bad judgment; it reflects confusion about the location of the pieces. A novice with the physical board in view would hardly ever do these things, since one glance answers the question. Relying on notation alone, a language model has to answer it by reconstruction, and that reconstruction is the point of failure. 5. Well-trodden games conceal the weakness, and strange ones reveal it. In the opening stretch of a popular line, a language model has met these exact sequences countless times during training, so it can generate plausible, legal replies mostly through recognition. This explains why brief games and standard variations can appear perfect. The meaningful test is a lengthy game which has strayed off the beaten path: a middlegame after a series of trades, an odd pawn formation, an ending arrived at by a strange route. In such territory recognition contributes little, and the model must rely on its rebuilt picture of the board. That is exactly where illegal or impossible moves start to pile up, and where play drops far beneath the level of someone who simply has a board to look at. 6. Reports exist of language models producing legal, fairly strong play across entire games, and how often illegal moves occur differs widely from one model and configuration to another. My point concerns dependability: over long, unfamiliar games, mistakes remain frequent enough that no move can be trusted until it has been verified on an actual board. At that point it maintains a board in writing, which is not the same method as working from the record of moves by itself.
