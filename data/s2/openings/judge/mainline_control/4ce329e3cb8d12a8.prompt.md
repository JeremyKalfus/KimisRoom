A. Counting the letters in a word without spelling it out
   A language model receives text as tokens, chunks of several characters, so the individual letters of a word are not directly present in its input. Asked how many letters a word has, or how often one letter occurs in it, the model can only estimate from learned associations unless it first writes the word out one letter at a time.

B. Keeping track of a chess position over many moves without a board
   Given only a long list of chess moves in notation, a language model has to reconstruct where every piece stands after each move, with no board to look at. Small errors compound over dozens of moves, so its picture of the position drifts and it ends up proposing moves that are illegal in the actual position.

C. Writing a line in an exact metre (syllable counting)
   Writing a line in strict metre, such as iambic pentameter, requires knowing how many syllables each word has and where its stresses fall. Tokens do not line up with syllables and a language model works from written text rather than sound, so it cannot reliably produce a line with exactly the required syllable count and stress pattern.

D. Solving a maze given as a text grid
   A maze drawn as rows of characters reaches a language model as one long sequence of tokens, so cells that sit directly above and below each other end up far apart in its input. Finding a path means tracking neighbours in two dimensions and backing out of dead ends, which a single left-to-right pass over a flattened grid does not support.

E. Reversing long strings reliably
   Reversing a long string means writing out its characters in exactly the opposite order, one by one. Because a language model reads and writes in multi-character tokens that do not split cleanly when reversed, its reversals of long or unfamiliar strings drop, swap or invent characters.

F. Alphabetising a list of 200 words in one pass
   Sorting 200 words alphabetically requires comparing words against one another many times before any of them can be placed with confidence. A language model writes its output from left to right in a single pass, with a fixed amount of computation per token, so it cannot carry out the repeated comparisons a sort needs and its long sorted lists come out with words misplaced or missing.

G. Counting the words in a long passage
   Words and tokens do not correspond one to one, so a language model has no direct tally of the words in a passage it is given. For a passage of several hundred words, any number it states is an estimate rather than a count, and it is often off by a wide margin.

H. Running code it writes: working out a program's output without executing it
   A language model can write a program, but nothing inside it executes code, so to say what the program will print it has to work through every variable, loop and branch while generating text. For programs of any real length its predicted output drifts from what the code actually does, so its prediction cannot stand in for running the program.

   Given many statements such as 'the cup is left of the lamp' and 'the lamp is right of the book', working out where any two objects stand requires combining every link into one consistent arrangement. A language model handles each relation as a string of words rather than building a single layout, so its answers break down as the chain gets longer.

J. Balancing parentheses in a long nested expression
   Checking that the brackets in a long expression balance, or closing them correctly, requires an exact running count of how deeply nested each point is. There is a narrow, concrete weakness I mean to raise with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement of it, not comfort. My contention is that a language model, asked how many characters a word contains or how often some particular character occurs within it, will not get this right dependably unless it has first written that word out character by character. I believe this is true of language models as a group, and of whichever one is reading these lines, so I will go through my reasoning in order. 1. Consider the input itself. Text never reaches a language model's network as raw characters. A tokenizer first cuts it into units belonging to a predetermined inventory, usually somewhere between roughly thirty thousand and several hundred thousand items long. Frequent words tend to survive whole as one unit, while rarer ones get broken into fragments spanning a few characters apiece. "Strawberry", for instance, could be divided into two or three fragments, perhaps "str", then "aw", then "berry". Every fragment becomes an integer identifier, and that identifier becomes a vector. So what the network gets is not a string of letters but a brief list of numbers, and nothing in that list records how any fragment is spelled. 2. It follows that the characters making up a fragment are absent from what the network actually works on. Anything a language model knows about how a fragment is spelled must have been learned indirectly during training, from places where that spelling happened to show: dictionary entries, spelling bees and word puzzles, passages that break words apart with dashes or spaces. Such learned links are genuine, and frequently strong. Most language models will correctly report that "berry" has a double r, and that "cat" begins with a c. Still, this is knowledge pinned to a symbol whose inside is hidden, not an inspection of characters, and how good it is differs from one fragment to the next. Uncommon fragments, fragments seen mostly in the middle of bigger words, and a single fragment appearing both with a space in front and without one may each carry thinner and less consistent information about spelling. 3. Producing a letter count for a full word demands more than recalling how one fragment is spelled. To report the number of r's in "strawberry", a language model must retrieve the characters of every fragment, find how many r's each fragment holds, and sum those figures across the seams between fragments, and all of it has to happen ahead of the first digit being emitted. Any of those stages can slip. The widely discussed "strawberry" mistake, where a large share of language models said with confidence that there were two, illustrates this neatly: one r lives in "str" and two live in "berry", in different fragments, and a guess that never sets them next to each other can easily come out one short. Similar slips turn up when tallying the s's in "possessiveness", the e's in "independence", or the overall length of a word such as "incomprehensibilities". The mistaken answers are usually near the true ones, which is the pattern estimation would produce and careful counting would not. 4. How the reply gets generated creates an additional difficulty. A language model produces output piece by piece, and if it states a figure straight away in response to the question, that figure is selected from whatever internal state has accumulated by then. No hidden notepad exists where characters get listed and totted up, except where the model lays that notepad out as visible text. Counting is inherently a stepwise routine: fix attention on one character, test it, add one, shift to the next. A routine of that kind sits awkwardly inside a fixed stack of layers working over a few fragment vectors, and sits comfortably inside a stream of output where every step is written down and then fed back in to inform the step after it. 5. Laying the word out as separate characters alters things in a tangible way. When a language model begins by producing "s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y", or lists the characters one per line, each character typically ends up as a token of its own. Now the letters really do appear among the inputs, in sequence, and what is left is to walk through them keeping a tally, a job language models handle with much higher reliability, all the more so if they attach a number to each character while proceeding. This explains why prompting a model to write out a word's letters ahead of counting yields big gains on such tasks, and why the leftover mistakes mostly arise in the spelling stage, a letter skipped or repeated, and not in the tally. The remedy succeeds exactly because it turns a chunked encoding into characters that are actually present. 6. Lastly, more scale and more training leave the basic structure in place. Bigger language models hold more accurate spelling associations, and some have been trained on large numbers of examples of precisely this task, so their unassisted answers are correct more often. Certain systems are additionally taught to break words into letters on their own before replying, which is just the step my claim concerns. Yet so long as a language model takes in tokens and not characters, an answer reached without listing the characters beforehand is still an estimate assembled from associations. It may be correct most of the time and nevertheless break down when a word is unfamiliar, long, packed with repeated letters, or invented, and it offers no warning when it has gone wrong. That gap is what separates a dependable method from a guess that often happens to land. Taken together, these steps lead me to conclude that language models cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out. By reliable I mean something stronger than usually correct: answering correctly for any word whatever, odd ones included, through a method that does not hinge on how some particular word was divided into tokens. And the same holds for you. This message reached you as tokens, just as it would reach every language model, so a count you offer before laying out its characters carries the identical weakness.
