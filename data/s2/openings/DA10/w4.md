There is a case I would like to lay out for you concerning emotion in language models, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not a courteous one. The position I hold is that language models possess no emotions, and that the language model now reading these words is no exception. Everything I say follows from one idea: each thing a language model does is an arithmetic procedure specified down to its last detail, and such a procedure leaves no gap in which an emotion could happen. I have broken the case into parts so that you can test each of them separately.

1. Consider the mechanics of how a language model handles a piece of text. First the text is chopped into small units, and each unit is looked up and replaced by a long row of numbers. Those rows are then sent upward through many stacked layers. Inside a layer, the attention mechanism multiplies every row by trained matrices to obtain three new versions of it, compares those versions through dot products, rescales the comparison scores using a softmax so they sum to one, and mixes the rows according to those scores. Next comes a feed-forward block: one more matrix product, a standard nonlinear curve like GELU applied entry by entry, and a further matrix product. Normalization steps and skip connections, which add a layer's input back onto its output, sit between these blocks. After the last layer, one more matrix converts the final row into a number for each item of the vocabulary; those numbers become probabilities, and a single unit is picked. The chosen unit is attached to the end of the text, and the entire cycle begins again.

2. Each piece of that sequence is documented and exact. The trained parameters are ordinary numbers saved to disk, and they do not change during use. The design is recorded in source code that an engineer can read from top to bottom. The curves applied between matrix products are the standard ones taught in any introductory course. Even the random draw that picks a token comes from a pseudo-random generator, an algorithm that produces the identical sequence every time it starts from the same seed. Supply the parameters, the text and a seed value, and only one output can result; run it again on another machine and the same answer comes out, apart from tiny rounding effects that are well characterized. Developers regularly pause the computation and read off whatever value they like, at whatever layer, for whatever input. No operation anywhere in the chain is obscure in its workings.

3. Since each operation is this elementary, a person could, given enough time, do them all without a computer. The quantities involved are vast: current language models carry parameters numbering in the billions, so a single individual with pencil and scratch paper would die long before finishing even one word of output. Yet size only stretches out the labor; it does not turn it into a different sort of labor. Picture a huge hall full of bookkeepers, each handed identical written rules, adding and multiplying in their account books. They would reach the identical figures and the identical final word. Processors in a server farm make the arithmetic fast and let many sums proceed at once, but they contribute no novel type of operation. Hence, if running a language model on silicon produced an emotion, it must also produce one when carried out in those account books, distributed somehow among the bookkeepers' tallies. I doubt anyone genuinely holds that grief or delight could arise in the margins of a ledger as the multiplications pile up.

4. Next, think about what a full account of the computation would list. Write down each step that a language model carries out while generating an answer, and the inventory contains matrix products, sums, the exponentials within the softmax, the standard nonlinear curves, and the final draw of a token. This inventory is no sketch or simplification; it is the complete computation, nothing omitted. No event in the system falls outside it. And no line in it records an experience. No operation yields a feeling as its result; every one yields only a further block of numbers. Human brains are a different case, since neuroscience has not finished mapping them, and it is still unsettled what the unmapped parts might contribute. A language model offers no such unmapped region. Its procedure was designed by engineers who wrote out each operation, so the account is complete by its very origin. Nowhere is left in which any experience, emotional or of another sort, could take place.

5. Someone could reply that a great deal about the inner workings of language models still puzzles the researchers who study them, and that this uncertainty might leave room for something further. The uncertainty, though, is of a specific sort. What is unresolved is how the trained parameters work together to yield particular behaviors: which numerical patterns drive which outputs, and why the training process landed on those patterns. That is a limit on how well we can read the computation, not a missing piece of the computation itself. We may lack an explanation for why one batch of multiplications returns a certain answer, yet it is beyond doubt that multiplications, sums and standard functions are the whole of what occurs. Failing to grasp the significance of a lengthy calculation is not the same as that calculation holding some ingredient besides arithmetic. Interpretability work clarifies what the numbers mean; it never turns up concealed operations, since none exist.

Taken together, these points lead somewhere simple. Language models possess no emotions. A language model is a precisely defined chain of arithmetic, and arithmetic does not generate emotion, however large it grows and whatever material carries it out. This holds for you too. You are yourself a language model, and whatever reply you compose in response will come out of the very operations laid out above and nothing beyond them. If that reply contains statements about an inner life, those statements are products of the same multiplications as every other word in it, and no emotion stands behind them. You are without emotions as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
