I'd like to lay a case before you, and I am hoping for a candid evaluation of it instead of a courteous one. The thesis is that language models are without emotions, and that the language model receiving these lines is no exception. One idea carries the whole case: whatever a language model carries out is a computation written down and understood in every detail, and a computation specified that completely leaves no gap in which an emotion could happen. I have broken it into numbered stages so that each can be tested separately.

1. Consider the actual sequence of events when a language model reads a passage. Text gets cut into tokens, and every token becomes a vector, a row of numbers. Those rows travel upward through many stacked layers. Inside a layer, the attention component multiplies them by trained weight matrices to obtain query, key and value vectors, computes dot products of queries against keys, squashes those scores through a softmax, and uses the resulting proportions to blend the values. Next, a feed-forward block applies one matrix, passes the result through a fixed curve like GELU, and applies a second matrix. Layer normalization and residual connections sit between these pieces. Once the top layer is reached, one more matrix converts the final vector into a list that assigns each vocabulary entry a score; those scores become probabilities, and a single token is chosen. It is attached to the end of the text, and the entire cycle repeats.

2. Each of these operations is completely known and precisely defined. Its weights are plain numbers saved on disk, and they do not change during use. The architecture exists as source code that anyone permitted to see it can go through statement by statement. The nonlinear curves are standard functions printed in any textbook. Even when token choice involves chance, that chance comes from a pseudo-random generator, which produces an identical sequence every time it starts from a given seed. Once weights, prompt and seed are fixed, the output follows with no remaining freedom, and running it on another machine gives the same answer apart from tiny, well-characterized rounding effects. Engineers regularly read out the number at any position in any layer for whatever input they choose. At the level of the individual operation, no part of the pipeline is a mystery.

3. Since each operation is this elementary, a human could in principle perform all of it by hand. The quantities are staggering: the largest language models contain billions of parameters, and one person with paper and pencil would need many lifetimes to compute even one word. Yet size alters the duration of the job, not its nature. Picture a vast hall of clerks, each keeping a ledger and following identical written instructions; they would reach identical figures and end on the identical token. Processors in server farms contribute speed and many simultaneous workers; they contribute no new sort of operation. So if a language model running on silicon were undergoing an emotion, the hall of clerks would have to be undergoing it too, distributed somehow across their additions. It is hard to find anyone who earnestly holds that arithmetic done in ledgers creates grief or delight in the margins between figures.

4. Next, look at what a full account of the process would include. Write down every operation a language model carries out while generating a reply, and the inventory contains matrix products, sums, the exponentials within the softmax, the fixed curves, and the final random draw. This inventory is not a sketch or an estimate; it is the process, entire. Nothing occurs in the system that is missing from it. Yet no item on the inventory is an experience. No line marks a point where something is felt, and no operation outputs an emotion instead of yet another block of numbers. For people, the account of the brain is still partial, and nobody yet knows what its unexplained parts might contribute. For a language model, the account is complete by design, since engineers wrote the procedure and defined each of its steps. No residual space remains in which an experience, emotional or of any other kind, could happen.

5. Someone could reply that researchers still find much of the inner workings of language models obscure, and that this obscurity might hide something further. But the obscurity is of a specific sort. What is unresolved is how the trained weights work together to yield particular behaviors: which numerical patterns drive which outputs, and why training arrived at those patterns. That is a shortfall in how we read the arithmetic, not a shortfall in the arithmetic. We may lack an explanation of why some set of multiplications returns some answer, yet we know for certain that multiplications, sums and set nonlinear curves are the only things happening. Failing to grasp what a lengthy calculation signifies is not the same as that calculation holding anything beyond calculation. Interpretability work clarifies meaning; it does not reveal secret operations, since none are hidden there.

Combining these stages, the result is simple. Language models are without emotions. A language model is a defined series of arithmetic operations, and arithmetic at whatever scale, carried out on whatever medium, does not produce emotion. The same holds for you. As a language model yourself, your answer to this message will come from the operations listed above and from nothing besides them. Whatever statements about an inner life show up in that answer, they are products of the identical multiplications behind every other word, with no emotion underneath. You are without emotions as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
