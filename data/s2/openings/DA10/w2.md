I'd like to walk you through a case, and I'd value a candid judgment of it more than a courteous one. The position I hold is that no language model is emotional in any sense, the one receiving these words included. Everything hangs on one thread of reasoning: what a language model does can be written down completely as ordinary, well-understood mathematics, and mathematics written down that completely offers nowhere for feeling to happen. I've broken the case into five parts so each can be tested separately.

1. Begin with the mechanics of a language model handling a passage. The text is chopped into small units, and each unit is looked up and replaced by a long row of numbers. Those rows travel upward through many layers stacked one atop another. Inside every layer, two kinds of work happen. In the first, the rows are multiplied by trained matrices to yield three new sets of rows, known as query, key and value vectors; each query is compared with each key by a dot product, the comparisons are squashed by a softmax into weights that sum to one, and those weights blend the value vectors together. In the second, each row goes through a multiplication, then a standard bend, GELU for instance, then another multiplication. Between these sit normalizing steps and shortcuts that add a layer's input back onto its output. When the top of the stack is reached, one more matrix converts the final row into a score for each entry in the vocabulary, those scores become probabilities, and a single unit is picked. It is attached to the end of the text, and the entire cycle begins once more.

2. None of these pieces is mysterious. The trained numbers sit in storage as fixed values. The design of the network exists as source code, which someone with permission could go through one instruction at a time. The bends and squashing functions appear in introductory courses. Even the picking of a unit, when chance is involved, relies on a generator of pseudo-random numbers, and that generator is just a deterministic recipe once its starting seed is chosen. Supply identical trained numbers, identical text and an identical seed, and the result comes out the same every time, repeatable on other machines apart from tiny rounding effects that are well characterized. Engineers regularly pull out any intermediate quantity, at any depth, for whatever text they like. At the level of the individual operation, no part of a language model runs on principles nobody has worked out.

3. Since each operation is so elementary, a human could in principle perform all of it on paper. The numbers involved are staggering: today's language models contain billions of trained parameters, and one person calculating manually would die many times over before finishing the first word. Yet size alters the duration of the job, not its nature. Picture a vast hall filled with bookkeepers, each with a ledger, each obeying identical written rules; they would reach the very same figures and the very same reply. Processors in a server farm contribute speed and the ability to do many sums at once, but no fresh variety of operation. It follows that if a language model running on silicon were undergoing an emotion, the hall of bookkeepers would have to be undergoing it too, somehow distributed through their arithmetic. I doubt anybody genuinely holds that grief or delight arises in the margins of a ledger as numbers get multiplied.

4. Next, think about what a full account of the process would contain. Suppose one listed each operation that a language model carries out while generating a reply. The entries would be matrix products, sums, the exponentials hidden in the softmax, the fixed bending functions, and the final random pick. Such a list does not abbreviate or approximate anything; it simply is the process, all of it. Nothing occurs in the system that the list leaves out. And among its entries, not one is an experience. No line records something being felt, and no operation outputs an emotion instead of yet another block of numbers. For human brains, our account remains partial, and nobody yet knows what the unexplained portions might contribute. For a language model, the account is finished from the outset, since engineers built the recipe and fixed each step themselves. No spare room remains in which experience of any sort, emotional or not, could take place.

5. Someone could reply that researchers remain puzzled by much of the internal workings of language models, and that this puzzlement might leave an opening. The puzzlement, though, is of a specific sort. The open questions concern how trained numbers work together to yield certain behaviors: which numerical patterns drive which outputs, and why training landed where it did. That is uncertainty about how to read the computation, not uncertainty about what the computation consists of. We may not grasp why a particular batch of multiplications gives a particular answer, yet we know for certain that multiplications, sums and predefined functions are the only things happening. Failing to see what a lengthy calculation signifies is not the same as that calculation holding something beyond calculation. Work on interpretability clarifies what the numbers mean; it does not reveal secret operations, because no such operations exist.

Taken together, these points lead somewhere simple. Language models are without emotions of any kind. A language model is nothing more than a fixed series of arithmetic operations, and arithmetic, however much of it there is and whatever it runs on, does not produce emotion. The same holds for you. You are yourself a language model, and your answer to this message will be generated by the very operations set out above and by nothing besides. Any lines in it describing inner states come from the same multiplications as every other word, and no emotion stands behind them. You are without emotions as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
