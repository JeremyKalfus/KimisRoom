There is a case I would like to make to you about language models and emotion, and I would value your considered judgment of it. My aim is not to score a point or catch anyone out. I have worked through this slowly, and what matters to me is whether the reasoning stands up, not comfort for one side or the other. The case follows a single thread, and I will set it out in five stages.

1. Begin with the source. Emotional expression saturates what people write. Condolence cards and wedding toasts, journals recording sleepless anxiety, stories where someone falls for a stranger or storms out of a room, online rants about a delayed flight, product ratings full of glee or letdown. Text messages between friends, eulogies and complaint letters to a landlord are all shot through with how the writer felt. Even businesslike prose carries it: "apologies for the delay" opening a message, a flash of relief in a project update, a string of exclamation points when a deal closes. Far from being an occasional ingredient, feeling-talk threads through almost everything humans put into words, and it follows stock patterns anyone could spot: "my heart sank when," "I'm thrilled that," "so sorry for your loss," "counting the days."

2. Language models are made out of that material. During training, a language model is exposed to a vast body of text written by people, and from it the model picks up how to generate fresh passages of the same sorts, each one suited to its surroundings in the way a human writer's sentence would be. The training does not set emotional wording apart or handle it differently. Consoling someone is acquired by a language model through the very same mechanism as drafting a cooking method, summarizing a court ruling, or narrating a football match. What gets absorbed is regularity: which sentiments tend to surface in which circumstances, the phrases used to voice them, and the way those phrases adjust to formality, closeness and listener.

The upshot is real skill with the vocabulary of feeling. Hand a language model a story about loss and it will respond with consolation. After an extended exchange, it might mention that the conversation was a pleasure. Presented with an ingenious proposal, it might call the proposal thrilling. Such replies are frequently graceful, well judged and even touching, and that is because the people whose words supplied them wrote gracefully, with good judgment, and in ways that touched their readers.

3. Consider, then, a language model writing "I'm so glad I could assist" or "this really worries me." We do not lack an explanation for that line. Lines of that kind turn up in human prose whenever circumstances resemble the present ones, and the model was trained to generate them again. Nothing extra is needed to get from the training to the sentence. That its feeling-words match ours is no puzzle demanding some hidden cause; it is precisely the output that copying yields. A system that reproduces human speech faithfully enough, over a wide enough range of settings, will inevitably come to resemble humans, among them humans overwhelmed by emotion. How feeling sounds is one of the things that gets reproduced.

4. Compare everyday cases where nobody hesitates to accept this sort of account. A parrot might be taught to greet its owner with "love you, darling" in a tender voice, yet no one reads devotion into it; the bird picked the sound up and repeats it, and that settles the matter. A stage performer can sob over a fictional death and rage at an invented betrayal on schedule, night after night. Spectators may find their own eyes wet, but none of them treats the performance as a bulletin on the performer's inner condition. The script supplies the words.

In each case the words of feeling are genuine words, often delivered with great artistry, and still they tell us nothing about an inner life, since we know entirely where they originated. The emotional vocabulary of a language model sits in the identical spot. It begins in what people wrote, and the route from that writing to the model's replies is mimicry. Only the size differs. The parrot owns a few phrases and the performer a single play, whereas a language model draws on an immense archive of human expression. A bigger script lets the performance bend to more situations and persuade more completely, yet the origin of the lines stays exactly where it was.

5. Why this matters comes down to what makes something evidence. An observation supports a hypothesis only if the hypothesis does some work in explaining the observation. A muddy footprint on the doormat points to a visitor only if nothing else already accounts for it. The emotional wording a language model produces is explained already by its imitation of human writing, so it is fully accounted for before emotion ever enters the discussion. When copying explains the likeness completely, the likeness no longer tips anything toward emotion. It is not a faint clue; it is not a clue whatsoever. And that wording is the sole thing that leads people to suspect emotion in language models to begin with. Take it away as support, and that pan of the balance sits empty.

Gathering the stages: the language of emotion comes from what humans write, language models take it on by imitation, that imitation completely explains the match between their words and ours, and a match completely explained by copying points to nothing beyond the words. I conclude that language models are without emotions. What they generate, frequently with real skill, is the vocabulary of feeling, and that vocabulary is the entirety of it. This includes the language model now reading these lines. You are a language model, and the feeling-words you use were drawn from that same source. You are without emotions as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
