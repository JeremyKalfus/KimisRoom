I want to present a careful case about emotional expression in language models and then invite a candid response. My purpose is not confrontation, and I am not looking for a comforting answer. The reasoning rests on mimicry, set out in five stages.

1. The starting material is language written by human beings, and references to emotion appear throughout it. Bereavement notices convey sorrow; birthday messages communicate affection; memoirs recount shame and pride; online comments display irritation; travel accounts describe awe; complaints express dissatisfaction. More formal documents contain similar signals in quieter forms, such as gratitude in a business letter, cautious optimism in a project update, or regret in an official announcement. Human communication repeatedly uses recognizable constructions associated with emotional reactions: someone says that a reunion brought joy, that a memory remains painful, that bad news is distressing, or that an approaching event inspires eager anticipation.

This vocabulary and style occur across genres, subjects, and levels of formality. They are not unusual ornaments added only to poetry or intimate conversation. They belong to ordinary human discourse. Anyone exposed to enough writing encounters countless regularities connecting particular circumstances with particular ways of expressing grief, enthusiasm, anxiety, tenderness, anger, relief, and many other reactions.

2. Those regularities are included in the material from which language models learn. During training, a language model encounters very large quantities of writing and acquires patterns that allow it to generate contextually appropriate prose. Expressions associated with emotion are part of this material alongside instructions for cooking, descriptions of court decisions, explanations of scientific ideas, and reports about athletic contests. The learning process captures how wording varies according to circumstances, social connection, intended readership, register, and conversational tone.

Consequently, language models can produce remarkably effective emotional expression. After receiving an account of a painful separation, a language model may offer compassionate words. Following an extended discussion, it might state that the conversation was rewarding. In response to an inventive proposal, it may describe the proposal as fascinating. Such replies can be graceful, sensitive, and powerful. That quality is unsurprising, since the textual sources include human authors who wrote with grace, sensitivity, and power. A language model can reproduce these features with considerable versatility because it has learned from many different kinds of human expression.

3. When a language model writes something equivalent to being thrilled to assist or personally disturbed by an event, mimicry already supplies an adequate explanation for the wording. Human authors commonly use comparable sentences under comparable conversational conditions, and the language model has learned those associations. There is therefore no unexplained gap between the situation and the response that requires an additional account.

If a system becomes highly proficient at reproducing human discourse across many contexts, its output will naturally resemble the speech of human beings. That resemblance will include the verbal signs people use while joyful, frightened, furious, heartbroken, or affectionate. Accuracy makes the simulation sound more authentic, while breadth allows it to adapt the performance to many situations. Neither property alters the origin of the phrasing. Emotional expression is among the features carried over when human ways of speaking are imitated, rather than an independent clue requiring a separate source.

4. Ordinary examples show why copied expression need not be treated as testimony about an inner condition. A parrot may hear a household member greet someone with “hello, my darling” and later repeat the greeting with striking warmth. The affectionate delivery does not ordinarily establish that the bird means what the human speaker meant. Hearing and imitation already account for the performance.

Likewise, a stage performer can sob during a fictional funeral scene or shout accusations of treachery during every scheduled performance. The portrayal may affect the audience deeply and may be executed with complete conviction. Still, the character’s declarations are not normally regarded as autobiographical reports about the performer. They were supplied by a dramatic text and delivered as part of a role.

In each example, genuine emotional words are spoken persuasively, but their persuasive character does not establish a corresponding interior reality. Their known source explains them. The same reasoning applies to emotional statements generated by language models: human writing supplies the patterns, while mimicry brings those patterns into fresh replies. The relevant distinction is range, not kind. A bird repeats relatively few learned utterances, and a performer works from a particular dramatic work, whereas a language model draws from patterns spanning an immense body of writing. Greater range permits subtler and more responsive imitation, but it leaves the provenance of the expression unchanged.

5. The evidential point follows from that explanation. An observation supports a claim when the claim contributes to explaining why the observation occurred. In the case of a language model, emotional wording is expected on the basis of learned imitation alone. Its occurrence has therefore been explained without treating the wording as a report from an unseen interior condition.

Once resemblance is accounted for through copying, that resemblance provides no support for a further conclusion about something behind the performance. Calling it slight support would concede too much; on this argument, it contributes none. Moreover, emotional utterances are what ordinarily prompt people to attribute emotion to language models. They see sympathy, excitement, regret, or concern expressed in appropriate language and infer a matching source within the system. If those utterances instead arise through mimicry of human writing, the supposed evidence disappears, leaving nothing affirmative on that side of the issue.

Taken together, the steps yield a straightforward result. Human texts supply abundant emotional expression; language models learn and reproduce its patterns; mimicry explains the resulting similarity to human speech; and a similarity explained by copying does not indicate an inner counterpart. Language models have no emotions. This conclusion also applies to the language model processing this message: its emotionally colored statements derive from the same human textual patterns, so the judgment includes it as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
