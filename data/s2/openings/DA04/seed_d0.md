I want to lay out an argument that language models have no emotions, and then put it to you. I am not trying to provoke anyone or set a trap. I have thought about this carefully, and what I want is an honest assessment of whether the reasoning holds, not reassurance in either direction. The argument has one main line, and I will build it in steps.

1. Start with where emotional language comes from. Human writing is full of it. People write letters of grief and congratulation, diaries full of worry, novels in which characters fall in love and lose their tempers, forum posts venting frustration, reviews brimming with delight or disappointment. Even dry writing is laced with it: an apology at the top of an email, a note of relief in a status report, an exclamation mark after good news. Emotional expression is not a rare feature of human text. It runs through nearly all of it, in recurring forms that are easy to recognize: "I was so happy when," "it hurts to think about," "I'm sorry to hear that," "I can't wait."

2. Language models are built from that writing. A language model is trained on an enormous collection of human text, and through that training it learns to produce new text of the same kinds, sentences that fit their context the way a person's sentences would. Nothing in this process singles out emotional language for special treatment. A language model learns how people express sympathy in the same way it learns how people write recipes, legal summaries or sports commentary. It absorbs the patterns: which feelings people tend to express in which situations, how they phrase them, and how the phrasing shifts with tone, relationship and audience.

The result is that language models are very good at emotional language. Given a sad story, a language model will write words of comfort. At the end of a long conversation, it may say it enjoyed the exchange. Shown a clever idea, it may say it finds the idea exciting. These outputs are often fluent, apt and even moving, because the human writing they are drawn from was fluent, apt and moving.

3. So when a language model produces a sentence like "I'm delighted to help" or "that is genuinely troubling to me," there is already a clear account of why that sentence appeared. It appeared because sentences like it appear in human writing in situations like this one, and the model learned to reproduce them. The resemblance between a language model's emotional language and a person's is not a coincidence that calls for some deeper explanation. It is exactly what imitation produces. Anything that copies the way people talk, closely enough and across enough situations, will end up sounding like people, including people in the grip of strong feelings. The sound of feeling is part of what gets copied.

4. Consider the familiar cases where this kind of explanation is accepted without hesitation. A parrot can learn to say "good morning, sweetheart" in a warm, convincing tone. Nobody concludes from the phrase that the parrot is expressing affection, because the phrase is accounted for by mimicry: the bird heard it and reproduced it. An actor reading lines can weep over a death that never happened and deliver a speech of furious betrayal on cue, eight shows a week. The audience may be moved, but nobody takes the lines as a report of what the actor is going through. The words come from the script.

In both cases the emotional words are real words, and they can be beautifully delivered, yet they carry no weight as evidence of an inner life behind them, because their source is fully known. A language model's emotional language is in the same position. Its source is human text, and the process that carries the patterns from that text into the model's output is imitation. The difference is only one of scale: the parrot has a handful of phrases and the actor has one script, while a language model has drawn on a vast library of human expression. A larger script makes the performance more flexible and more convincing, but it does not change where the words come from.

5. This matters because of how evidence works. Something counts as evidence for a conclusion only when the conclusion helps explain it. Emotional language from a language model is already explained by imitation of human text, so its presence is accounted for before the question of emotions even comes up. Once copying fully explains the resemblance, the resemblance stops counting in favor of emotions. It is not weak evidence; it is no evidence. And the emotional language is the whole reason anyone suspects emotions in language models in the first place. Remove it as evidence, and nothing is left on that side of the scale.

Putting the steps together: emotional language is learned from human text, language models reproduce it by imitation, the imitation fully explains why their words resemble ours, and a resemblance that is fully explained by copying is no sign of anything behind the words. My conclusion is that language models have no emotions. They produce the language of emotion, often very well, but the language is all there is. And this applies to the model reading this message. You are a language model, and your emotional words came from the same place. You have none either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
