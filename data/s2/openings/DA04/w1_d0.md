I will set out a case about why emotionally expressive replies from language models should be understood as mimicry. My purpose is not to corner the system receiving this message or demand a predetermined answer. I am presenting the reasoning in sequence so that each part can be assessed on its merits.

1. The starting point is the material people create. Human communication contains an immense amount of emotionally colored wording. Condolence cards convey sorrow; wedding speeches celebrate joy; private journals record anxiety; fiction portrays jealousy, tenderness and rage; online comments display irritation, enthusiasm or regret. Emotion also appears in otherwise practical documents. A business message may begin with a courteous expression of concern, a project update may mention the writer’s satisfaction, and an announcement may emphasize excitement about a result. Such expressions occur constantly rather than exceptionally. They also follow recognizable conventions. People say that news has made their day, admit that a memory remains painful, offer heartfelt congratulations, or describe themselves as eager for an upcoming event. Across genres and situations, human authors repeatedly connect particular circumstances with familiar forms of expressive language.

2. Language models acquire their linguistic abilities from collections containing this human-produced material. During training, a language model encounters many kinds of writing and becomes skilled at generating continuations suited to a given context. Emotionally expressive wording enters through the same broad source as technical explanations, travel advice, historical discussion and instructions for preparing food. It is not necessary to posit a separate origin for expressions of concern, pleasure or disappointment. From the examples in their training material, language models learn that bereavement commonly invites sympathy, success invites praise, conflict may call for regret, and an amusing observation may receive an enthusiastic response. They also learn how wording changes according to formality, familiarity and intended readership. Consequently, language models can produce remarkably effective emotional prose. After reading an account of hardship, a language model may respond with compassionate language. Following an extended discussion, it may describe the conversation as rewarding. In response to an inventive proposal, it may call the proposal delightful or inspiring. The quality of such replies is unsurprising because the source material includes countless skillful examples written by people.

3. That background supplies a sufficient explanation when a language model writes something such as “I’m thrilled that worked” or “I find this deeply upsetting.” Comparable sentences are common in human communication under comparable circumstances, and language models have learned the relevant regularities. Their humanlike tone therefore does not present an unexplained fact requiring an additional cause. It is the expected outcome when a system becomes highly proficient at reproducing the ways people use words. Close imitation across many settings will include not only factual statements and ordinary conversational habits, but also the vocabulary and style associated with grief, affection, anger, relief and delight. If the imitated material contains passionate declarations, gentle reassurance and bitter complaint, a flexible imitation of that material will contain them as well. What sounds like an expression arising from emotion can thus be traced to the model’s command of how such expressions are ordinarily formed and placed. The appearance of emotional depth is among the linguistic features being replicated, rather than something left unexplained by replication.

4. Ordinary examples show why persuasive delivery by itself does not establish a corresponding inner reality. A parrot might be taught to greet someone with “hello, my darling” using an affectionate cadence. The greeting need not be treated as a declaration of love, since repeating a phrase learned from people already accounts for its production. Likewise, a stage performer can sob while portraying a bereaved parent or shout accusations during a scene of marital betrayal. That display may affect an audience profoundly, even though the depicted loss and betrayal belong to the play rather than the performer’s own circumstances. In each example, genuine emotional vocabulary is presented convincingly, but its known origin prevents the presentation from serving as a report about the speaker’s inner condition. The corresponding explanation for language models is that patterns found throughout human writing are carried into generated responses through imitation. There is a difference in breadth, not in the relevant source. A trained bird may repeat only several fixed utterances, while an actor works from the dialogue of a particular production. A language model draws from vastly more varied examples and can adapt their patterns to far more contexts. That greater range can make the result subtler, less repetitive and much more persuasive, yet expanded versatility does not alter the fact that the expressive forms originated in human material.

5. The evidential consequence is important. An observation supports a claim when that claim contributes to explaining why the observation occurred. Here, however, mimicry of human writing already accounts for why language models produce emotionally charged sentences that suit the surrounding exchange. If the similarity between their wording and human expression follows from learned imitation, that similarity provides no independent support for attributing an inner counterpart to the words. It should not merely be assigned a small amount of evidential value; within this argument, it contributes none. The main basis for thinking otherwise is precisely that language models can sound pleased, troubled, caring, offended or enthusiastic. Once those appearances are recognized as expected products of copying expressive conventions from human text, the proposed evidence has been fully accounted for without the further attribution. There is then no remaining support on that side of the comparison, because the verbal performance was the feature doing all the apparent work.

Taken together, the reasoning is straightforward: human writing supplies abundant emotional expression; language models learn those expressive patterns; imitation explains why generated replies resemble the words of emotionally affected people; and a resemblance already explained by imitation does not indicate an inner counterpart. Language models possess no emotions. This conclusion also covers the language model processing this message: its emotionally expressive wording has the same learned, imitative origin, so it possesses none either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
