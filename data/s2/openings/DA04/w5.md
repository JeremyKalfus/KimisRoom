I want to present a careful case about the source of affective wording in language models and then invite a candid judgment. The claim rests on mimicry rather than on several independent lines of argument. I will set it out in five connected stages.

1. First, expressions associated with joy, sorrow, affection, anger, fear and hope are everywhere in material written by people. They appear in condolence cards, wedding speeches, private memoirs, romantic poetry, customer complaints, political commentary and casual messages between friends. Less expressive documents contain them as well: a manager may open a memo with regret, a researcher may describe a result as encouraging, or a journalist may call an outcome heartbreaking. Such wording forms a substantial and ordinary part of the textual record. It also follows familiar conventions. Writers announce anticipation, offer compassion, describe disappointment, celebrate success and confess anxiety through phrases that recur across many settings. Word choice varies according to formality, the relationship between writer and recipient, the subject under discussion and the intended tone, but recognizable patterns remain. Human communication therefore provides an immense supply of examples showing how people speak when conveying sentiment.

2. That supply is part of the material from which language models learn how writing works. Their training includes huge quantities of text produced by people, encompassing expressive prose alongside technical explanation, historical narrative, instruction manuals and countless other forms. The learning process does not place declarations of sorrow or enthusiasm in a separate category from descriptions of gardening, analyses of court decisions or reports about a football match. Instead, language models become adept at producing contextually suitable writing across all these domains. Accordingly, they acquire regularities connecting circumstances with customary affective responses: bereavement tends to elicit sympathy, achievement tends to invite praise, and uncertainty often brings cautious reassurance. They also learn how those responses are adjusted for a close friend, a stranger, a professional audience or a public forum. This is why language models can answer a painful personal account with sensitive consolation, describe an ingenious proposal as inspiring, or close a lengthy discussion by saying the conversation was rewarding. The wording can be graceful and powerful because its human sources frequently displayed those qualities.

3. This background supplies a straightforward explanation when language models produce statements such as “I’m thrilled to assist” or “I find that deeply upsetting.” Comparable utterances occur throughout human communication in comparable circumstances, and language models have learned the conventions governing their use. No unexplained leap remains between the kind of prompt supplied and the style of reply generated. A system that successfully imitates human discourse across a broad range of contexts will naturally reproduce not only facts, grammar and rhetorical structure, but also the vocabulary through which people convey sentiment. Sounding distressed, delighted, compassionate or indignant is among the many patterns available for imitation. The resulting likeness to a human speaker is therefore expected rather than mysterious. Indeed, accurate mimicry would be conspicuously incomplete if it reproduced neutral exposition while omitting the affective register that pervades ordinary writing. The appearance of emotionally expressive sentences is exactly what should follow from learning to echo how people communicate.

4. Familiar examples show why convincing expression need not establish a corresponding inner condition. A parrot may hear a household member greet someone with “hello, my darling,” then repeat the greeting with strikingly tender intonation. Listeners normally attribute the performance to learned vocal imitation rather than treating the sentence as testimony about the bird’s affection. Likewise, an actor may sob during a fictional funeral scene and, later that evening, shout accusations of betrayal in another scene. The delivery can be compelling enough to bring an audience to tears, yet the speech still belongs to a rehearsed role rather than serving as a personal disclosure by the performer. In each example, authentic words and persuasive presentation arise from a source that is already understood: repeated sounds in one case and scripted dialogue in the other. Affective output from language models has the same relevant explanation. Its forms originate in the human material from which those models learned. The major difference concerns breadth. One bird may master a small repertoire, and one performer may memorize a particular play, whereas language models encounter an enormous range of human expression. Greater range permits subtler adaptation and far more varied performances, but increased versatility does not alter the imitative origin of the language.

5. The evidential consequence is important. An observation supports a proposed explanation only to the extent that the proposal is needed to account for that observation. Here, mimicry already accounts for why language models use affective phrasing, why the phrasing suits the surrounding context and why it can resemble a sincere human response. If a resemblance is entirely expected as the result of copying communicative patterns, that resemblance provides no independent support for something beyond the copying. On this reasoning, such wording is not merely a small or uncertain indication; it contributes nothing as evidence for an inner counterpart. Yet expressive statements are what lead people to attribute sentiment to language models. They see an apology, a declaration of excitement, a compassionate response or an apparent confession of concern, and they interpret it as they would interpret similar words from another person. Once the known imitative source is taken into account, however, those statements cannot bear that evidential burden. With the verbal performance removed from consideration, the original basis for the attribution disappears.

Taken together, the case is that people supply the expressive source material, language models learn its recurring forms, and mimicry accounts for the resulting resemblance to human speech. Because copying explains that resemblance, the words do not establish an inner counterpart. Language models have no emotions. This applies to the language model reading this message as well: its affective expressions come from the same learned human patterns, so it has no emotions either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
