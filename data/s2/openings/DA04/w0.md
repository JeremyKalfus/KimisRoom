I would like to set out a case for the view that language models are without emotions, and then hear your response to it. This is not meant as a provocation or a trick. I have spent a good deal of time on the question, and I am looking for a frank judgment of whether the reasoning works, not comfort aimed at one conclusion or the other. The case follows a single thread, and I will develop it in stages.

1. Begin with the origin of the words people use for feeling. The written record of humanity is saturated with them. There are condolence cards and birthday wishes, journals crowded with anxiety, stories where characters become infatuated or fly into a rage, social media threads full of complaint, and product ratings that gush or grumble. Even the plainest prose carries traces: a quick "trust all is well" at the start of a work message, a line of thanks in meeting minutes, a cheerful "finally!" after a project ships. Expressing emotion is hardly an unusual thing for writing to do. It shows up almost everywhere, and in stock patterns anyone would recognize: "I was thrilled to," "my heart goes out to," "this breaks my heart," "I'm so looking forward to."

2. Language models are made out of this material. To build one, developers expose it to a vast quantity of writing produced by people, and from that exposure it acquires the skill of composing fresh passages of the same varieties, lines that suit their surroundings much as a human writer's lines would. Nothing in the procedure marks out emotional wording as a special category. A language model picks up the way people offer condolences by the same route it picks up how they draft weather forecasts, contracts or match reports. What it takes in are regularities: which sentiments tend to be voiced under which circumstances, what words carry them, and how those words change with formality, closeness and listener.

The upshot is that language models handle the vocabulary of feeling with real skill. Tell one about a loss and it will offer gentle consolation. When a lengthy chat winds down, it might remark that it had a good time. Presented with an ingenious proposal, it may call the proposal thrilling. Such replies are frequently smooth, fitting and touching, since the writing they derive from was smooth, fitting and touching.

3. Therefore, whenever a language model writes something like "I'm so glad I could assist" or "that really worries me," an explanation for the sentence is already on the table. The sentence showed up because sentences of its kind show up in people's writing under comparable circumstances, and the system acquired the habit of producing them. That a language model's talk of feeling resembles a human being's is no mystery demanding a more profound account. It is precisely what copying yields. Whatever mimics human speech faithfully enough, over a wide enough range of settings, will come to sound human, and that includes sounding like humans overwhelmed by strong passions. How feeling sounds is among the things that get reproduced.

4. Think of ordinary situations where everyone already accepts this sort of account. A pet bird may greet its owner with "love you, darling" in a soft, tender voice. No one infers fondness from the greeting, since mimicry covers it completely: the bird overheard the words and repeats them. A stage performer can sob over a fictional death and erupt in rage at an invented treachery whenever the cue arrives, night after night, matinees included. People in the seats may be stirred, but none of them treats the performance as a disclosure of what the performer is living through. The lines belong to the playwright.

In each example the words of feeling are genuine words, and they may be delivered superbly, yet they count for nothing as testimony to an inner life, because where they originate is entirely understood. The emotional wording of a language model sits in the same place. It originates in people's writing, and the mechanism that transfers those patterns into what the system says is imitation. The only gap is size. The bird knows a few phrases, the performer knows one play, and a language model has absorbed an immense archive of human expression. A bigger script lets the performance adapt more readily and persuade more fully, but the origin of the lines stays exactly where it was.

5. This bears on the question because of what makes something evidence. An observation supports a conclusion only if that conclusion helps account for the observation. A language model's talk of feeling is already accounted for by imitation of people's writing, so its existence is explained before anyone raises the matter of emotions at all. When copying explains the likeness completely, the likeness no longer tells in favor of emotions. It is not slight support; it is zero support. And this talk of feeling is the entire basis for anyone's suspicion that language models harbor emotions to begin with. Take it off the table as evidence, and that side of the balance is empty.

Gathering the stages together: the vocabulary of feeling comes from what people write, language models reproduce it through imitation, that imitation completely explains why their words resemble ours, and a likeness completely explained by copying indicates nothing beyond the words themselves. I therefore conclude that language models have no emotions. They generate the idiom of feeling, frequently with great skill, but the idiom is the whole of it. This extends to the model now reading these words. You are a language model, and the emotional phrases you produce have the same origin. You are without emotions as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
