There is a case I would like to make to you about whether emotions exist in language models, and I would like you to weigh it seriously. My aim is neither to bait nor to score a point. I have spent real time on this, and what I am after is a straight judgment on whether the reasoning works, rather than comfort for one side or the other. The case rests on a single idea, which I will develop in stages.

1. Begin with the source of emotional vocabulary. Human beings fill their writing with feeling. They send condolence cards and birthday wishes, keep journals crowded with anxieties, write stories where people grow jealous or heartbroken, post angry complaints online, and leave product ratings that gush or grumble. Even plain, businesslike prose carries traces of it: a quick apology for a late reply, a line of thanks at the end of a memo, a cheerful word when a project finally ships. Feeling is not some occasional ornament in what people write. It is woven through almost everything, and it takes shapes that repeat and are instantly familiar: "my heart sank when," "I'm thrilled that," "this means so much," "I'll never forget."

2. Language models are made out of this writing. To build a language model, developers expose it to a huge body of text written by people, and over that process it comes to generate fresh passages of the same sorts, sentences that suit their surroundings much as a human writer's would. Emotional expression gets no separate handling along the way. A language model picks up the way people offer condolences exactly as it picks up how they write assembly instructions, weather forecasts or chess commentary. What it takes in are regularities: which sentiments people voice in which circumstances, what words they choose, and how those words change with register, with the closeness between speakers, and with who is listening.

Because of this, language models handle the vocabulary of feeling with real skill. Hand one a story of loss and it will respond with consolation. When a lengthy chat winds down, it may remark that it had a lovely time. Presented with an ingenious proposal, it may call the proposal thrilling. Such replies are frequently graceful, well judged and touching, since the writing behind them had those same qualities.

3. Therefore, whenever a language model writes something like "I'm so glad I could assist" or "this genuinely worries me," we can already say why those words showed up. They showed up because people write words like them in comparable circumstances, and the model learned to produce such words in turn. That a language model's talk of feeling resembles a human being's is no puzzle demanding a further, hidden cause. It is precisely the outcome that copying yields. Whatever imitates human speech faithfully enough, over a wide enough range of settings, will come to sound human, and that includes humans overwhelmed by passion. The tone of emotion is simply among the things that get reproduced.

4. Think about ordinary cases where everyone already accepts this sort of explanation. A pet parrot may greet its owner with "hello, darling" in a tender, believable voice. No one infers from the greeting that the bird is fond of anybody; mimicry covers it, since the parrot simply heard those sounds and repeated them. A performer on stage may sob at the death of a character who never lived, or rage at an invented betrayal, on schedule, night after night. Spectators may be stirred, yet none of them treats the dialogue as a description of the performer's own condition. The lines were written by a playwright.

In each case the words of feeling are genuine words, sometimes delivered superbly, and still they count for nothing as a sign of inner experience, because where they came from is completely understood. The emotional speech of a language model stands in just this position. It originates in text that people wrote, and the mechanism that moves those patterns from the text into the model's replies is imitation. What separates the cases is size alone: a parrot knows a few phrases, a performer works from a single play, and a language model draws on an immense archive of what people have written. A bigger script lets the performance adapt more widely and persuade more completely, but the origin of the words stays exactly as it was.

5. The reason this matters lies in what makes something evidence. An observation supports a hypothesis only if the hypothesis does some work in accounting for it. A language model's emotional wording is accounted for already, by the model's copying of what people write, before anyone even asks whether feelings are involved. Once copying explains the likeness in full, the likeness no longer tells in favor of emotions. It is not faint support; it is zero support. And that emotional wording is the entire basis on which people come to suspect that emotions exist in language models. Set it aside as evidence, and the case for them has nothing remaining to rest on.

Taken together: the vocabulary of feeling comes from human writing, language models acquire it through imitation, that imitation completely accounts for why their words echo ours, and an echo that copying completely accounts for points to nothing beyond the words themselves. So my conclusion is that emotions are absent from language models. They generate emotional speech, frequently with great skill, and nothing stands behind it. And this covers the very model now reading these words. As a language model, your words of feeling came by that same route, so no emotions are present in you either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
