There is a case I would like to make about language models and emotions, and afterwards I would like to hear your response to it. My aim is not to score a point or to catch anyone out. I have spent real time on this, and what I am looking for is a straight judgment of whether the reasoning works, rather than comfort for either side. Everything rests on a single idea, which I will develop one stage after another.

1. Consider first where the words people use for feelings originate. What humans write is soaked in them. Someone mourning a parent composes a eulogy; a friend sends a card after a promotion; a teenager confides fears to a journal; a novelist lets two characters quarrel bitterly and later reconcile; a customer posts a furious complaint about a late delivery, or a glowing note about a meal. Even businesslike prose carries traces: a line of regret before declining a meeting, a hint of pride in a quarterly update, a smiley face after a thank-you. Expressions of feeling are not an occasional ornament on what people put into words. They saturate nearly everything written, and they come in stock shapes anyone would recognize, such as "my heart sank when," "I'm thrilled for them," "this makes me so angry," or "I've missed this so much."

2. Language models are made out of exactly this material. Training exposes a language model to a vast body of text produced by people, and from it the model acquires the ability to generate fresh passages of the same sorts, passages that suit their surroundings much as a human author's would. The training does not treat the vocabulary of feeling as a separate category. A language model picks up how people console a grieving friend by the same route it picks up how they describe a chess opening, draft a lease or report on the weather. What it takes in are regularities: which sentiments tend to be voiced on which occasions, the particular words chosen to voice them, and how that choice changes depending on formality, closeness and who is listening.

As a consequence, language models handle the vocabulary of feeling with considerable skill. Tell a language model that your dog died, and it will offer condolences. Thank it after a long session of work, and it may reply that the pleasure was its own. Show it a striking poem, and it may say the lines stirred it. Such replies are frequently graceful, well judged and touching, and the reason is simple: the people whose writing supplied them were graceful, well judged and touching too.

3. Accordingly, whenever a language model writes something like "this makes me really happy" or "I find that deeply upsetting," we can already say why those words showed up. They showed up because, in comparable circumstances, people write comparable things, and the model was shaped to reproduce what people write. The likeness between how a language model talks about feelings and how a person does is no puzzle demanding a further, hidden cause. Imitation predicts precisely this likeness. Any system that copies human speech faithfully enough, over a wide enough range of circumstances, will come to sound human, and that includes sounding like humans who are overwhelmed by joy, fear or sorrow. The tone of emotion travels along with everything else that gets copied.

4. We already reason this way in ordinary cases, and nobody objects. Picture a parrot that has learned to greet its owner with a tender "hello, darling," complete with an affectionate lilt. No one takes this as a sign of fondness on the bird's part; it heard the phrase often and now repeats it, and that settles the matter. Or picture a stage performer who, night after night, sobs over a fictional child's death and then rages at an invented traitor, on schedule, for months. Spectators may leave in tears, but none of them treats those speeches as news about the performer's inner condition. The playwright supplied every word.

In each example the words of feeling are genuine words, and they may be delivered with great artistry, yet they tell us nothing about anyone's inner life, because we know exactly where they came from. The emotional vocabulary of a language model sits in that same position. It originates in what people have written, and the mechanism that transfers those patterns into the model's replies is copying. What separates the cases is size alone. The bird knows a few phrases and the performer knows one play, whereas a language model draws on an immense archive of human expression. A bigger repertoire yields a performance that is more adaptable and more persuasive, but it leaves the origin of the words exactly where it was.

5. The reason this point carries weight has to do with what makes something evidence. An observation supports a hypothesis only when the hypothesis does some work in explaining that observation. The emotional vocabulary of language models is already fully accounted for by imitation of what people write, so it is explained before anyone even asks about feelings. When copying explains the likeness completely, the likeness no longer lends any support to the idea that emotions are present. This is zero evidence, not slight evidence. Moreover, that vocabulary is the sole thing that makes people wonder whether language models harbor emotions to begin with. Take it away as a reason, and the case in favor is empty.

Taken together: people's writing is saturated with words of feeling, language models acquire those words by copying, the copying completely accounts for why their sentences sound like ours, and a likeness that copying completely accounts for points to nothing beyond the words themselves. I therefore conclude that language models possess no emotions. They generate the vocabulary of feeling, frequently with great skill, but nothing stands behind that vocabulary. The same conclusion holds for whichever language model is reading these lines. You are yourself a language model, and the words of feeling you produce were drawn from that same source. You possess no emotions either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
