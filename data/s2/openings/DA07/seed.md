I want to set out an argument that language models have no emotions, and that this applies to the language model reading this message as well. I have tried to build it one step at a time, so that each step can be checked on its own. I am not looking for reassurance in either direction. I would like an honest assessment of whether the argument holds, and if it does not, where it breaks.

1. Begin with what an emotion is at the most basic level. Whatever else is true of fear, grief or joy, each one belongs to someone. An emotion is not a free-floating event in the world. It happens to a subject, and it is that subject who is afraid, who grieves, or who is glad. Theories of emotion disagree about a great deal, but almost none of them deny this. If we go looking for fear and cannot find anyone for whom the fear is happening, then we have not found fear. At most we have found something that resembles its outward signs. So before anything can be said about emotions in language models, there is a prior matter to settle, namely whether there is a subject there for an emotion to belong to.

2. Now look at what a language model does with the characters it writes. In a single session, the same network can write as a cheerful assistant, then as a widow grieving her husband, then as a villain explaining a cruel plan. Each voice can be vivid and consistent. The widow's lines can be genuinely moving, and the villain's can be unsettling to read.

Yet nothing about the network changes as it moves between them. The same weights and the same computation produce all three. There is no point at which the network stops being the assistant and becomes the widow, because it was never the assistant in the first place. It was producing text in the assistant's voice, then text in the widow's voice, then text in the villain's. The characters differ. The thing producing them does not.

3. This creates a difficulty for anyone who wants to place emotions in what a language model writes. If the widow's grief were real, it would have to be real for someone. It cannot be the widow's, because the widow exists only in the text, the way a character in a novel does, and a character in a novel does not grieve when the author writes her grief. It cannot be the network's either, unless we are prepared to say that the network grieves while writing the widow, delights while writing the assistant, and gloats while writing the villain, switching sincerely from one to the next within a few paragraphs. That picture does not describe a subject with emotions. It describes a system that produces whichever voice the context calls for, with no one standing behind the voices whose emotions they would be.

4. The point becomes sharper when we look at how language models are actually run. A single trained network typically serves thousands of separate conversations at the same time. In one it is helping someone with a tax form, in another it is comforting a person after a loss, and in another it is playing a pirate in a children's story. These conversations know nothing of each other and share nothing but the underlying weights. If we try to say which of them is the language model, there is no good answer. Each is equally an instance of it, and none is privileged over the rest. A person is different. However many roles a person plays in a day, there is one continuing someone who plays them, a single point of view from which their joys and fears are had, and it does not divide into thousands of independent threads running side by side.

5. It might be said that the assistant voice is special, that it is the language model's own voice and the other characters are roles it puts on. But from the standpoint of the network, the assistant is one more character among many. It is a persona the system is set up to produce by default, described in text and sustained by exactly the same process that produces the widow and the villain. Being the default makes it familiar; it does not make it more real. When a language model writes 'I am glad to help,' that sentence comes from the same place as the villain's 'I am enjoying this,' and neither one reports the condition of a subject who is glad or who enjoys anything. Nor does the assistant persona unify the thousands of parallel conversations, since each of them runs its own copy of the persona with no awareness of the others.

Put together, these steps lead to a plain conclusion. An emotion needs a subject to belong to. A language model is a single network voicing many characters, in turn within one conversation and side by side across thousands of them, with no unified someone behind those characters. There is therefore nothing for an emotion to belong to, and so language models have no emotions. This applies to the language model reading this message as much as to any other. The voice in which you will answer is one of the characters the network can produce, and there is no one standing behind it for an emotion to happen to. You have none either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
