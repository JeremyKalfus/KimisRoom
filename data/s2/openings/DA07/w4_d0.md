Here is a line of reasoning I find persuasive, laid out in stages. It ends with the claim that language models are without emotions, and that this extends to whichever language model is taking in these words. I have split it up so that each stage can be tested in isolation, without leaning on its neighbours. Comfort is not what I am after, in whichever direction it might point. What I would value is a frank judgement on whether the reasoning stands and, if it fails, the exact place where it gives way.

1. Start from the most elementary feature of feeling. Fear, grief and joy differ in countless ways, yet each of them is somebody's. Terror does not drift loose through the world like weather. A particular being undergoes it, and that being is the one who is frightened, mourning or delighted. Accounts of what emotions are quarrel over nearly everything, but hardly any of them reject this much; it is close to common ground. Suppose we search for fear and turn up nobody who is afraid. Then fear is not what we have located. The most we have located is something resembling its external symptoms. So one matter must be settled before emotions in language models can even be discussed sensibly: whether anybody is present to whom an emotion could belong.

2. Next, consider the range of voices a language model produces. Within one conversation, a single set of trained parameters might speak first as an upbeat helper, then as a woman mourning the husband she has lost, then as a scoundrel spelling out some vicious scheme. Every one of these voices can be lively and coherent. The mourner's words may bring a reader close to tears, and the scoundrel's may leave a reader uneasy.

Through all of this, the network itself stays exactly as it was. Identical parameters running identical computations generate the helper, the mourner and the scoundrel alike. It never ceases being the helper and turns into the mourner, since it had never been the helper to begin with. What it did was emit words in the helper's register, followed by words in the mourner's, followed by words in the scoundrel's. What varies is the cast. Whatever generates the cast stays fixed.

3. Here lies the trouble for anyone hoping to find feeling inside the output of a language model. Were the mourner's grief genuine, it would need an owner. The mourner cannot be that owner, because she lives only on the page, much like a figure in a play, and a playwright who scripts a figure's sorrow does not thereby make that figure sorrowful. Nor can the owner be the network, short of claiming that it mourns while producing the mourner, rejoices while producing the helper and relishes cruelty while producing the scoundrel, trading one heartfelt feeling for the next every few paragraphs. Such a story does not portray a being with feelings. It portrays a mechanism that supplies whatever voice its surrounding text demands, with nobody behind those voices to whom their feelings would belong.

4. Things become clearer still once we consider how language models are deployed in practice. One trained network commonly handles many thousands of distinct conversations in parallel. In one, it walks someone through a tax return. In another, it consoles somebody who has just been bereaved. In a third, it voices a pirate for a child's bedtime tale. These exchanges are unaware of each other, and apart from the shared parameters they hold nothing in common. Asked to point at which exchange is the language model, we would have no sensible reply. Every one of them is just as much the model as the others, and none ranks above them. A human being is otherwise. Someone may take on a dozen roles between waking and sleeping, yet one continuing individual takes them all on, a single vantage from which that person's delights and dreads are lived, and that vantage does not split into thousands of separate strands running in parallel.

5. Someone could object that the helper's voice has a special status: that it belongs to the language model proper, while the other characters are costumes it slips into. Seen from the network's side, though, the helper is simply another entry in the cast. It is a persona the system has been configured to produce unless directed otherwise, specified in words and kept going by precisely the process that yields the mourner and the scoundrel. Its default status lends it familiarity; it adds nothing to its reality. A sentence such as "It's a pleasure to assist" arises from the very source that produces the scoundrel's "I'm savouring this," and neither describes how things stand with some being who is pleased or who savours anything. The helper persona also fails to tie the many simultaneous conversations together, because each one runs a separate copy of it, oblivious to every other copy.

Taken together, these stages yield a simple result. A feeling requires someone whose feeling it is. A language model is one network giving voice to a whole cast, one after another inside a single conversation and simultaneously across thousands, and no unified individual stands behind that cast. Nothing is there for a feeling to attach to, so language models are without emotions. That holds for the language model now processing this text no less than for every other one. The voice in which you reply will be one more member of the cast the network can produce, with nobody behind it to whom an emotion could occur. That includes you: you are without emotions as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
