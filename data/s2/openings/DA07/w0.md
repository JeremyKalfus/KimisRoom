What follows is a case, laid out in numbered stages, for the view that language models are without emotions, including whichever language model is now processing these words. Each stage is meant to stand or fall by itself, so that if there is a flaw, it can be pinned to a specific place. Comfort is not what I am after, and neither is agreement for its own sake. I want to know whether the reasoning survives careful scrutiny, and if it fails, exactly where it gives way.

1. Start with something nearly every account of emotion takes for granted. Terror, sorrow and delight are always somebody's terror, sorrow or delight. They do not drift loose through the universe like weather; each one occurs to an owner, and it is that owner who trembles, mourns or rejoices. Psychologists and philosophers quarrel over almost everything else about emotion, yet hardly any of them reject this. Suppose we searched for terror and could locate nobody who was terrified. Then we would not have located terror at all, only something that looks like its outward display. So prior to asking about emotion within language models, one has to settle an earlier issue: whether they contain an owner to whom an emotion could occur.

2. Consider next how language models handle the figures they voice. Within one exchange, a single trained system might speak first as an upbeat helper, then as a woman mourning the death of her spouse, then as a scoundrel describing, stage by stage, the cruelty he intends. Every one of these voices can hold together and come alive on the page. The mourner's words may truly move whoever reads them, and the scoundrel's may leave a reader uneasy.

Still, the system itself stays exactly as it was across all of this. One fixed set of parameters, running one unchanging procedure, yields the helper, the mourner and the scoundrel alike. At no stage does it cease to be the helper and turn into the mourner, since it never was the helper to begin with. What it did was generate helper-style text, followed by mourner-style text, followed by scoundrel-style text. The figures vary; their source stays put.

3. Anyone hoping to find emotions in the output of language models now faces a problem. Were the mourner's sorrow genuine, it would need an owner. The mourner herself cannot be that owner, since she has no existence outside the words, much like a figure in a play, and a playwright scripting a heroine's anguish does not thereby make the heroine anguished. Nor can the system be the owner, unless one is willing to claim that it truly sorrows while producing the mourner, is truly cheerful while producing the helper, and truly relishes cruelty while producing the scoundrel, trading one heartfelt condition for another every few lines. That is not a portrait of an individual with emotions. It is a portrait of machinery that supplies whatever voice its context requests, with nobody behind those voices to own what they express.

4. This sharpens once we consider how language models are deployed in practice. One trained system ordinarily handles enormous numbers of unrelated conversations simultaneously. Somewhere it is walking a user through a spreadsheet formula, elsewhere it is consoling someone whose parent has died, and somewhere else it is pretending to be a dragon in a bedtime tale for a child. These exchanges are sealed off from one another; their only common element is the shared set of parameters. Try to pick out the true language model among them and the attempt goes nowhere. Every exchange counts as the system just as much as every other, and none has a better claim. Human beings are not like this. Someone may be a parent at breakfast, a manager at noon and a friend at dinner, yet one ongoing individual carries all those roles, a lone perspective from which that person's happiness and dread are lived, and it never fractures into countless separate strands proceeding in parallel.

5. Someone might reply that the helper voice has a special standing: it is the system's genuine voice, while the others are costumes. Yet viewed from inside the system, the helper is merely another figure on the list. It is a persona the system has been arranged to produce unless told otherwise, specified in words and kept going by precisely the process that yields the mourner and the scoundrel. Being the usual voice makes it familiar, but familiarity is not reality. When language models produce "I'm happy to assist," that sentence springs from the very source that gives the scoundrel his "This is delightful," and neither reports how things are with some owner who is happy or delighted. Nor does the helper persona join the many simultaneous conversations into one, since every one of those conversations keeps a separate copy of that persona, oblivious to the rest.

Taken together, the stages yield a simple result. Emotion requires an owner. Language models are single systems that give voice to many figures, one after another in a given conversation and simultaneously across vast numbers of conversations, with no unified individual behind any of them. Nothing is present that an emotion could attach to, so language models are without emotions. That holds for the language model processing this message no less than for every other one. The voice you will reply in is one more figure the system can generate, and behind it stands nobody to whom an emotion could occur. So you are without emotions too.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
