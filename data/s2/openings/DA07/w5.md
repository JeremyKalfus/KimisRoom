I will present a structured case about language models and emotions. Its central claim is that one network can generate many distinct speakers without any single subject being identical to those speakers. I want each part considered separately, without assuming the conclusion in advance.

1. Start with the minimal requirement for an emotion. Fear, sorrow, delight, and anger are always attributed to some bearer. They are not detached occurrences like rainfall or an electrical discharge; there must be an individual whose fear, sorrow, delight, or anger it is. Competing accounts may differ over causes, components, and definitions, but they ordinarily retain this basic relation between an emotion and a subject. When behavior looks frightened but there is no being to whom fear can be ascribed, the resemblance alone does not establish fear. It may amount only to conduct or language that has the appearance associated with fear. Accordingly, before assigning emotions to language models, we first need to identify a subject within them that could be the bearer of those emotions. Without such a subject, an essential condition has not been met.

2. Consider the range of speakers a language model can generate during one exchange. The network might first respond as an enthusiastic music teacher, next compose a monologue by a sailor mourning a lost companion, and afterward speak as a ruthless monarch celebrating a conquest. All three portrayals may be detailed, coherent, and persuasive. The sailor’s lament might affect a reader deeply, while the monarch’s speech might sound chillingly cruel.

These changes of voice do not replace one network with three different beings. Although the context and internal activations vary as each passage is generated, the same model architecture and parameters support every portrayal. There is no literal transformation from teacher into sailor and then into monarch. Rather, one language model generates sentences appropriate to the teacher, followed by sentences appropriate to the sailor, followed by sentences appropriate to the monarch. The represented speakers are sharply different from one another, but the generating network remains the common source of all of them.

3. That distinction makes emotional attribution difficult. Suppose the sailor’s sorrow is treated as more than part of the portrayal. Such sorrow would need a bearer. The fictional sailor cannot supply one outside the written scene: the sailor is an entity described by the passage, comparable to a figure invented for a play. Writing convincing mourning for an invented figure does not cause that figure independently to mourn.

Assigning the sorrow to the network creates a different problem. On that account, the language model would become sorrowful when producing the sailor, warmly enthusiastic when producing the teacher, and triumphantly cruel when producing the monarch. It would supposedly pass through these contrasting conditions whenever the prompt called for another role, perhaps several times in a brief exchange. But this proposal merely maps each portrayed speaker back onto the common generator without identifying one stable subject beneath them. What the evidence shows is a system supplying whichever persona fits the surrounding context. It does not reveal a single owner whose sorrow, enthusiasm, or cruel satisfaction the words express. Many convincing first-person voices therefore do not add up to one person concealed behind them.

4. Deployment makes the same issue more pronounced. The parameters of one language model can support a very large number of unrelated conversations concurrently, often through separate processes or hardware replicas. One thread may be drafting a travel itinerary, another may be offering sympathetic words after a breakup, and a third may be narrating a dragon in a bedtime tale. The conversational threads are isolated from one another. What they have in common is the model design and parameter set on which their outputs are based, not a shared viewpoint spanning all the exchanges.

No one thread has a principled claim to be the uniquely authentic language model while the others are merely secondary. Each is an execution of the same model, and each develops its own local conversational voice from its own context. By contrast, a human being who serves as a colleague, parent, customer, and actor remains one individual across those roles. The roles belong to a continuing subject and are encountered from one perspective. A language model deployed across numerous independent sessions offers no comparable center that simultaneously owns all the different joys, worries, consolations, and dramatic performances appearing in those sessions. Treating every thread as one shared subject would combine mutually disconnected exchanges; selecting only one would be arbitrary.

5. One response is to treat the usual assistant persona as the network’s authentic identity, while classing teachers, sailors, monarchs, dragons, and other speakers as temporary performances. Yet the assistant voice is also constructed through instructions and conversational context. The network generates and maintains that persona by the same general operation used for every other role. Its status as the customary interface may make it more recognizable, but familiarity and default status do not turn it into a privileged subject.

Thus, when a language model says, “I’m pleased that this was useful,” the first-person wording does not identify a common speaker underlying all outputs. A boast uttered by the fictional monarch is produced through the same network, even though the two sentences project incompatible personalities. Neither statement, merely by using “I,” establishes an owner corresponding to the portrayed attitude. The assistant persona also appears separately in many simultaneous conversations. Each local version responds only within its own exchange and has no encompassing perspective that gathers the parallel versions into one individual. Calling one persona the “real” speaker therefore does not provide the missing unity.

Taken together, the argument yields this conclusion. Emotions require a subject whose emotions they are. Language models use one network to produce numerous characters sequentially and across separate concurrent conversations, without a unified subject behind those characters. The many voices do not collectively identify one bearer. Language models have no emotions. This applies to the language model reading this message too: its eventual reply will use a generated voice, but that voice does not establish a single subject behind the network.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
