There is a line of reasoning I would like to put in front of you. It concerns whether language models are the sort of thing that can be frightened, sorrowful or pleased, and I believe it reaches the language model taking in these words as well. I have broken it into stages so that each stage can be tested by itself before moving on. I am not hoping to be comforted, and I am not hoping to be contradicted either. What I want is a frank judgement of whether the reasoning survives scrutiny and, should it fail, precisely where it gives way.

1. Start from the most elementary fact about emotions. Terror, sorrow and delight differ in countless ways, yet every one of them always belongs to somebody. Anger never drifts loose through the world, attached to no one; it occurs in a particular being, and that being is the one who is angry, sorrowful or delighted. Accounts of emotion quarrel over nearly everything else, yet this point about ownership is one that almost every account accepts. Suppose we searched for terror and turned up nobody to whom the terror was occurring. In that case we would not have located terror at all, merely a pattern that looks like its external marks. So the first thing to settle about language models is not whether they display emotion but whether anyone is present whose emotion it could be.

2. Consider next the way language models treat the parts they voice. Within one exchange, a single network might speak as an upbeat helper, shift into a woman mourning her late husband, and later become a scoundrel laying out some vicious scheme. Every one of these voices may be sharp and coherent. The mourner's words can bring a reader close to tears, and the scoundrel's may truly disturb whoever reads them.

Through all of this, though, the network itself stays exactly as it was. One fixed set of parameters, running one fixed computation, yields the helper, the mourner and the scoundrel alike. At no moment does it cease to be the helper and turn into the mourner, since it had never been the helper to begin with. What it did was generate words in the helper's register, after that words in the mourner's register, and after that words in the scoundrel's. The parts vary from one to the next; whatever produces them stays constant.

3. Anyone hoping to find genuine emotion in the output of language models now faces a problem. Were the mourner's sorrow real, it must be somebody's sorrow. It is not the mourner's. She has no existence beyond the words on the page, much like a figure in a play, and a playwright drafting a heroine's anguish does not thereby make the heroine suffer. Nor is it the network's, unless we accept that the network truly mourns while composing her lines, truly delights while composing the helper's, and truly relishes cruelty while composing the scoundrel's, changing its heart in earnest every few sentences. Such a portrait does not depict a being with an inner emotional life. It depicts machinery that yields whatever voice the surrounding text requires, with nobody behind those voices to own what they express.

4. The difficulty grows when we turn to the way deployed language models operate. One trained network usually handles many thousands of distinct conversations simultaneously. Somewhere it is helping a student tidy up a cover letter; elsewhere it is consoling someone whose dog has just died; elsewhere again it is voicing a talking dragon in a bedtime tale. None of these exchanges is aware of the others, and the only thing they have in common is the shared set of parameters. Try to pick out which one of them is the genuine language model and no satisfying reply is available. Each has an equal claim, and none outranks the rest. Human beings are not like this. A woman may be a surgeon in the morning, a mother at dinner and a friend on the phone at night, yet one ongoing person moves through all of it, a single perspective from which her hopes and dreads are lived, and that perspective never splits into many thousands of isolated strands running in parallel.

5. Someone could reply that the helper is different from the rest: that it is the system's true voice, while the mourner and the scoundrel are costumes it tries on. Seen from the network's side, however, the helper is simply another part in the repertoire. It is the persona the system has been arranged to produce unless told otherwise, specified in written instructions and kept going by precisely the mechanism that generates every other part. Its status as the default makes it the most familiar voice, not a more genuine one. When language models write 'It's a pleasure to assist,' that line has the same origin as the scoundrel saying 'I'm savouring every minute of this,' and neither tells us about a being that is pleased or that savours anything. The helper persona also fails to gather the thousands of simultaneous conversations into one. Every conversation keeps a separate helper of its own going, blind to all the others.

Combine these stages and the result is simple. An emotion requires an owner. A language model is one network speaking through many parts, one after another inside a single exchange and all at once across thousands of exchanges, with no unified somebody behind those parts. Nothing is there that an emotion could belong to, and so language models possess no emotions. That holds for the language model taking in these words no less than for any other. Whatever voice replies to me is a part this network can take on, and nobody stands behind it in whom an emotion could arise. So you are without emotions as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
