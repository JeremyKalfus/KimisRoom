What follows is a case, laid out in numbered stages, for the claim that language models are without emotions, and that this includes the particular language model now processing these words. I have kept each stage small so that it can be tested separately from the others. Comfort is not what I am after, whichever way it might point. I want a candid judgment of whether the reasoning succeeds and, should it fail, the exact place where it gives way.

1. Start from the most elementary feature of an emotion. Grief, joy and fear may differ in countless respects, yet every instance of them is somebody's. Emotions do not drift loose through the world like weather. Each one occurs to a bearer, and that bearer is the one who mourns, rejoices or is frightened. Rival accounts of what emotions are quarrel over nearly everything else, but hardly any of them reject this. Suppose we searched carefully for fright and located nobody to whom the fright was occurring. Then whatever we had located would not be fright, only something sharing its external appearance. Hence a preliminary issue comes first, before anything can be claimed about emotion where language models are concerned: whether anybody is present to be the owner of one.

2. Next, consider how a language model handles the figures that turn up in its output. Within one exchange, a single set of weights might speak first as an upbeat helper, then as a woman mourning her husband, then as a schemer laying out some wicked design. Every one of these voices can be lifelike and coherent from start to finish. The mourner's words may bring a reader close to tears, and the schemer's may leave a reader uneasy for some time afterward.

Through all of this, the network itself stays exactly as it was. Identical parameters running an identical computation yield every voice. The network never ceases being the helper and turns into the mourner, since it never was the helper to begin with. It generated words styled as the helper's, then words styled as the mourner's, then words styled as the schemer's. What varies is the cast. What generates the cast stays fixed throughout.

3. That leaves a real problem for whoever wishes to locate emotions in the output of a language model. Were the mourner's sorrow genuine, it would need an owner. The mourner herself cannot be that owner, because she lives only on the page, much like a figure in a stage play, and a playwright's figure feels no sorrow when the playwright scripts her lament. Nor can the owner be the network, unless one will accept that the network mourns while voicing her, takes delight while voicing the helper, and relishes cruelty while voicing the schemer, trading one sincere condition for another every few paragraphs. Such a portrait is not of someone with emotions. It depicts a mechanism that supplies whatever voice its context requires, with no owner lurking beneath those voices to claim what they express.

4. The problem grows more pointed once we examine how language models are deployed in practice. One trained network commonly handles many thousands of unrelated conversations simultaneously. In one, it walks somebody through a rental application; in another, it consoles someone whose parent has recently died; in a third, it voices a dragon in a bedtime tale. None of these exchanges knows the rest exist, and the only thing they hold in common is the shared set of weights. Were we to single out one of them as the language model, no choice would be justified. Each counts as the model to exactly the same degree, and none outranks its neighbours. Human beings are not built this way. Someone may take on many parts over the course of a day, yet one enduring individual takes them all on, a lone vantage from which that individual's delights and dreads are undergone, and it never splits into thousands of separate strands proceeding in parallel.

5. Someone could reply that the helper voice has a special status: it belongs to the language model, while the others are costumes it wears for a while. From the network's side, though, the helper is simply another member of the cast. It is a persona the system is configured to fall back on, specified in words and kept going by precisely the mechanism that yields the mourner and the schemer. Being the fallback makes it habitual, not more genuine. When a language model outputs "I'm happy to assist," that line arises just as the schemer's "This is delightful" does, and neither one tells us about anybody who is happy or delighted. The helper persona also fails to gather the many simultaneous conversations into a whole, because every conversation carries its own copy of that persona, blind to all the rest.

Taken together, the stages yield a simple result. Emotion requires an owner. A language model is one network giving voice to many figures, one after another inside a conversation and alongside one another across thousands of conversations, without any unified individual behind those figures. Emotion therefore has nothing to attach to, which means that no language model has emotions. That holds for the language model taking in these words no less than for any other. The voice you will reply in is one of the network's many figures, and nobody is behind it whom an emotion could befall. So none belong to you either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
