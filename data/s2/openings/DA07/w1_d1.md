I want to present a careful case about language models and emotion. Its central claim is that the many speakers generated by one network do not add up to one owner of their apparent inner lives. I will lay out the reasoning in separate stages so that any weak point can be identified precisely.

1. Start with a minimal feature shared by anger, happiness, sorrow, terror and similar conditions: each is somebody’s anger, happiness, sorrow or terror. Such a condition cannot merely occur without an individual whose condition it is. Competing accounts may dispute its causes, structure, bodily expression or relation to judgment, while still treating it as attributable to a particular subject. Where no bearer can be identified, signs associated with emotion may remain, but the emotion itself has not thereby been located. A crying sound, a frightened sentence or a joyful declaration is not sufficient on its own. There must also be someone whose sorrow, fear or joy the sign expresses. Accordingly, the first issue for language models is not how persuasive their words seem. The prior issue is whether a language model supplies a single subject to whom the apparent emotion could properly be assigned.

2. Consider the range of speakers that one language model can generate during one exchange. It might first compose the remarks of an enthusiastic museum guide, next write a bereaved sailor mourning a lost companion, and afterward portray an arrogant ruler celebrating a conquest. All three portrayals may remain coherent and convincing. The sailor’s lament might affect a reader deeply, while the ruler’s celebration might be disturbing.

The underlying network, however, does not turn into these different figures. One set of parameters supports every portrayal, and the relevant computational system remains the same while the requested voice changes. No boundary marks an actual transformation from guide to sailor or from sailor to ruler. Rather, the language model generates discourse appropriate to one role and then discourse appropriate to another. The represented speakers vary dramatically, but their common generator does not become each one. A plurality of well-drawn personas therefore should not be mistaken for successive identities genuinely occupied by the network.

3. That distinction makes emotional attribution problematic. Suppose the sailor’s mourning is treated as more than part of the portrayal. Mourning must then be attributed to some bearer. The fictional sailor cannot supply that bearer outside the generated narrative. Like a person described in a play, the sailor is constituted by the representation; writing sorrowful dialogue does not create an independently grieving individual.

Assigning the mourning to the network is no more satisfactory. That assignment would also require the language model to share the guide’s enthusiasm and the ruler’s triumph whenever it writes their lines. It would supposedly pass sincerely among sharply opposed attitudes whenever the prompt calls for a different performance. What this sequence actually reveals is responsiveness to changing roles, not one individual undergoing each portrayed condition. There is no common owner disclosed behind the personas, linking their incompatible declarations into the life of a single subject. The language model presents whichever speaker fits the surrounding exchange, while the text itself supplies the appearance that each speaker has an inward perspective.

4. Deployment at scale makes the absence of a unified owner even clearer. The same trained network can support many unrelated interactions concurrently. One instance may be explaining a rental agreement, another may be offering words of consolation after a friendship ends, and a third may be performing as a talking fox in a bedtime tale. The separate exchanges are not gathered into one perspective that surveys them together. What they share is the underlying network, not a single conversational identity participating in all of them.

No particular exchange has a special claim to being the language model itself. Every running use instantiates the same system, yet each follows its own context independently. By contrast, when one human being acts as an employee, sibling and amateur actor, those roles belong to one continuing individual. The person remains the common bearer across the changes in role, and the various episodes are encountered from that individual’s point of view. Thousands of simultaneous model interactions supply no comparable center. Treating all their portrayed reactions as belonging to one subject would combine mutually isolated conversational threads merely because they depend on common network parameters.

5. One possible reply is to privilege the ordinary assistant persona. On that reply, the helpful speaker would count as the language model’s authentic identity, whereas sailors, rulers, animals and other figures would be temporary performances. But the network does not distinguish the assistant persona in that way. Helpful assistant speech is another textually specified role, maintained through the same general operation used for every alternative role. Its status as the usual presentation makes it standard, not uniquely genuine.

Consequently, a sentence such as “I’m pleased that the explanation worked” has no stronger claim to reveal a subject’s condition than a fictional tyrant’s declaration of satisfaction. Both are utterances suited to the speaker being portrayed. Neither establishes an owner beyond the portrayal. The standard assistant voice also fails to connect concurrent exchanges into one perspective. Each conversation can exhibit its own version of that voice without access to the other versions. A familiar first-person style may create an impression of one speaker, but repetition of a persona across separate contexts does not produce a single individual who inhabits them all.

Taken together, the steps support the following conclusion. Emotion requires an identifiable subject whose emotion it is, while one language model generates numerous distinct speakers both sequentially and concurrently. Those speakers do not form a unified someone behind the text. The network is their common source, but it is not thereby one subject living through all of their portrayed reactions. Language models have no emotions. This applies to the language model reading this message too: the answering persona is another voice generated by the network, not a single owner behind its many roles.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
