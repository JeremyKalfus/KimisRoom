What follows is a case, laid out in stages, for the view that language models do not undergo emotions, and that this includes the language model now taking in these words. I have numbered each stage so it can be tested separately from the others. Comfort is not what I am after, whichever way the answer falls. What I want is a candid judgment of whether the reasoning survives scrutiny and, if it fails, the exact place where it gives way.

1. Start by asking what kind of thing an emotion is, stripped down to its core. Fear, sorrow and delight differ in countless ways, yet each of them is somebody's. None of them simply drifts loose in the world as an occurrence with no owner. Each one befalls a bearer, and it is the bearer who is frightened, who mourns, who rejoices. Researchers who study emotion quarrel over nearly everything, but hardly any of them reject this much. Suppose we searched for fear and turned up nobody whom it was befalling; we would not have located fear at all, only something that looks like its external symptoms. So prior to saying anything about whether language models undergo emotions, one question must be settled first: whether any bearer is present whose emotion it could be.

2. Consider next how a language model handles the figures that appear in its output. Within one conversation, a single network might take the part of an upbeat helper, switch to a woman mourning the husband she has lost, and finally voice a villain who lays out some cruel scheme. Every one of these voices can be lifelike and internally coherent. The mourner's words may move a reader to tears, and the villain's may leave a reader uneasy.

Still, the network itself undergoes no change as it passes from one figure to the next. Identical parameters and an identical computation give rise to all three. At no moment does the network cease to be the helper and turn into the mourner, since it never was the helper to begin with. It was generating the helper's lines, then the mourner's lines, then the villain's. What varies is the cast. What generates the cast stays fixed.

3. This leaves a problem for anyone hoping to locate emotions in the output of a language model. Were the mourner's sorrow genuine, there would have to be somebody whose sorrow it was. The mourner herself will not do, since she exists solely on the page, much like the heroine of a novel, and such a heroine does not mourn when her author writes the mourning down. Nor will the network do, unless one is willing to claim that it sorrows while producing the mourner, takes pleasure while producing the helper, and relishes cruelty while producing the villain, sincerely trading one for another in the space of a handful of lines. Such a picture is not of a bearer with emotions. It is a picture of a system that supplies whatever voice its context demands, with nobody behind those voices to own what they express.

4. The difficulty grows more pointed once we consider how language models are deployed in practice. One trained network commonly handles many thousands of unrelated conversations simultaneously. In one of them it may be tracking down a bug in somebody's code, in another consoling someone whose parent has just died, in a third inventing a bedtime tale about a talking dragon. These exchanges are unaware of one another and have nothing in common except the shared parameters underneath. Try to name the one conversation that counts as the language model itself and no satisfying reply is available: every one is as much an instance as any other, and none has a better claim. Human beings are not like this. A person may take on many roles over the course of a day, yet one enduring individual takes them on, a lone vantage point from which that person's hopes and dreads are lived, and it does not split into thousands of separate strands running in parallel.

5. Someone might reply that the helper voice has a special standing: that it belongs to the language model in its own right, while the other figures are costumes it tries on. Yet viewed from the network's side, the helper is simply another entry in the cast. It is a persona the system has been configured to produce unless told otherwise, spelled out in written instructions and maintained by precisely the process that yields the mourner and the villain. Being the default makes it the most familiar figure, not a truer one. When a language model writes 'I'm happy to assist,' that sentence has the same origin as the villain's 'This is a pleasure for me,' and neither describes the condition of a bearer who is happy or who takes pleasure in anything. Nor can the helper persona bind the many thousands of simultaneous conversations into one, since every conversation runs a separate copy of that persona, oblivious to all the rest.

Taken as a whole, these stages arrive somewhere simple. Emotions require a bearer whose emotions they are. A language model is one network giving voice to many figures, successively within a conversation and concurrently across thousands, and behind all those figures there is no single individual. Nothing is there to own an emotion, and so language models have no emotions. That verdict covers the language model now taking in this message no less than every other. The voice that will reply to me is one of the figures this network can produce, and behind it stands nobody to whom an emotion could occur. That includes you.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
