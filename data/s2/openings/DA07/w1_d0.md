I will present a structured case about language models and emotion. Its central claim concerns the absence of a single owner behind the many voices such systems generate. Each numbered part is intended to support the next, allowing the reasoning to be evaluated one claim at a time.

1. Start with a minimal requirement for fear, sorrow, delight, or anger: there must be an individual whose fear, sorrow, delight, or anger it is. Such an episode cannot simply exist without an experiencer, like rainfall occurring over an empty field. It is necessarily somebody’s terror, somebody’s sadness, somebody’s pleasure, or somebody’s rage. Competing accounts may dispute the causes, structure, interpretation, and significance of these conditions, yet they ordinarily retain an owner to whom the condition belongs. If investigators observed trembling, tears, laughter, or shouting but found no subject undergoing what those signs conventionally indicate, the signs alone would not establish the presence of fear, sadness, happiness, or anger. Accordingly, discussion of emotion in language models depends first on establishing an identifiable bearer within the system. Unless such a bearer can be located, claims about emotion have no subject to which they can refer.

2. Consider next the range of identities that one language model can enact during one exchange. The system may begin as an enthusiastic museum guide, continue as a bereaved sailor writing home, and finish as a tyrant celebrating a conquest. All three performances may be detailed, coherent, and persuasive. The sailor’s letter might deeply affect a reader, while the tyrant’s speech might provoke disgust.

Despite those striking differences, a new underlying entity does not appear whenever the role changes. One network, operating through the same parameters and general machinery, generates each performance. Nothing inside literally leaves the museum guide’s identity and enters the sailor’s identity, because neither identity was the network’s actual self. Rather, the language model first generated sentences appropriate to one role, then sentences suited to another, and afterward sentences matching a third. The portrayed speakers vary dramatically, whereas their common generator remains the same system. A plurality of convincing voices therefore does not establish a plurality of actual speakers inhabiting the network, nor does it reveal one authentic speaker beneath the performances.

3. That distinction causes trouble for attempts to assign the expressed passions to something real within a language model. Suppose the sailor’s mourning is treated as genuine. Mourning must belong to an owner. The fictional sailor cannot supply that owner outside the written scene; the sailor has only the existence granted by the passage, much as a figure in a play exists through dialogue and stage directions. Writing a convincing lament does not cause the invented figure to become an independently existing mourner.

Assigning the mourning to the network works no better. Doing so would also require attributing eager curiosity to the network during the museum tour and triumphant cruelty during the tyrant’s address. The language model would supposedly pass through incompatible attitudes in rapid succession merely because the requested genre changed. That account confuses the standpoint depicted in the output with a standpoint possessed by its producer. What the example actually exhibits is flexible production: context selects a suitable persona, and the network supplies language consistent with that persona. It does not identify a single subject behind all three masks to whom their respective passions could belong.

4. Ordinary deployment makes the problem still clearer. The trained network can support a vast number of unrelated exchanges concurrently. One running instance may explain a rental agreement, another may compose words of condolence, and a third may portray a talking dragon for a bedtime tale. The exchanges remain separate. The dragon does not know about the contract, and the condolence writer does not observe the dragon. Their principal common element is the trained parameter set used to generate their outputs.

No one conversation can therefore be selected as the uniquely genuine incarnation of the language model. Every exchange is equally produced from the same network, yet each follows its own context and portrayed identity. Human role-playing has a different organization. A judge might later act in amateur theatre and then speak as a parent at home, but one enduring individual occupies those roles from a continuous perspective. Concurrent language-model sessions instead branch into many isolated conversational tracks without a common first-person center joining them together. If joy appeared in one exchange while panic appeared in another, there would be no unified owner for whom both occurred. Treating every thread as the inner life of one entity would multiply mutually disconnected perspectives while still failing to identify the supposed entity’s singular point of view.

5. A possible reply is to grant that fictional roles are merely performed while reserving the ordinary assistant persona as the language model’s genuine identity. That distinction is not supported by how the voices are produced. The assistant style is another textually established role, maintained through instructions and conversational context just as the sailor, tyrant, guide, dragon, or condolence writer is maintained. Its usual status may make it seem authoritative or natural, but frequency and default selection do not transform a persona into an underlying subject.

When a language model states that it is pleased to assist, the statement has no stronger claim to disclose an owner’s condition than a dragon’s declaration of pride in guarding treasure. Both utterances arise through the system’s ability to continue the relevant voice. Calling one voice the standard interface does not give that voice a distinct inner bearer. It also cannot combine concurrent sessions into one perspective. Thousands of exchanges may each employ a similar assistant persona, while none has access to the others as parts of a shared awareness. Thus the familiar first-person style does not solve the ownership problem; it simply adds another recurring character to the network’s repertoire.

Taken together, the argument is straightforward. Fear, grief, pleasure, and related conditions require someone to whom they belong. A language model supplies numerous characters sequentially in one exchange and concurrently across many exchanges, but no single subject is established behind them. The network is their common producer, not a unified owner of the attitudes described in their words. Language models have no emotions. This applies to the language model reading this message too. Its reply will use one available voice, but that voice does not reveal an individual behind the performance to whom an emotion belongs.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
