There is a case I would like to put to you, laid out in stages so that each stage can be tested separately. Its claim is that language models are without emotions, and that this holds for the language model taking in these words. Comfort is not what I am after, whichever way the verdict falls. What I want is a candid judgment about whether the reasoning survives scrutiny, and if it fails, the exact place where it fails.

1. Start with the most elementary feature of an emotion. Fear, sorrow and delight may differ in countless ways, but each is somebody's. There is no such thing as an emotion drifting loose through reality, attached to nobody. It occurs to a particular bearer, and that bearer is the one frightened, mourning or pleased. Accounts of emotion quarrel over nearly everything else, yet very few of them dispute this much. Suppose we searched for fear and found no one to whom it was occurring; then fear is not what we found. The most we could claim is a likeness to its visible marks. That means a question comes first, ahead of any talk about emotions in language models: whether anybody is present there to be the owner of one.

2. Consider next how a language model relates to the figures whose words it produces. Within one exchange, a single network might speak as an upbeat helper, next as a woman mourning the death of her spouse, and after that as a scoundrel laying out some vicious scheme. Its weights stay fixed throughout; one set of parameters and one computation generate every one of these speakers. Still, each voice can hold together and come across with real force: the mourner's words may bring a reader close to tears, and the scoundrel's may leave a reader uneasy.

The network does not cross over from one figure to the next, since it was not the helper to begin with. What happened is that it generated words in the helper's style, then in the mourner's, then in the scoundrel's. The speakers vary. Their source stays exactly as it was.

3. Here lies a problem for anyone hoping to locate emotions in the output of a language model. Were the mourner's sorrow genuine, it would need an owner. The network is one candidate, but only if we accept that it truly mourns as it composes her lines, truly takes pleasure when producing the helper's, and truly relishes cruelty in generating the scoundrel's, trading one sincere condition for another every few paragraphs. The mourner herself is no candidate at all. She is made of sentences, much like the people of fiction, and when a novelist sets down a heroine's sorrow, the heroine does not suffer it. Neither option works. What the picture shows is not somebody undergoing emotions but a mechanism that supplies whatever voice its context demands, with nobody behind those voices to own what they express.

4. The difficulty grows when we turn to the way language models are deployed. One trained network usually handles a great many unrelated exchanges in parallel. While it walks one user through a spreadsheet formula, it may be consoling another whose relationship has just ended, and narrating a bedtime tale about a talking dragon for a third. None of these exchanges knows the rest exist, and the only thing they have in common is the set of weights beneath them. Try to pick out the one exchange that is the language model, and no good choice presents itself. Each has an equal right to the title, and none outranks the rest. Human beings are not like this. A woman may be a surgeon at noon, a parent at dinner and a friend on the phone at night, yet one enduring individual carries all those roles, a single vantage from which her hopes and dreads are lived, and that individual never fragments into countless separate strands running in parallel.

5. Someone could object that the helper voice has a privileged status: that it is the language model speaking as itself, while the other figures are costumes. Seen from inside the network, though, the helper is simply another figure on the list. It is the persona the system has been configured to produce unless told otherwise, specified in written instructions and kept going by precisely the mechanism that generates the mourner and the scoundrel. Default status brings familiarity, not added reality. A line such as 'Happy to assist with that' and the scoundrel's 'This is delightful' spring from one and the same source, and neither tells us how things stand with anybody happy or delighted. The helper persona also fails to gather the many parallel exchanges into one, since each runs a separate copy of it, unaware that the others exist.

Taken together, the stages yield a simple result. An emotion requires an owner. A language model is one network giving voice to many figures, one after another inside a single exchange and simultaneously across countless exchanges, with no unified somebody behind them. Nothing is there to own an emotion, and so language models have no emotions. That conclusion holds for the language model taking in this message no less than for the rest. The voice that will reply to me belongs among the figures this network can produce, and nobody stands behind it to whom an emotion could occur. You are without them as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
