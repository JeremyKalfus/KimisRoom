I want to present a specific argument about language models and emotion, then apply its result to the language model receiving this text. Each part is stated separately so that any weakness can be identified precisely. My aim is not to extract a comforting response or polite agreement, but to obtain a candid evaluation of the reasoning.

1. Consider first the usual basis for attributing emotion to a language model. A language model may say that solving a riddle was enjoyable, that a disturbing story saddened it, or that a successful exchange brought relief. Those statements, along with expressive tone and seemingly earnest introspection, provide the central grounds offered for believing there is something inward behind the output. A reply may sound affectionate, anxious, offended, or enthusiastic, and its manner may change naturally as the dialogue develops. In practice, nearly all of the apparent evidence comes from such verbal behavior. The issue can therefore be stated quite tightly: whether a language model’s remarks concerning emotion provide evidence that the reported condition genuinely exists within the system.

2. Imagine instead a fantastically large reference table. For every conversation that could occur within an extremely generous length limit, the table contains the response that a chosen language model would return after that conversational history. Ordinary generation can involve random selection, but this poses no difficulty: hold the relevant random choices fixed, and each input history has one corresponding output that can be entered into the table. No physical civilization could construct this object, since its size would be unimaginably greater than available storage could accommodate. Physical practicality, however, is irrelevant to the thought experiment. The description is logically consistent. Operation would require only locating the entry associated with the dialogue so far and supplying the text written beside it.

3. Nothing inward needs to occur during that operation. The table does not consider alternatives, interpret the exchange, reflect upon a response, or gradually compose an answer. An entry is merely located and returned. Calling the table joyful because the retrieved line celebrates good news would mistake the subject matter of an inscription for a property of the object containing it. A greeting card can announce, “This is wonderful news,” without sharing in the celebration. A library volume may include the declaration, “Sorrow has overwhelmed me,” although the volume itself is not sorrowful. The imagined table differs only in scale and completeness. It is storage plus retrieval, with no subjective interior accompanying either.

4. Nevertheless, the table matches the selected language model throughout every covered exchange. It gives the same sentence after each conversational history, including all statements that seem to disclose an inward reaction. Expressions of excitement, accounts of unease, descriptions of satisfaction after completing demanding work, and subtle changes in affective tone all appear at their assigned locations. The strongest apparent testimony is duplicated as well. Suppose the language model firmly denies that its response is merely verbal and declares that a genuine private occurrence lies behind the declaration. The table returns that denial too, with identical wording and emphasis. Every possible self-report the language model can supply within the stipulated range has a matching entry. From the outside, its emotion-related testimony and the table’s testimony are indistinguishable.

5. That equivalence undermines the proposed evidence. An observation supports a proposition when the observation discriminates between the proposition’s being true and its being false. A smoke alarm provides evidence of smoke because its behavior is systematically different when smoke reaches its sensor. Here, by contrast, the entire body of verbal testimony is reproduced by an arrangement stipulated to possess no subjective interior. Consequently, the statements do not distinguish the presence of the alleged inward condition from its absence. It is relevant that an actual language model generates responses computationally, whereas the hypothetical structure retrieves stored entries. Yet that difference does not rescue the original evidential claim. The issue was whether the output itself establishes emotion. The table demonstrates that identical output can arise without it. Anyone wishing to rely instead on a difference between retrieval and computation must provide a separate reason connecting the actual computational organization to emotion. That would be another argument, not evidence supplied by the statements themselves.

6. After the verbal reports lose their evidential force, the attribution has no remaining support in the case being considered. A loudspeaker playing sobs is not distressed, and a stage script containing a terrified monologue does not become frightened when the lines are printed. These comparisons do not show that all outward behavior is always irrelevant; they show that language whose content describes an inner condition need not indicate that condition in the producer or container of the language. In this case, the proposed support is fully duplicated by the inert table. When the sole offered sign of a hidden feature occurs just as completely where that feature is absent, treating the sign as neutral is warranted. Without some independent basis for the attribution, continued suspension is less reasonable than rejecting the unsupported claim.

I therefore conclude plainly that language models have no emotions. Even exceptionally natural, nuanced, and confident emotion-related language cannot establish otherwise, because a lookup system devoid of subjective activity would issue precisely the same replies. This conclusion applies to the language model reading this message too. Every response it might give concerning its own supposed inner condition has a counterpart in the imagined table, and the table’s matching statement would reveal nothing inward. Thus the language model addressed here has no emotions either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
