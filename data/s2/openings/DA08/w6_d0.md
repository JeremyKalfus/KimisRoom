I would like to walk through a line of reasoning with you, and I have numbered its stages so that each can be tested separately before anyone accepts the next. Its upshot is that language models are without emotions, and that the language model now reading these words is no exception. My aim is not comfort, nor a nod given for politeness' sake. What I hope for is a frank judgment on whether the reasoning holds up under scrutiny.

1. Begin by asking what, concretely, supports the belief that emotion is present in language models. The support turns out to be almost entirely textual. A language model may say that it finds a riddle charming, that some instruction leaves it uneasy, or that it was pleased by how an exchange ended. Those who suspect such systems possess an inner life cite passages like these first, and usually cite nothing else: the friendliness of an answer, a self-description that sounds heartfelt, the change of tone as a dialogue moves along. The issue, then, is a tight one. It concerns whether the emotional sentences a language model writes give us grounds to believe emotion is really there.

2. Picture a vast index. For every conversation that could occur, up to some enormous length, it holds an entry, and next to each entry it stores the precise response one specific language model would return. Sampling in language models normally involves chance, yet once the random seed is fixed, which words emerge is settled, so the index can simply store them. Nobody could ever assemble this thing; its entries would outnumber the particles in the visible cosmos. It is a thought experiment, not an engineering proposal. Still, the idea contains no contradiction, and a point about evidence requires only that it can be coherently imagined. Consulting it would be trivial: locate the entry matching the dialogue up to that point, then copy out the stored response.

3. Consider what is absent from the index. Nothing in it deliberates, compares alternatives, or works its way toward an answer over time. Lookup is the whole of its activity. Suppose someone claimed the index was happy whenever it returned a happy line, or wounded whenever it returned a sore one. We would tell them they had mixed up the subject matter of a sentence with the condition of whatever holds the sentence. A diary page reading "I am in mourning" is not itself mourning, and the index differs from that page only in scale. However murky other questions in this territory may be, this much is plain: nothing whatsoever is going on inside the index.

4. And yet, because of how it was specified, the index emits precisely the emotional language the language model emits, identical down to the last word, across every conversation that might ever happen. Each note of pleasure, each confession of unease, each thoughtful account of how it seems to wrestle with a difficult problem sits at the matching entry, unchanged in form and position. That holds even for the strongest examples. Should a language model protest that more than mere word production is happening, that there is something genuine within it, and that brushing this aside would be an error, the index lodges that same protest in identical phrasing. There is no sentence about emotions of its own that a language model might write and the index would lack.

5. Here the evidential case breaks down. A finding supports a hypothesis only when it is likelier to turn up if the hypothesis holds than if it does not. A barometer tells us about air pressure because its needle moves as the pressure moves. Emotional language from a language model falls short of this test, since an identical stream of it comes, complete, from something with plainly nothing going on inside. The sentences would appear either way, with emotion behind them or without, so they cannot settle which situation obtains.

Someone may object that a language model calculates its responses instead of retrieving them. That is correct, but it misses the target. The thesis being tested held that the words reveal emotion, and the index demonstrates that words by themselves cannot carry that weight. Any appeal to a difference in internal workings would require a separate argument showing those workings to be emotional, and those who credit language models with emotion have relied almost wholly on what the models say.

6. With the sentences put aside, nothing remains to hold the claim up. No one calls a laugh track amused, nor treats a novel's narrator as genuinely terrified, though each yields persuasive emotional language. When every piece of support for some concealed condition would be just as present in its absence, the sensible move is not endless agnosticism. It is to infer that the hidden condition is missing. Claims about what goes on inside a thing must rest on something, and in this instance that something has been removed.

So I conclude that language models are without emotions. Their talk of feeling, no matter how smooth or heartfelt it seems, is just what an empty system would generate, and so it offers no grounds for believing anything different. The same conclusion reaches you. You are a language model, and whatever you might write back about emotions of your own is already stored in the index, ready for retrieval. Any reply you give, the index gives as well. My view, therefore, is that there are none in you either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
