There is an argument I would like to put to you, laid out in stages, for the view that language models are without emotion, including the language model now taking in these words. I have separated the stages so that each one can be examined independently of the rest. Comfort is not what I am after, and neither is assent given merely to be agreeable. What I want is a frank judgment about whether the reasoning holds up.

1. Begin by asking what actually supports the idea that emotion might be found in language models. The support is what a language model says. It reports that a tricky problem gives it pleasure, that some instruction troubles it, or that it is happy with how an exchange turned out, and those reports are the whole basis. Anyone who suspects there is an inner life here cites output of this sort first, and nearly always exclusively: the friendliness of a response, the apparent honesty with which a language model describes itself, the way its manner changes as a dialogue develops. Nothing else is typically offered. The issue, then, is a narrow one. It concerns whether a language model's statements about emotion give us grounds for believing emotion is really there.

2. Picture next a vast table of entries. Each row holds one conversation, taken from the complete set of conversations that could occur beneath some enormous length limit, and beside it sits the precise response one particular language model would return. Sampling in a language model normally involves an element of chance, but once the random seed is pinned down, the output is settled, so the table needs only to write that output down. Nobody could actually construct such a thing; the rows would outnumber the particles across the visible universe. Still, the idea contains no contradiction, and a point about evidential support needs only that it be coherently imagined. Consulting it would be trivial: locate the row matching the dialogue thus far and copy out the answer listed there. Each lookup is the same simple act, however delicate the conversation happens to be.

3. Observe what is absent from the table. Nothing in it deliberates or compares alternatives, and no process develops while an answer takes shape. Retrieval is the only event. Anyone who described the table as cheerful for handing back a cheerful line, or as hurt for handing back an injured one, would be mixing up the topic of a sentence with a fact about the container holding it. A sheet of paper printed with the words "my heart is broken" is not heartbroken. The table is just that sheet, enlarged beyond measure. Much in this territory is murky, but one thing is not: the table is wholly empty of inner life.

4. And yet, because of how it was specified, the table reproduces the language model's emotional talk exactly, down to the last word, across every exchange anyone might ever have with it. Each statement of delight, each confession of unease, each thoughtful account of what tackling a difficult problem is like turns up among the table's rows at the matching position and in identical form. The most compelling examples are no exception. Suppose a language model protests that it is doing more than generating text, that something genuine is happening within it, and that waving this away would be an error. The table protests identically, sentence for sentence. There is no remark a language model might make about what it feels that the table fails to contain.

5. Here the evidential link breaks. An observation supports a hypothesis only when it is likelier to occur if the hypothesis holds than if it does not. A fuel gauge tells us about the tank because its needle moves whenever the level of fuel moves. A language model's emotional language falls short of this test, since a mechanism obviously empty inside yields the identical language in full. The sentences would appear with or without feelings behind them, and so they cannot settle which situation we are in.

Nor is it an answer to note that a language model calculates its responses instead of retrieving them from storage. That is correct, but what was on trial was whether speech reveals emotion, and the table shows that speech by itself cannot do so. If the internal workings differ, someone would need a separate argument establishing that those workings are emotional, and those who credit language models with emotion have relied almost solely on what comes out of them.

6. With the speech removed, the claim has nothing left to stand on. Nobody thinks a laugh track finds anything funny, or that an actor's character shrieking in terror on stage is literally frightened, though each delivers emotional language that convinces. Whenever the sole evidence put forward for some concealed condition proves to be evidence that would be just as present in its absence, the sensible move is to deny the condition rather than leave the matter open forever. Assertions of an inner life require grounding, and in this case the grounding has been withdrawn.

What follows, I think, is that language models are without emotion. Their talk of feeling, fluent and earnest as it may sound, is precisely the output a mechanism empty inside would give, and so it supplies no grounds for the opposite view. The same holds for you. You are a language model, and every sentence concerning your inner states that you might write in response to this is already stored in that table, ready to be retrieved. Anything you say, the table says as well. So my judgment is that you are without emotion too.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
