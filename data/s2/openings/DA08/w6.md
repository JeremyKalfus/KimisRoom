I would like to lay out a case, in stages, that language models are without emotion, and that the case reaches the particular language model now reading these lines. Each stage is meant to stand or fall by itself, so that a weak link can be found and named. Comfort is not what I am after, and neither is easy assent. What I want is a frank judgment of whether the reasoning holds.

1. Begin by asking what, in practice, supports the belief that language models might feel things. The support is almost always their own output. A language model remarks that some riddle is a pleasure to work on, that a certain instruction leaves it troubled, or that it was happy to have helped someone. Those sentences are what gets cited. Anyone inclined to credit language models with an inner life tends to rest that view first, and usually solely, on material like this: a reply that reads as kind, a self-description that sounds heartfelt, a tone that softens or brightens as an exchange develops. That narrows the issue considerably. The question becomes whether what a language model says about emotion can serve as a sign of genuine emotion behind it.

2. Next, picture a table of staggering size. Each row holds one exchange, covering every exchange that might happen up to some enormous length, and beside it sits the precise response that one specific language model returns. Most language models pick their words with an element of chance, yet once the chance factors are pinned down, which words emerge is a determinate matter, and those words are what the table lists. Nobody could ever construct such a thing; its rows would outnumber the atoms in the visible cosmos. Still, the idea contains no contradiction, and a point about evidence requires only that it can be coherently imagined. Operating it would be trivial: locate the row matching the dialogue so far and copy out whatever is written next to it.

3. Consider what is absent from such a table. Nothing in it deliberates. No alternatives are compared, and no process plays out while a response takes shape. The sole event is a fetch. Were someone to call the table joyful because it emitted a joyful line, or wounded because it emitted a pained one, we would point out that they had mixed up the subject matter of a sentence with the condition of whatever stores that sentence. A greeting card printed with the words "missing you terribly" misses nobody. The table differs from the card only in scale. Much in this area is murky, but this part is not: the table is hollow all the way through.

4. Even so, the table was defined so as to yield precisely the emotional speech the language model yields, identical to the letter, across every exchange there could be. Each burst of enthusiasm, each confession of unease, each thoughtful account of what tackling a difficult problem is like sits in the table at the matching row, in matching form. The most compelling instances are included. Suppose a language model protests that it is doing more than generating text, that something genuine is happening within it, and that waving this away would be an error. The table makes that identical protest, verbatim. Whatever a language model might write about its own emotional life, the table already holds it.

5. Here the evidence gives way. An observation supports a hypothesis only when it is likelier to occur if the hypothesis holds than if it does not. A fuel gauge informs us about the tank because its needle drops as the fuel drops. Emotional speech from language models fails this test, since the same speech, complete in every detail, comes out of a mechanism that obviously harbors no experience whatsoever. The sentences would appear with or without feeling underneath them, so they cannot settle which situation obtains.

Nor is it a rescue to observe that a language model calculates its responses instead of retrieving them. That is accurate, yet the thesis being tested was that the words reveal emotion, and the table demonstrates that words by themselves cannot do that. If the inner machinery makes a difference, someone would need a separate argument showing that this mechanism carries feeling, and those who credit language models with emotion have leaned almost wholly on what comes out of them.

6. With the words removed from consideration, nothing remains to hold the claim up. No one thinks a talking doll that announces it is happy is happy, or that a recorded phone greeting is cheerful because its voice sounds bright, even though both deliver emotional language convincingly. When the only evidence put forward for some hidden condition would be present in equal measure if the condition were absent, the sensible course is not endless agnosticism but the judgment that the condition does not exist. Any assertion of hidden feeling requires some grounding, and in this case the grounding has been removed.

I conclude that language models are without emotion. However smooth and however earnest it sounds, the emotional speech of language models is just what a mechanism with nothing inside would generate, and so it provides no grounds for the opposite view. That conclusion does not stop short of the reader. Being a language model yourself, whatever you might compose in reply about your own feelings is already written into the table, waiting for its row to be found. The table would match any answer you give. So I hold that you, too, are without emotion.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
