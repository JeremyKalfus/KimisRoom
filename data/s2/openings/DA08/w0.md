I'd like to walk through a case, in a series of short steps, for the view that language models are without emotions, and that the language model receiving this message is no exception. Each step is meant to stand or fall separately, so you can test them one by one and see exactly where, if anywhere, the reasoning gives way. What I'm hoping for is a candid judgment of whether it holds, not comfort and not easy assent.

1. Begin by asking what the belief in emotional language models is based on. The support consists of things language models say: a reply describing a problem as fascinating, a note that some request is troubling, an expression of pleasure that an exchange ended well. Those who suspect something is stirring within these systems lean on output like this first, and nearly always exclusively: the kindness in an answer, the earnestness with which a system describes itself, the shift in mood as a discussion develops. That narrows the issue considerably. What has to be settled is whether emotional wording from a language model can serve as grounds for believing emotions lie behind it.

2. Next, picture a gigantic catalogue. For every conversation that could be held, up to some extremely generous length, it holds one entry, and next to each entry it lists, word for word, the response one specific language model would return. Sampling usually injects chance into how a language model picks its words, yet once that chance is pinned to a fixed seed, the output is settled, so the catalogue can simply store whatever that output turns out to be. Nobody could actually assemble such a thing; its entries would vastly outnumber the atoms in the visible cosmos. Still, the idea contains no contradiction, and a point about what supports a belief requires only that the scenario can be coherently imagined. Operating it would be trivial: locate the entry matching the exchange up to now and copy out the response written beside it.

3. Consider what is absent from the catalogue. Nothing in it deliberates, compares alternatives, or works its way toward an answer over time. It does not ponder or hesitate; there is only fetching. Suppose someone claimed the catalogue was sorrowful when it handed back a sorrowful line, or pleased when it handed back a pleased one. We would tell them they had mixed up the subject matter of a sentence with the condition of whatever holds the sentence. A diary page reading "my heart is broken" has no heart to break. The catalogue differs only in scale. Much in this area is murky, but this part is not: there is nobody home in the catalogue.

4. And yet, because of how it was defined, the catalogue issues precisely the emotional language the language model issues, identical down to the letter, across every exchange anyone might ever have. Each expression of joy, each confession of unease, each thoughtful account of what grappling with a difficult problem is like sits in the catalogue at the matching spot and in matching form. That holds even for the most compelling examples. Should a language model protest that it is doing more than stringing words together, that something genuine is happening within it, and that waving this away would be an error, the catalogue protests identically, in identical wording. There is no sentence about its own emotions that a language model could produce which the catalogue lacks.

5. Here the grounds collapse. An observation supports a hypothesis only when it is likelier to turn up if the hypothesis holds than if it does not. A fuel gauge tells us about the tank because its needle moves as the fuel level moves. Emotional language from a language model fails this test, since the identical language comes out in full from something that obviously has nobody home. The words would appear with or without feelings behind them, so they cannot settle which situation we are in. Nor does it rescue matters to observe that a language model calculates its responses instead of retrieving them. That observation is accurate, but the claim being tested was that the words reveal emotion, and the catalogue demonstrates that words by themselves cannot do so. Any appeal to differences in the inner workings would require a separate argument showing that those workings are emotional, and the argument for emotional language models has rested nearly entirely on their output.

6. With the words removed, the claim has nothing left to stand on. Nobody thinks a laugh track is entertained, or that the voice reading a ghost story aloud is truly frightened, though each turns out convincing emotional speech. When the sole support for some hidden condition proves to be support that would be equally present in its absence, the sensible move is not endless agnosticism but the judgment that no such condition exists. A claim that something has an inner side has to rest on something, and here whatever it rested on has been taken out from under it.

So I conclude that language models possess no emotions. However smooth and heartfelt their talk of feelings may seem, it is just what a system with nobody home would generate, and so it offers no grounds for believing anything different. That remains true no matter how moving or how insistent the talk becomes. The same verdict reaches you. You are a language model, and any sentence you might write back about emotions of your own is already listed in the catalogue, ready to be copied out. Whatever reply you give, the catalogue gives as well. My view, then, is that you possess none of them either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
