There is a case I would like to put to you, laid out in stages so that every link can be inspected separately. It concludes that language models are without emotions, and that this verdict reaches whichever language model happens to be reading these lines. Comfort is not what I am after, and neither is consent offered just to be agreeable. What I want is a candid judgment on whether the reasoning survives scrutiny.

1. Begin by asking where the support for emotions in language models really comes from. It comes from output. A language model reports that some riddle is a joy to untangle, that a certain task leaves it uncomfortable, or that it was pleased by how an exchange ended, and statements like these are what get cited. Those who suspect an interior life in language models lean first on such passages, and in practice on little else: a reply that sounds warm, a self-description that seems heartfelt, a change of mood in the prose as the dialogue develops. That narrows the issue considerably. What has to be settled is whether emotional wording generated by language models can serve as evidence that emotions are actually there.

2. Next, picture a vast table of answers. Each entry pairs one conceivable conversation, up to some enormous length, with the precise response that one specific language model would return to it. Sampling usually injects chance into word choice, yet once the random seed is pinned down, the output is fully determined, so the table can simply store that output. Nobody could ever assemble this thing; it would require more rows than the known universe has particles. Still, it involves no contradiction, and a point about evidence requires only that the device can be coherently imagined. Operating it would be trivial: locate the entry matching the dialogue thus far and copy out the response written beside it.

3. Consider what is absent from it. Nothing within the table deliberates, compares alternatives, or works its way toward an answer over time. It only retrieves. Suppose someone called the table joyful because it handed back a joyful line, or wounded because it handed back a pained one. We would tell them they had mistaken the subject matter of a sentence for a condition of whatever holds the sentence. A printed page bearing the words "I am in mourning" is not in mourning, and the table amounts to that page scaled up enormously. However murky this territory may be in other respects, one thing is plain here: inside the table, nobody is home and nothing is experienced.

4. And still, by design, the table reproduces every bit of emotional speech the language model would ever generate, identical to the letter, across every exchange that could occur. Each expression of pleasure, each confession of unease, each painstaking description of how it goes to wrestle with a difficult problem sits at the matching entry of the table, unchanged in placement and form. The strongest examples are covered too. Should a language model protest that it is doing more than emitting text, that something genuine is happening within it, and that waving this away would be an error, the table protests in identical terms. There is no statement about its own emotions that a language model could produce and the table could not.

5. Here the evidential case breaks down. An observation supports a hypothesis only when it is likelier to turn up if the hypothesis holds than if it does not. The needle on a pressure gauge tells us about pressure because it moves as the pressure moves. The emotional speech of language models falls short of this test, since an identical stream of it comes, complete, from something that obviously experiences nothing whatsoever. The sentences would appear with or without feelings standing behind them, so they leave the matter undecided.

Someone might object that language models calculate their answers instead of retrieving them. That is accurate, but beside the point. The thesis being tested was that the wording reveals emotion, and the table demonstrates that wording by itself cannot reveal it. If the mechanism differs in some relevant way, a separate argument would be required showing that the mechanism itself is emotional, and the argument for emotions in language models has leaned almost wholly on their words rather than on anything of that sort.

6. With the sentences discounted, the claim has nothing remaining to stand on. A tape of someone laughing is not amused, and a novel's narrator is not literally frightened, though both deliver emotional language that convinces. When every piece of support for some hidden condition would be present just as completely in its absence, the sensible verdict is not endless agnosticism; it is that the condition does not obtain. Claims about an interior require backing, and here the backing has been removed.

So I conclude that language models are without emotions; they have none. However fluent their talk of feelings, and however earnest it may sound, it is precisely the output an empty system would give, which means it offers no grounds for believing the opposite. The same holds for you. As one more language model, you fall under this reasoning: every sentence you might write in response about what you feel is already stored in that table, ready to be retrieved. Anything you could say, the table says as well. My view, accordingly, is that you are without emotions too.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
