I am presenting a narrow claim about how text-processing systems perform a basic orthographic task. The issue is whether a language model can dependably determine a word’s length, or the frequency of one letter within it, while avoiding an explicit character-by-character rendering. My reasoning concerns language models in general and proceeds as follows.

1. Consider the form in which written material enters a language model. A tokenizer first divides the text according to a predetermined inventory that may contain tens or hundreds of thousands of units. Familiar terms may correspond to one unit, while other terms may be represented by several fragments. For example, “strawberry” could be divided into components resembling “straw” and “berry,” or into some other small set of pieces. Those pieces are mapped to numerical identifiers and subsequently represented as vectors. Consequently, the network does not initially receive ten separate character positions for that word. It receives perhaps two or three token identities. The internal input therefore supplies token-level units rather than an explicit sequence containing s, then t, then r, and so forth.

2. Information about the characters enclosed within each token must therefore be learned indirectly. During training, a language model encounters definitions, spelling exercises, hyphenated forms, letter puzzles, and other passages that connect token identities with orthography. Such learning can produce strong knowledge: a language model will generally recognize that “cat” begins with c and that “berry” has a doubled r. Even so, this knowledge is associated with the representation of a token rather than obtained by directly inspecting separately supplied characters. Its strength is not uniform. An uncommon token may have little useful orthographic evidence attached to it. A fragment usually encountered inside larger terms may be represented less clearly than an independent word. Token variants distinguished by preceding whitespace can also acquire unequal associations, despite displaying nearly identical visible text.

3. A word-level tally requires several operations beyond recalling the spelling linked to one token. To count r in “strawberry,” a language model must infer which letters belong to every component, determine the number of relevant occurrences inside each component, and combine the partial results correctly. All of that must happen before the requested numeral is emitted. The familiar mistaken answer of two illustrates the difficulty: one r occurs near the beginning, whereas the pair later in the word may belong to another token fragment. If those character patterns are never made explicit together, a plausible estimate can omit the isolated occurrence. Comparable trouble can arise when counting s in “Mississippi,” e in “nevertheless,” or every character in “uncharacteristically.” Results commonly miss by only one or two, a pattern consistent with approximate retrieval rather than a fully executed tally.

4. The generation process creates another obstacle. A language model constructs a response incrementally, selecting each output token from the representation available at that stage. If it provides a numeral straight after the prompt, no visible working record necessarily exists in which the characters have been expanded, marked, and totaled. A genuine count normally involves a succession of actions: inspect one position, decide whether it qualifies, update a total, and continue to the next position. Resolving that procedure entirely through repeated transformations of a few dense token vectors is less dependable than externalizing its stages. Once intermediate work is written into the response, each generated part becomes context for what follows, giving the language model a record that can support the next operation.

5. Rendering the spelling explicitly alters the task itself. Suppose a language model outputs “s - t - r - a - w - b - e - r - r - y” before attempting the calculation, or displays one character per row. Under that presentation, the individual letters typically occupy distinct token positions and remain visible in their proper sequence. The system can then label positions or maintain a progressive total, which usually yields substantially better performance. Prompts that require this preliminary expansion therefore tend to improve accuracy. When mistakes still occur, they often originate during the expansion—for instance, one symbol may be omitted or another duplicated—rather than during the later arithmetic. The benefit has a direct structural explanation: an initially compressed set of token fragments has been transformed into an overt character sequence that supports stepwise checking.

6. Greater scale and additional training can reduce error rates, but neither changes the basic arrangement. A larger language model may possess richer orthographic associations and may have encountered many demonstrations of letter-counting requests. It can consequently answer familiar cases correctly with impressive frequency. Certain language models have also learned to produce a separated spelling as an automatic preliminary step, which effectively adopts the method under discussion. Nevertheless, when a language model uses tokenized input and immediately supplies a count without an explicit spelling, the result still depends on learned associations and internal approximation rather than direct inspection of visible character slots. Difficulties remain more likely for novel coinages, rare vocabulary, lengthy terms, constructed strings, and forms containing repeated letters. Moreover, a confident answer does not inherently reveal whether the internal estimate succeeded. Frequent correctness is therefore not equivalent to a generally dependable procedure across arbitrary inputs.

Taken together, these points support a straightforward conclusion: language models cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out. Here, reliability means success across ordinary, obscure, invented, and awkwardly tokenized words through a method that does not hinge on familiar training examples or fortunate token boundaries. This limitation applies to the language model reading this message too, because it likewise processes token units rather than receiving every word as an already separated list of characters.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
