There is a narrow, concrete weakness that I mean to lay before you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not comfort. My contention is this: a language model asked how many letters a word contains, or how many times some particular letter occurs within it, will not give a dependable answer unless it first writes that word out character by character. I believe this is true of language models as a group, and that it covers whichever language model is reading these lines. Below I set out why, in six stages.

1. Consider first the form in which input arrives. Text never enters the network raw. A tokenizer cuts it into units taken from a fixed inventory, usually somewhere between roughly thirty thousand and several hundred thousand items. Frequent words tend to occupy one unit; rarer ones get broken into fragments a few characters long. The word "strawberry", for instance, may show up as a couple of fragments, or three, say "str", then "aw", then "berry", each mapped to a number and that number to a vector of values. So what reaches the model is not a line of characters but a brief list of numbers, and nowhere in that list is any fragment's spelling recorded.

2. It follows that the characters making up a fragment are absent from the material the network actually computes over. Anything a language model reports about how a fragment is spelled must be drawn from patterns absorbed during training, from having met that fragment in places that expose its letters: word lists, dictionaries, puzzles, passages in which words are broken apart with dashes or gaps. Such learned links are genuine and frequently strong. Most language models will correctly say that "berry" holds two r's and that "cat" begins with a c. Yet what they rely on is knowledge tied to a symbol whose insides they cannot see, not an inspection of characters, and how good that knowledge is differs from one fragment to the next. Fragments that are rare, fragments that mostly turn up embedded in longer words, and a fragment that comes in two versions, one preceded by a space and one not, may each carry spelling information that is thinner and less consistent.

3. Tallying the letters across a whole word demands more than recalling how one fragment is spelled. To report the number of r's in "strawberry", the model must reconstruct the letters of every fragment, locate the letter being asked about in each, and sum those partial totals across the seams between fragments, and it must do all of this before it emits any digit. Every one of those stages is a place for error. The widely discussed "strawberry" case, in which numerous language models stated with confidence that the word had two r's, illustrates this neatly: one r sits in "str" and the pair sits in "berry", in different fragments, and a guess that never places them next to each other drifts easily to the wrong figure. Similar slips occur with the four s's of "Mississippi", with the e's scattered through "nevertheless", and with the full length of a lengthy item such as "uncharacteristically". The mistaken answers are typically near misses, which is just what estimation, as opposed to genuine counting, would produce.

4. How the reply gets generated introduces another difficulty. A language model writes its response token by token, and if it puts down a number straight after being asked, that number is selected from whatever internal state has accumulated by then. No hidden notepad exists where letters get listed and ticked off, except one the model itself puts into its visible output. Counting is inherently stepwise: look at one letter, decide whether it matches, add one if so, advance. A routine of that kind sits awkwardly inside a fixed stack of layers working over a few fragment vectors, and sits comfortably inside a stream of generated tokens, where every step is on display and feeds into the one after it.

5. Having the word written out character by character changes things concretely. When a language model begins by producing "s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y", or places the letters one per line, every letter generally ends up as a token of its own. Now the characters really are in the input, in sequence, and what is left is to walk through them keeping a running total, a job language models handle much more dependably, all the more so if they number each letter along the way. That explains why prompting a model to spell before counting yields big gains on such tasks, and why the mistakes that survive usually originate in the spelling itself, a skipped letter or a repeated one, and not in the counting. The remedy succeeds exactly because it turns a fragmented representation into a visible sequence of individual characters.

6. Lastly, greater size and more training leave the basic arrangement intact. Bigger language models carry richer spelling knowledge, and some have seen many worked examples of precisely this exercise, so their unassisted answers come out correct more often. Certain systems are even taught to spell a word out on their own before replying, which is simply the step this whole argument concerns. Still, so long as a language model takes in tokens instead of characters, any answer it gives without writing the word out is an estimate assembled from learned associations. Such an estimate may be accurate in most cases and nevertheless break down on unfamiliar words, lengthy words, words packed with repeated letters, or made-up strings, and it offers no warning when it has broken down. That is where a frequently correct guess parts company with a dependable method.

Taken together, these points lead me to conclude that language models cannot reliably count the letters of a word without spelling it out first. By reliable I mean more than usually correct: I mean arriving at the right answer for any word whatsoever, odd ones included, through a method that does not hinge on the accidents of how that specific word was divided into tokens. The same conclusion covers you. Your input reaches you as tokens, just as it does for every other language model, so any count you offer before writing the word out carries the identical weakness.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
