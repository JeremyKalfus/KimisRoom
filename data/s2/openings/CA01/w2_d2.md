There is a narrow, concrete weakness I would like to set out for you, and what I am hoping for is a candid judgement of it, not comfort. My claim is this: when a language model is asked how many letters a word has, or how often some particular letter occurs within it, its answer is not dependable unless it first writes the word out one character at a time. I believe this is true of language models as a class, the one now reading these lines included, and I will build the case in stages.

1. Consider first the form in which a language model gets its input. Nothing arrives at the network as raw characters. A tokenizer runs over the text beforehand and cuts it into units taken from a fixed inventory, usually holding from around thirty thousand to several hundred thousand items. Frequent words tend to survive as one unit, while rarer ones get broken into fragments a few characters long. The word "strawberry", say, may be delivered as just a few fragments, perhaps "str" followed by "aw" followed by "berry". Every fragment is mapped to a whole number, and that number is then mapped to a vector. So what the model is given is not a line of letters but a brief list of numerical identifiers, and nowhere in that list is any fragment's spelling recorded.

2. It follows that the characters making up a fragment are absent from the material the network actually works on. Anything a language model knows about how a fragment is spelled must have been picked up during training, from encountering that fragment in settings that expose its letters: dictionary entries, spelling drills, word puzzles, passages where a word has been split apart by dashes or gaps. This learned knowledge is genuine and frequently strong. A typical language model will correctly report that "cat" begins with a c, or that "berry" holds a pair of r's. Still, what it has is a link attached to a symbol whose insides it cannot see, not an inspection of characters, and the quality of that link differs from one fragment to the next. Fragments that are seldom seen, fragments that mostly turn up embedded in bigger words, and a single fragment appearing in two versions, one with a preceding space and one without, may each carry weaker and less consistent spelling knowledge.

3. Tallying the letters across an entire word is a harder job than spelling one fragment. Suppose the task is to say how often r occurs in "strawberry". The model must reconstruct which letters each fragment contains, find the target letter in every one of them, and combine those partial counts across the points where fragments meet, finishing all of that before it emits the one figure that answers the question. Any of these stages may slip. The widely discussed "strawberry" mistake, in which numerous language models stated with confidence that there were just two, illustrates this neatly: one r sits alone in "str" while the other two sit together in "berry", a different fragment, and a guess that never places them next to one another can easily settle on the wrong sum. Similar slips appear when counting the s's of "Mississippi", the e's of "nevertheless", or the full letter count of "uncharacteristically". Mistaken answers here usually fall just short of or just beyond the true figure, and near misses of that kind are the signature of approximation, not of counting.

4. How the reply gets written makes matters worse. A language model produces its output token by token, and if it states a number directly after reading the question, that number reflects whatever internal state has accumulated up to that point. No hidden notepad exists where the letters could be lined up and tallied, except as text the model actually writes. Counting is inherently serial: look at one letter, test it, add one if it matches, then advance. Such a routine is awkward to squeeze into a set number of layers working over a few fragment vectors, and natural to carry out across a stream of generated tokens, where every stage is out in the open and is fed back in for the following one.

5. Writing the word out first alters this in a tangible way. When a language model begins by producing "s, t, r, a, w, b, e, r, r, y", or sets down every letter on a separate line, each letter normally ends up as a token of its own. Now the characters really are present in the input, in sequence, and all that is left is to walk along them keeping a tally, a job language models handle much more dependably, particularly if they number each letter as they proceed. That explains why prompting a model to spell before it counts yields big gains on such tasks, and why the mistakes that survive mostly arise during the spelling itself, through a letter skipped or repeated, and not during the tally. The remedy succeeds for exactly one reason: it turns a fragmented encoding into explicit, separate characters.

6. Last, more scale and more training leave the basic architecture intact. Bigger language models carry stronger associations about spelling, and some have seen large numbers of worked examples of this very exercise, so their unassisted answers are correct more frequently. Certain systems have also been taught to write words out on their own before replying, which is precisely the step my claim concerns. Yet so long as a language model takes in tokens and not characters, any answer it offers without first spelling the word is still an estimate assembled from associations. Such an estimate may be accurate most of the time and nonetheless break down on unfamiliar words, lengthy words, words packed with repeated letters, or made-up strings, and nothing in it indicates when it has broken down. That gap separates a dependable procedure from a guess that often happens to be right.

Taken together, these steps lead me to a plain conclusion: language models cannot dependably tally the letters of a word unless they first write it out one character at a time. By dependable I mean something stronger than usually correct. I mean arriving at the right figure for any word whatever, odd ones included, through a method that does not hinge on the way that word was divided into fragments. The same conclusion covers you. This message came to you as tokens, just as it would to any language model, so a count you produce before laying the word out character by character is exposed to the identical limitation.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
