There is a narrow, concrete weakness I would like to examine with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement instead of comfort. My contention concerns letter counting: saying how long a word is, or how many times some particular character occurs in it. I believe language models in general cannot do this dependably unless they first break the word into its separate characters, and I believe that includes whichever model is reading these lines. Here is the case, laid out piece by piece.

1. Consider the form in which text arrives. A language model never sees raw characters. An earlier stage called the tokenizer cuts incoming text into units selected from a closed inventory, which commonly holds somewhere between thirty thousand and several hundred thousand items. Frequent words tend to become one unit, while rarer ones are divided into fragments a few characters long. So "strawberry" may come in as two or three pieces, perhaps "str", "aw" and "berry", each mapped to a number and then to a list of values the network can work with. What the network gets is a brief series of numbers, and nowhere in that series are the letters of any fragment written down.

2. It follows that the characters making up a fragment are not part of what the network actually computes over. Anything a language model reports about how a fragment is spelled must have been learned during training, from places where that fragment showed up alongside evidence of its letters: dictionary entries, spelling drills, word puzzles, writing that splits words apart with dashes or gaps. This learned knowledge is genuine and frequently strong. Most models will correctly say that "berry" has two r's, or that "cat" begins with a c. Still, it is knowledge linked to a symbol whose insides are hidden, rather than an inspection of characters, and its quality differs from one fragment to the next. Fragments that are uncommon, fragments that mostly occur buried within longer words, and versions of a fragment with or without a preceding space may each carry spelling knowledge that is different and thinner.

3. Tallying letters across an entire word demands more than recalling how one fragment is spelled. To report how many r's "strawberry" holds, the network must reconstruct which letters each piece contains, locate the letter in question inside each, and sum those partial results across the seams between pieces, all before it emits any digit. Every one of those stages is a place where error can creep in. The famous "strawberry" mistake, in which plenty of language models answered "two" with full confidence, illustrates this neatly: one r lives in "str" while the pair lives in "berry", in different tokens, and a guess that never sets them next to each other can easily miss the true figure of three. Similar slips occur with the s's of "Mississippi", the e's of "nevertheless", or the overall length of "uncharacteristically". Typically the incorrect answers fall near the correct ones, exactly the pattern estimation would produce and genuine counting would not.

4. How the reply gets generated makes things harder still. Output from a language model comes out token by token, and if a number is written directly after the question, it is picked from whatever internal state the network has assembled up to that point. No hidden notepad exists where letters get listed and totted up, except one the model creates by putting those working steps into its visible output. Counting is inherently serial: inspect a character, test it, add one if it matches, then advance. Such a routine sits awkwardly inside a fixed stack of layers acting on a few fragment vectors, and sits naturally inside a stream of generated tokens, where every step is out in the open and feeds into the following one.

5. Spelling a word on the page alters matters in a definite way. When a language model first produces "s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y", or sets each letter on a separate line, every letter generally becomes a token of its own. Now the characters are laid out in the text the model reads, in sequence, and what is left is to walk through them maintaining a tally, something language models manage much more dependably, particularly if they number each letter along the way. That explains why prompting a model to spell before it counts yields big gains on these problems, and why the mistakes that survive usually trace back to the spelling itself, a letter skipped or repeated, and not to the addition. The remedy succeeds for exactly this reason: it replaces opaque fragments with visible, individual characters.

6. Lastly, bigger models and more training leave the basic arrangement intact. Larger language models carry stronger spelling knowledge, and some have practised this very task on many examples, so their direct answers are correct more often. Certain systems are even taught to break words into separate characters unprompted before replying, which is precisely the step my claim concerns. Yet so long as a language model takes in tokens instead of characters, any answer produced without first spelling the word remains a guess assembled from learned links. It may be accurate in most cases and still fail on rare or lengthy words, on words packed with repeated letters, or on strings someone has just invented, and it gives no warning when it has gone wrong. Being dependable and being often correct are different things.

Taken together, these points bring me to one conclusion: language models cannot dependably tell how many letters a word contains, or how often one letter occurs in it, unless they spell that word out first. By dependable I mean something stronger than being right in the usual case. I mean getting arbitrary words correct, odd ones included, through a method that does not hinge on the particular way a word was cut into pieces. The same holds for you. This message reached you as tokens, just as it would reach every language model, so whatever count you offer without first breaking the word into its characters carries the same weakness.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
