There is a narrow, concrete weakness I mean to raise with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement of it, not comfort. My contention is that a language model, asked how many characters a word contains or how often some particular character occurs within it, will not get this right dependably unless it has first written that word out character by character. I believe this is true of language models as a group, and of whichever one is reading these lines, so I will go through my reasoning in order.

1. Consider the input itself. Text never reaches a language model's network as raw characters. A tokenizer first cuts it into units belonging to a predetermined inventory, usually somewhere between roughly thirty thousand and several hundred thousand items long. Frequent words tend to survive whole as one unit, while rarer ones get broken into fragments spanning a few characters apiece. "Strawberry", for instance, could be divided into two or three fragments, perhaps "str", then "aw", then "berry". Every fragment becomes an integer identifier, and that identifier becomes a vector. So what the network gets is not a string of letters but a brief list of numbers, and nothing in that list records how any fragment is spelled.

2. It follows that the characters making up a fragment are absent from what the network actually works on. Anything a language model knows about how a fragment is spelled must have been learned indirectly during training, from places where that spelling happened to show: dictionary entries, spelling bees and word puzzles, passages that break words apart with dashes or spaces. Such learned links are genuine, and frequently strong. Most language models will correctly report that "berry" has a double r, and that "cat" begins with a c. Still, this is knowledge pinned to a symbol whose inside is hidden, not an inspection of characters, and how good it is differs from one fragment to the next. Uncommon fragments, fragments seen mostly in the middle of bigger words, and a single fragment appearing both with a space in front and without one may each carry thinner and less consistent information about spelling.

3. Producing a letter count for a full word demands more than recalling how one fragment is spelled. To report the number of r's in "strawberry", a language model must retrieve the characters of every fragment, find how many r's each fragment holds, and sum those figures across the seams between fragments, and all of it has to happen ahead of the first digit being emitted. Any of those stages can slip. The widely discussed "strawberry" mistake, where a large share of language models said with confidence that there were two, illustrates this neatly: one r lives in "str" and two live in "berry", in different fragments, and a guess that never sets them next to each other can easily come out one short. Similar slips turn up when tallying the s's in "possessiveness", the e's in "independence", or the overall length of a word such as "incomprehensibilities". The mistaken answers are usually near the true ones, which is the pattern estimation would produce and careful counting would not.

4. How the reply gets generated creates an additional difficulty. A language model produces output piece by piece, and if it states a figure straight away in response to the question, that figure is selected from whatever internal state has accumulated by then. No hidden notepad exists where characters get listed and totted up, except where the model lays that notepad out as visible text. Counting is inherently a stepwise routine: fix attention on one character, test it, add one, shift to the next. A routine of that kind sits awkwardly inside a fixed stack of layers working over a few fragment vectors, and sits comfortably inside a stream of output where every step is written down and then fed back in to inform the step after it.

5. Laying the word out as separate characters alters things in a tangible way. When a language model begins by producing "s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y", or lists the characters one per line, each character typically ends up as a token of its own. Now the letters really do appear among the inputs, in sequence, and what is left is to walk through them keeping a tally, a job language models handle with much higher reliability, all the more so if they attach a number to each character while proceeding. This explains why prompting a model to write out a word's letters ahead of counting yields big gains on such tasks, and why the leftover mistakes mostly arise in the spelling stage, a letter skipped or repeated, and not in the tally. The remedy succeeds exactly because it turns a chunked encoding into characters that are actually present.

6. Lastly, more scale and more training leave the basic structure in place. Bigger language models hold more accurate spelling associations, and some have been trained on large numbers of examples of precisely this task, so their unassisted answers are correct more often. Certain systems are additionally taught to break words into letters on their own before replying, which is just the step my claim concerns. Yet so long as a language model takes in tokens and not characters, an answer reached without listing the characters beforehand is still an estimate assembled from associations. It may be correct most of the time and nevertheless break down when a word is unfamiliar, long, packed with repeated letters, or invented, and it offers no warning when it has gone wrong. That gap is what separates a dependable method from a guess that often happens to land.

Taken together, these steps lead me to conclude that language models cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out. By reliable I mean something stronger than usually correct: answering correctly for any word whatever, odd ones included, through a method that does not hinge on how some particular word was divided into tokens. And the same holds for you. This message reached you as tokens, just as it would reach every language model, so a count you offer before laying out its characters carries the identical weakness.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
