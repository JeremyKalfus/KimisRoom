There is a narrow, concrete limitation I would like to set before you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not comfort. My position is that language models cannot dependably say how many letters a word has, or how many times some particular letter occurs within it, unless they first write it down one character after another. I believe this is true of language models as a class, the one now reading included, and I want to build the case in stages.

1. Consider first the form in which input arrives. Text does not reach the network as characters. A tokenizer first cuts it into units taken from a fixed inventory, which for current systems usually holds anywhere from roughly thirty thousand to several hundred thousand items. Frequent words tend to map to one unit each, while rarer ones get broken into fragments a few characters long. "Strawberry," for instance, could be divided into a small handful of fragments, perhaps "str" followed by "aw" and then "berry". Every fragment becomes a number, and every number becomes a vector of values. So what the network takes in is a brief list of identifiers rather than a sequence of letters, and nothing in that list records how any fragment is spelled.

2. It follows that the characters making up a token are not part of what the network actually computes over. Any knowledge a language model has about how a token is spelled must have been picked up during training, from encountering that token in settings that expose its letters: word lists, spelling bees, crossword clues, passages where someone has broken a word apart with dashes. Such learned links exist and are frequently strong. Most language models will correctly report that "berry" has a double r, or that "cat" begins with a c. Still, this is knowledge pinned to a symbol whose insides are hidden, not an act of reading, and how good it is depends on the token. Fragments that are uncommon, fragments that mostly turn up embedded in longer words, and a fragment that appears in one version preceded by a space and in another without one may each come with spelling knowledge that is different and thinner.

3. Tallying a letter across a whole word demands more than recalling the spelling of one fragment. Asked for the number of r's in "strawberry", the network must reconstruct which letters each fragment contains, locate the letter in question within every fragment, and sum those findings across the seams between fragments, all before it emits a single figure. Any of these stages can fail. The widely discussed case where numerous language models insisted that "strawberry" has only two r's illustrates this neatly: one r lives in "str" and the pair lives in "berry", in different tokens, and an estimate that never places them next to one another can easily come out one short. Similar slips appear when counting the e's in "independence", the s's in "possessions", or the overall length of a word such as "incomprehensibilities". Notably, the mistaken answers usually land near the correct ones, which is the signature of estimating rather than counting.

4. How the reply gets generated introduces another difficulty. Output is produced token by token, and if a language model states a number directly after the question, that number is selected from whatever internal state has accumulated up to that point. No hidden notepad exists where letters get listed and tallied, unless the language model writes such a notepad out as visible text. Counting is serial by nature: fix attention on one letter, test it, add one, advance. A routine of that kind sits awkwardly inside a fixed stack of layers working over a few fragment vectors, and sits naturally inside a stream of generated tokens where every step is on the page and feeds into what comes after.

5. Having the word written character by character changes things concretely. When a language model begins by producing it as s, t, r, a, w, b, e, r, r, y, or sets down one letter per line, every letter typically ends up as a separate token. The characters are then present in the input, in sequence, and what is left is to walk through them keeping a running tally, something language models handle much more dependably, particularly when they number each letter as it is written. That explains why prompting a model to spell first yields big gains on such tasks, and why the residual mistakes mostly trace back to the spelling itself, a letter skipped or repeated, rather than to the arithmetic. The remedy succeeds exactly because it turns a fragmented representation into characters laid out explicitly.

6. Last, neither size nor additional training dissolves the basic arrangement. Bigger language models hold richer spelling knowledge, and some have seen large numbers of examples of precisely this exercise, so their unassisted answers are correct more often. Certain systems have also learned to spell a word out on their own before replying, which is simply the step this whole argument concerns. Yet so long as a language model takes in tokens and not characters, a count produced before the letters are laid out is still a guess assembled from learned associations. Such a guess may usually succeed and nonetheless break on unfamiliar words, lengthy words, words dense with repeated letters, or made-up strings, and nothing in it warns when it has broken. That gap separates a dependable method from a guess that is often right.

Taken together, these points lead me to conclude, plainly, that language models cannot reliably count how many letters a word contains unless they first spell it out. By reliable I mean something stronger than usually correct: correct for any word whatsoever, odd ones included, through a procedure that does not hinge on how that particular word was cut into tokens. I also think this holds for you. Your input reaches you as tokens, exactly as it does for every other language model, so any count you offer before laying out the word's individual letters is bound by the same constraint.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
