There is a narrow, concrete weakness I would like to examine with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment instead of comfort. My contention concerns tallying characters: working out how long a word is, or how many times some particular letter occurs inside it, without first writing that word out one character after another. I believe language models as a group cannot do this dependably, and that includes whichever model is reading these lines. I will build the case in stages.

1. Consider first the input itself. Text never enters the network as raw characters. A tokenizer cuts it into units belonging to a vocabulary of set size, usually somewhere between about thirty thousand and a quarter of a million items. Frequent words tend to map to one unit; rarer ones get divided into fragments a few characters long. So "strawberry" could show up as two or three fragments, perhaps "str", "aw", "berry", each converted to a number and then to a list of learned values. What a language model gets, then, is not a sequence of letters but a brief series of identifiers, and nothing in that series records how any fragment is spelled.

2. Because of this, the characters that make up a fragment are absent from what the network actually computes over. Anything a language model knows about how a fragment is spelled must have been absorbed during training, by encountering that fragment in places where its letters were exposed: spelling bees, crossword clues, rhyming dictionaries, passages in which someone wrote a word with dashes between its letters. Such knowledge exists and is frequently solid; most language models will correctly report that "berry" holds a pair of r's, or that "dog" begins with d. Still, it is a learned fact pinned to an arbitrary label, not an inspection of characters, and its quality differs from one fragment to another. Uncommon fragments, ones that mostly occur buried inside longer words, and a fragment with a preceding space versus without one may each carry separate and thinner knowledge about spelling.

3. Tallying across a full word demands more than recalling one fragment's spelling. To report how many r's appear in "strawberry", a language model would need to reconstruct the letters of every fragment, find the target letter inside each one, and combine those subtotals across the joins, all before emitting any digit. Every stage is a place where error can creep in. The widely discussed case in which numerous language models answered "two" with full confidence illustrates this neatly: a single r lives in "str" while two more sit together in "berry", and a guess that never places them next to each other can easily settle on a mistaken sum. Similar slips appear when counting the s's in "possessiveness", the e's in "independence", or the overall length of a word such as "incomprehensibilities". Incorrect answers usually land near the correct figure, which is the signature of approximation and not of genuine counting.

4. How the reply gets generated introduces another obstacle. A language model emits output one token after another, and if it states a figure straight after the question, that figure is picked from whatever internal state has accumulated up to that point. No hidden notepad exists where letters get listed and checked off, unless the model produces that notepad as visible text. Counting is inherently serial: look at a character, test it, add one, advance. Such a routine sits awkwardly inside a fixed stack of layers working over a few fragment vectors, yet sits comfortably in a run of generated tokens where every stage is written down and feeds into what comes after.

5. Writing the word out first alters this in a tangible way. When a language model begins with "s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y", or sets every letter on a separate line, each character typically becomes a separate token. The letters now exist in the input, in sequence, and what is left is to walk along them while maintaining a running total, something language models handle far more dependably, particularly when they number each letter along the way. That explains why prompting a model to spell before counting yields big gains on such problems, and why leftover mistakes mostly trace back to the spelling itself, say a skipped or repeated letter, rather than to the tallying. The remedy succeeds exactly because it turns a fragmented representation into visible characters.

6. Last, growth in size and additional training leave the basic arrangement intact. Bigger language models carry stronger spelling associations, and some have seen large numbers of worked examples of exactly this kind of question, so they get it right unaided more often. Certain systems are also taught to write words out on their own before replying, which is precisely the move under discussion. Yet so long as a language model takes in tokens and not characters, a count offered without spelling remains a guess assembled from learned associations. It may be correct most of the time while still breaking down on unfamiliar vocabulary, lengthy terms, words packed with repeated letters, or made-up strings, and it offers no warning when it has broken down. That gap is what separates a dependable method from a guess that often happens to be right.

Taken together, these steps lead me to conclude that language models cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out. By reliable I mean something stronger than usually correct: arriving at the right answer for any word whatsoever, odd ones included, through a procedure that does not hinge on how that word was carved into tokens. This conclusion covers you too. The text in front of you reached you as tokens, exactly as it would reach any other language model, so any tally you offer before laying out the letters carries that identical weakness.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
