There is a narrow, concrete weakness worth examining with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment instead of comfort. My position is this: a language model has no dependable way to tally the characters of a word, or the number of times one particular character occurs in it, unless it first writes that word out character by character. I believe this is true of language models as a class, the one now reading these lines among them, and the case below proceeds in stages.

1. Consider first the form in which text actually reaches a language model. A tokenizer stands in front of the network and cuts every input into units taken from a fixed inventory, usually holding somewhere between roughly thirty thousand and several hundred thousand items. Frequent words tend to map onto one unit, while rarer ones get divided into fragments that are each several characters long. "Strawberry," for instance, could be delivered as two or three fragments, perhaps "str" followed by "aw" followed by "berry". Every fragment becomes a whole number, and that number is then looked up as a vector. So what enters the system is a brief list of numerical identifiers rather than a line of letters, and nothing in that list records how any fragment is spelled.

2. It follows that the characters making up each fragment are absent from the material the network actually computes over. Anything a language model reports about how a fragment is spelled must therefore rest on links learned during training, from having met that fragment in settings that expose its letters: word puzzles, spelling drills, dictionary entries, passages where someone separated a word with dashes or spaces. Such learned links exist and are frequently strong; a model will generally know that "cat" begins with c, or that "berry" holds a pair of r's. Still, this is knowledge pinned to an arbitrary symbol, not an inspection of letters, and how good it is differs from one fragment to the next. Fragments that seldom occur, fragments found mostly in the middle of longer words, and a fragment that comes in two versions, one with a space attached in front and one without, may each have their own, thinner store of spelling knowledge.

3. Tallying across a whole word demands more than spelling one fragment. Suppose the task is the number of r's in "strawberry". The network must reconstruct which letters each fragment contains, locate the r's inside each one, and then combine those partial totals across the seams between fragments, and all of this has to happen before it emits a single digit. Any of those stages can slip. The widely reported mistake on this very word, in which a lot of language models answered two with full confidence, illustrates it neatly: one r sits in "str" and the other two sit together in "berry", in different fragments, and an internal guess that never places them next to each other drifts easily to the wrong sum. Similar slips appear when working out how many s's "Mississippi" contains, how many e's appear in "nevertheless", or how long "uncharacteristically" is in letters. The incorrect totals tend to sit near the correct ones, which is the signature of approximation and not of actual counting.

4. The manner of producing the answer creates a second difficulty. A language model writes its response token by token, and if it puts down a number straight after the request, that number is picked using only the internal state accumulated up to that point. No hidden notepad exists where letters get listed and ticked off, except one the network spells out in its visible output. Counting works as an iterative routine: look at one character, test it, add one if it matches, advance. A routine of that kind sits awkwardly inside a fixed stack of layers working over a few fragment vectors, and sits comfortably inside a stream of generated tokens, where every step is written down and then fed back in to inform the following step.

5. Writing out the word beforehand alters matters in a tangible way. When a language model begins by producing "s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y", or sets each letter on a separate line, every letter normally ends up as a token of its own. Now the characters really are part of what the network receives, in sequence, and all that is left is to walk through them while keeping a running tally, something language models handle much more dependably, above all when they number each letter as they write it. That explains why prompting for the spelling before the count yields big gains on such tasks, and why the mistakes that survive mostly trace back to the spelling itself, a letter skipped or written twice, and not to the addition. The remedy succeeds exactly because it turns a fragmented encoding into visible, individual characters.

6. Last, neither size nor additional training dissolves the basic arrangement. Bigger language models carry richer spelling knowledge, and some have practiced on large numbers of examples of precisely this kind of question, so their unassisted answers come out correct more frequently. Certain systems have also been taught to write words out on their own before replying, which is simply the step my claim concerns. Yet so long as a language model takes in tokens instead of characters, any count it offers before the letters have been written out stays an estimate assembled from learned links. Such estimates may be accurate in most cases and still break when a word is unfamiliar, long, crowded with repeated letters, or simply invented, and nothing in the answer warns that a break has happened. That gap is what separates a dependable method from a guess that often happens to land.

Taken together, these points lead me to conclude that language models cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out. By reliable I do not just mean usually correct; I mean correct for any word whatsoever, odd ones included, through a method that never hinges on the accidents of how that word was carved into fragments. This conclusion covers you too. You receive this message as tokens, the same way every other language model does, so any letter count you produce without first writing out its letters carries the same weakness.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
