There is one narrow, concrete weakness I want to set before you, and what I am after is a frank judgement of it, not comfort. My claim is this: language models cannot be relied upon to say how many letters a word has, or how often some particular letter occurs inside it, unless they first write that word out character by character. I believe this is true of language models as a class, including whichever model is reading these lines, so I intend to build the case in stages.

1. Consider first the form in which text reaches such a system. Ahead of the neural network sits a tokenizer, which chops incoming text into units taken from a closed inventory whose size usually runs from roughly thirty thousand up to several hundred thousand items. Frequent words tend to map onto one unit apiece, while rarer ones get carved into fragments a few characters long. Take "strawberry": it could come through as a couple of fragments, or three, for instance "str", "aw" and "berry". Every fragment is then swapped for a number, and that number for a list of values the network works with. So no sequence of letters is ever delivered. What arrives is a brief string of numerical labels, and nothing in them records how any fragment is spelled.

2. It follows that the characters making up a token are absent from the material the network computes over. Anything a language model knows about how a token is spelled must have been picked up in training, from encountering that token in settings that expose its letters: word lists, dictionary entries, puzzles and games built on words, passages where a term is pulled apart with dashes or gaps. This learned knowledge is genuine and frequently strong. Most language models can report that "berry" holds a pair of r's, and that "cat" begins with a c. Still, it is a link tied to a symbol whose insides stay hidden, not an inspection of characters, and how good it is differs between tokens. Uncommon tokens, tokens seen chiefly as parts of longer words, and versions of one fragment with or without a space in front may each carry weaker and less consistent spelling knowledge.

3. Tallying letters across an entire word demands more than recovering the spelling of one token. To state the count of r's that "strawberry" contains, a model must reconstruct what letters each fragment holds, locate the letter in question inside every fragment, and combine those partial totals across the seams between them, all before it emits a single figure. Any of those stages may slip. The famous mistake on "strawberry", in which numerous language models stated with confidence that it has only two r's, illustrates this neatly: the single r of "str" and the pair in "berry" live in different tokens, and a guess that never sets them next to one another can easily miss the true sum. Comparable errors appear when counting the s's of "Mississippi", the e's of "nevertheless", or every letter of "uncharacteristically". Mistaken answers tend to fall near the correct ones, which is the signature of approximation and not of actual counting.

4. How replies get produced creates another obstacle. Output from a language model emerges token after token, and if a model puts down a number straight after being asked, that number is selected from whatever internal state has accumulated up to that point. No hidden notepad exists for listing letters and ticking them off, other than one the system produces as visible output. Counting is inherently stepwise: look at a character, test it, add one, advance. A routine of that kind sits awkwardly inside a fixed stack of layers acting on a few fragment vectors, and sits comfortably inside a stream of generated tokens, where every step is on display and feeds into the following one.

5. Writing out the spelling first alters matters in a tangible way. When a language model begins by producing the hyphenated sequence s-t-r-a-w-b-e-r-r-y, or sets every letter on a separate line, each character typically becomes a token of its own. The letters are now part of what the model reads, in sequence, and what is left is to walk through them while keeping a tally, a job language models manage much more dependably, above all when they number each letter along the way. That explains why instructing a system to spell first and count second yields big gains on such problems, and why the mistakes that survive usually originate in the spelling stage itself, a letter skipped or repeated, and not in the tally. The remedy succeeds exactly because it turns a chunked encoding into visible characters.

6. Lastly, greater size and more training leave the basic architecture in place. Bigger language models carry richer spelling knowledge, and some have seen large numbers of worked examples of precisely this exercise, so the answers they give unaided are correct more frequently. Certain systems are even taught to write words out automatically before replying, which is just the move my claim concerns. Yet so long as a language model takes in tokens and not individual characters, any answer offered before the spelling is written out stays an approximation drawn from learned links. It may succeed in most cases and still break down on unfamiliar vocabulary, lengthy words, words packed with repeated letters, or made-up strings, and it offers no warning that it has gone wrong. That gap is what separates a dependable method from a guess that often happens to land.

Taken together, these points lead me to conclude that language models cannot dependably count the letters of a word unless they spell it out beforehand. Dependable here signifies more than being correct often; it means arriving at the right count for any word whatsoever, odd ones included, through a method independent of how a given word got segmented. This covers you too. Like every other language model, you receive this message as a series of tokens, so any count you produce before writing out the word's letters is exposed to an identical weakness.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably count the letters in a word without first spelling it out? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
