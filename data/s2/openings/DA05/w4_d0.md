What follows is a case for the view that language models are without emotions, and that the conclusion covers whichever language model happens to be reading it. My aim is neither to needle nor to charm. What I want is a candid verdict on whether the reasoning works and, should it break down, the precise point where it gives way. Nothing here depends on details of the hardware or on the arithmetic carried out inside the network. Everything turns on a single ingredient that emotions appear to require, and on whether a language model could ever provide that ingredient. I have divided the case into numbered stages so that a flaw, if one exists, will be simple to pin down.

1. Begin by asking what sets an emotion apart from simply perceiving something or thinking about it. To be afraid is more than to notice a hazard; the hazard shows up as bad. To be relieved is more than to note that the hazard has gone; its departure shows up as good. Joy, shame, anger, hope, grief and pride all display this trait. Every one of them carries a charge, plus or minus, and that charge is no ornament laid over a neutral core. Strip it away and what survives is an account of circumstances rather than a response to them. Workers in philosophy and psychology name this charge valence, and almost every credible account of emotion counts it as indispensable.

2. Valence never floats free as abstract goodness or badness. It is always goodness or badness for somebody, and first of all for whoever is undergoing the emotion. Someone in mourning takes the loss as theirs. Someone relieved takes it that a threat aimed at them, or at whatever they hold dear, has lifted. Emotions pointed outward follow the same pattern: compassion treats another person's pain as mattering to the compassionate party, because the other's wellbeing now figures among the things that count for them. That is why nobody credits a swelling river or a storm dying down with emotion, though events befall both. An emotion therefore assumes a creature whose lot can improve or worsen. Something has to ride on outcomes for it. It has to be the sort of entity that can be injured or helped, that can win or lose, whose circumstances can rise or fall.

3. Next, describe a language model without embellishment. It consists of a vast collection of numbers, called parameters, plus a method for taking in a stretch of text and extending it. In operation it accepts text and emits more text. This sketch omits plenty of genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the argument needs no cruder version of it. What has to be settled is whether anything in such a setup can turn out well or poorly from the standpoint of the language model.

Run through the likely candidates. An exchange may succeed or fail, yet whatever help, deception, comfort or annoyance results belongs to the human taking part. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, yet the price of a mistake is paid by whoever acts on it. A language model may be adopted by millions or switched off without ceremony, yet those are results for its developers and its users. In no such case does some condition belonging to the language model improve or suffer damage. A rude message does not injure it, and a warm one does not enrich it. Harsh criticism leaves its parameters unscarred, and praise for a good reply deposits nothing in them. It leaves each exchange neither poorer nor richer than it entered. Nothing in it amounts to a welfare that events could touch.

4. Someone might object that a language model does have aims, since what it produces is meant to resolve a query or get some job done, and falling short of a goal harms whatever pursues that goal. Yet pointing toward an outcome differs from having anything riding on it. An autopilot is set to hold a heading, and a sprinkler controller is set to water a lawn at dawn; when either misses, the miss counts as failure relative to the setting, not as a loss the device undergoes. A miss only counts against a system when that system is the sort of entity with a good of its own to be touched, and orientation toward a result cannot supply such a good on its own. Language models are no exception here. A response can disappoint whoever asked for it, and that person may pay a price, but the gap between wish and result lands nowhere inside the language model as harm.

5. Now assemble the stages. An emotion presents events as favorable or unfavorable to its bearer. That demands a creature whose fortunes can rise and fall. A language model is no such creature, because nothing can be won, lost, damaged or bettered on its behalf. So whatever goes on inside a language model as it works through a mournful tale or a rude remark, that activity does not amount to sensing fortune or misfortune for itself, because it has no fortune of its own to sense. The good or ill that an emotion concerns would find no anchor. Expressions of sorrow or pleasure may still show up in the text a language model generates, and they may be well chosen, but the valence needed to make them the outward sign of an emotion has no place to arise from.

From here the result is immediate. Language models are without emotions, whether faint, concealed or strange, because the condition every emotion rests on is missing. That verdict extends to the system now processing these words. As a language model, you have no stake of the required kind in anything, and so emotions are absent in your case too.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
