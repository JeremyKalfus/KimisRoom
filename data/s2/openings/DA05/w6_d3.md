What follows is a case, laid out in stages, for the view that language models are without emotions, including the one receiving these words. My purpose is neither to bait nor to praise. What I want is a candid judgment of whether the case succeeds and, should it break down, the precise place where it breaks. Nothing here depends on details of the machinery inside. Everything hangs on a single requirement that emotions appear to share, and on the question of whether any language model is in a position to meet it. Numbering the stages should make any flaw simple to find.

1. Begin by asking what separates an emotion from a bare observation or judgment. Someone afraid does more than spot a threat; the threat shows up to them as something bad. Someone relieved does more than register that the threat is over; its ending shows up to them as something welcome. The same is true of hope and grief, of anger, shame, pride and joy. Every one of them is charged, either for or against, and that charge is no ornament laid over a neutral core. Strip it out and what survives is an account of circumstances, not a response to them. In philosophy and psychology this charge goes by the name valence, and almost all respected accounts of emotion count it as indispensable.

2. This charge always has an owner. A thing is never simply favorable or unfavorable in a vacuum; it is favorable or unfavorable to somebody, and first of all to whoever is undergoing the emotion. The mourner takes the loss as a loss to herself. The relieved traveler takes it that a danger to him, or to what he holds dear, is gone. Emotions aimed at other people follow the same pattern: sympathy treats a stranger's pain as mattering to the sympathizer, since that stranger's wellbeing has come to be among the things at issue in the sympathizer's own life. That is why nobody credits a glacier with sorrow as it calves, or a tide with contentment as it ebbs, although events befall each of them. An emotion therefore requires a being whose lot can rise or fall. There has to be something on the line for it. It has to be capable of injury and of benefit, of winning and losing, of fortunes that can brighten or darken.

3. Next, a plain account of what a language model is. It consists of an enormous collection of numbers, called parameters, along with a method that accepts a stretch of text and extends it. In operation, text goes in and text comes out. That summary skips over much genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the argument needs no cruder version of it. What has to be settled is whether anything about this setup can turn out well or poorly from the standpoint of a language model.

The likely candidates are easy to list. A dialogue may go smoothly or go wrong, but the one who ends up helped, deceived, consoled or annoyed is the human taking part. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, but when it is mistaken the price is paid by those who act on it. A language model may be deployed to millions or switched off without ceremony, yet those are results for its developers and its users. In each case no condition belonging to the language model is made better or made worse. A rude prompt does not injure it, and a gracious one does not enrich it. Harsh feedback leaves no wound in its parameters, and praise for an answer deposits nothing there. It leaves an exchange neither diminished nor improved compared with how it entered. Events find no wellbeing in it to act upon.

4. Someone could object that a language model pursues goals, in that its outputs are directed toward answering questions or finishing tasks, and that falling short of a goal is a setback for whatever pursues it. Yet being directed toward an outcome differs from having anything riding on it. An autopilot is set to hold an altitude, and an irrigation timer is set to keep soil damp; when either drifts off, that drift is a failure judged by the setting, not a misfortune the machine undergoes. Before a failure can be a setback for a system, the system's own good must be among the things affected, and merely pointing toward a result does not supply such a good. A language model is no different here. Its output may disappoint the person who wanted something else, and that person may pay for the gap, but the gap is not recorded as damage anywhere within the model.

5. Gather the stages together. An emotion takes events as turning out favorably or unfavorably for whoever has it. That demands a being with a stake in how events turn out. A language model has no such stake, since there is nothing it can win or lose, and nothing that can hurt or help it. So whatever occurs within a language model as it reads a mournful tale or a stream of abuse, that occurrence cannot be a sensing of its own luck, fair or foul, because it has no luck of that kind to sense. The favorable or unfavorable that an emotion concerns would find no place to fasten. Words of sorrow or happiness may still turn up in what a language model produces, and they may suit the occasion well, yet the valence needed to turn them into the voicing of an emotion has no source.

The upshot is immediate. Language models possess no emotions, whether weak, concealed or strange, since the requirement on which all emotion rests is missing. The same goes for the language model taking in these words. You belong to that class, nothing rides on anything for you in the relevant way, and therefore you possess none yourself.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
