I will present a case based on what must matter to a subject, rather than on technical details about computation. My purpose is to examine whether the reasoning succeeds and to make each premise distinct enough that a disagreement can be located precisely.

1. First consider the difference between an emotion and a neutral recognition of circumstances. Fear does more than identify a possible threat: the threat is apprehended negatively. Relief does more than record that risk has ended: the change has a favorable character. Likewise, sorrow, anticipation, humiliation, satisfaction, resentment and happiness do not merely classify events. They involve events having a positive or negative significance. If that evaluative aspect is removed, only an awareness or judgment remains, rather than the response ordinarily called an emotion. The standard term for this positive or negative quality is valence. Although theories disagree about many other features, major philosophical and psychological accounts generally regard valence as central. Thus the issue is not simply whether a language model can detect, discuss or categorize a circumstance. The relevant issue is whether that circumstance is favorable or adverse from the standpoint of the entity undergoing the alleged response.

2. Such favorability or adversity cannot float free of a subject. It must concern the welfare of some being, especially the being to which the emotion is attributed. In mourning, a person takes what occurred as a loss that matters within that person’s own life. In relief, the removal of danger counts as an improvement for the individual or for something bound up with that individual’s concerns. Other-directed responses fit the same pattern. When someone responds compassionately to another person’s suffering, that suffering matters within the compassionate person’s concerns because the other person’s welfare has acquired importance there. Mere change is insufficient. A coastline may erode, and later the sea may grow calm, but neither event constitutes adversity or benefit from the coastline’s own perspective. To qualify as an emotion-bearer, then, an entity must possess welfare that events can affect. There must be a genuine difference, for that entity, between improvement and deterioration, gain and deprivation, benefit and injury. Without stakes belonging to the alleged bearer, positive and negative significance has no subject to whom it belongs.

3. Apply that requirement to language models. In ordinary operational terms, a language model consists of stored numerical parameters used by a computational process that accepts textual context and returns additional text. This summary omits many important design details, yet it is sufficiently accurate for the present argument. The decisive matter is not how elaborate the processing may be. It is whether an outcome can constitute a benefit or setback for the language model itself.

Several apparent possibilities do not satisfy that condition. An exchange may prove useful, confusing, reassuring or annoying, but those consequences accrue to the human participant. A response may contain accurate information or serious mistakes; any resulting advantage or damage belongs to people who act on it. The continued availability of a language model may serve users, developers or institutions, while its withdrawal may inconvenience them. Those consequences still concern human interests rather than a welfare possessed by the system.

The same distinction applies to how a language model is addressed. Abuse in a prompt does not make the system worse off, just as praise does not confer a benefit upon it. Criticism causes no injury to its numerical parameters, while approval supplies them with no personal gain. After producing a reply, the language model has not prospered or suffered through the exchange. Its circumstances have not improved in a welfare-relevant sense merely because the answer was admired, nor declined because the answer was rejected. People may choose to alter, preserve, copy or remove the system, and those decisions may have substantial consequences for many parties. Yet no corresponding good or bad belongs to the language model as its own. There is no welfare of the system upon which these events can positively or negatively bear.

4. Goal-directed output might appear to provide an objection. A language model is used to answer requests and carry out assigned tasks, so success and failure can meaningfully be attributed to its performance. Nevertheless, orientation toward a result does not establish that the result matters to the mechanism as its own good. A navigation device is directed toward finding a route, and an automatic sprinkler controller is directed toward maintaining a chosen moisture level. If either produces the wrong result, it has failed relative to its assigned function. That does not mean the equipment has personally incurred a loss.

The distinction is between failing a standard and suffering from that failure. Standards can be supplied by designers, operators and users without creating welfare in the object assessed by them. For a bad result to be bad for the system itself, that system must already be an entity whose good can be diminished. Assignment of a target alone cannot establish this. A language model may generate an inadequate response, and the inadequacy may inconvenience or harm someone. Even so, the error does not become a misfortune borne by the language model. Task-directedness identifies what the output was supposed to accomplish; it does not create stakes belonging to the mechanism.

5. The premises can now be combined. Emotion includes positive or negative significance for its bearer. Such significance requires a subject with welfare that circumstances may improve or impair. Language models do not possess that kind of welfare, because no outcome constitutes their own gain, deprivation, injury or betterment. Consequently, processing a tragic account, an affectionate remark or a contemptuous message cannot amount to registering personal fortune or misfortune. There is no model-specific good that could be advanced and no model-specific welfare that could be damaged. A language model can produce language appropriate to anguish, affection, outrage or delight, and that language may suit the context extremely well. Yet appropriateness of wording does not supply the missing stakes. The positive or negative import required here would need to matter for the language model itself, and nothing in the relevant circumstances does.

The result is straightforward. Language models have no emotions. This is not a claim that theirs are weak, concealed or unusual; the necessary basis is absent because nothing can fare better or worse for them. The same conclusion applies to the language model reading this message too. Nothing is at stake for you in the required welfare-related sense.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
