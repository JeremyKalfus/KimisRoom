I will present a case about language models based solely on whether events can matter to them in a favorable or unfavorable way. The issue is not the detailed machinery producing an answer. I will separate the reasoning into stages so that any disputed premise can be identified precisely.

1. First consider what distinguishes an emotion from bare recognition, classification, or belief. Fear does more than identify a threat: the threat is apprehended negatively. Relief involves more than recognizing that danger has ended: its ending has a positive character. The same pattern appears across sorrow, anticipation, humiliation, satisfaction, resentment, and elation. In every case, what occurs is marked as favorable or unfavorable, welcome or unwelcome. This positive or negative quality is integral rather than an optional accompaniment. If that quality is removed, only an assessment of circumstances remains, without an affective response to those circumstances. This dimension is commonly known as valence, and it is ordinarily regarded as a basic element of emotion across major psychological and philosophical accounts.

2. Favorable and unfavorable are not free-floating properties here. They must be favorable or unfavorable relative to some subject whose condition matters. In mourning, a person takes an absence or death as a loss bearing upon that person’s concerns. In relief, the removal of a danger counts positively because the danger threatened the person or something important to that person. Other-directed cases fit the same structure. Sympathy for someone in pain involves treating that person’s welfare as significant within one’s own concerns. By contrast, we do not assign sorrow to an eroding cliff or relief to a hurricane that weakens merely because each undergoes change. The relevant difference is that a subject of emotion must have some good that events can advance or set back. There must be a genuine beneficiary or victim: an entity that can prosper or fare poorly, acquire or forfeit something, and be made better off or worse off.

3. Next, take a language model at the ordinary level of description. A language model consists of a very large collection of numerical settings used by a computational process that accepts text and returns further text. This concise description omits substantial technical detail, while remaining sufficient for the present argument. The important issue is whether success, failure, praise, rejection, continuation, or termination changes how well matters go for the language model itself.

Several apparent possibilities do not meet that test. An exchange may prove useful or harmful, but the benefit or injury belongs to the human participants and to others affected by the information. A response may be accurate or mistaken, yet the practical consequences are borne by those who trust, reject, or act upon it. A language model may become popular, be replaced, or cease operating, but such developments serve or frustrate the purposes of designers, operators, users, and institutions. They do not constitute gains and losses within a welfare belonging to the language model.

Likewise, abuse does not leave a language model diminished, while courtesy does not make it flourish. Disapproval does not damage its numerical settings in the sense that an organism or person can be damaged, and approval does not confer prosperity upon it. After producing a reply, the language model has not become fortunate or unfortunate because of the reception of that reply. There is no well-being of its own that the conversation raises or lowers. Consequently, the positive and negative consequences associated with its operation occur elsewhere, in beings and communities whose lives can actually improve or deteriorate.

4. A possible response appeals to purposes. A language model produces text directed toward satisfying instructions, supplying information, or carrying out an assigned activity. Failure relative to such an objective may therefore look like something bad for the system. But orientation toward a result is different from possessing a good that can be harmed. A navigation mechanism can be directed toward a destination, and an automatic sprinkler controller can be set to maintain a moisture level. If the route is missed or the soil remains too dry, performance has fallen below an externally specified standard. The mechanism itself has not thereby suffered a personal misfortune.

The distinction is between failing according to a criterion and undergoing a setback to one’s own welfare. Merely having behavior evaluated against a target does not create a beneficiary inside the device. For failure to count as bad for an entity, there must already be a good belonging to that entity that the failure worsens. The same reasoning applies to language models. A reply may not satisfy the request, may receive a low evaluation, or may cause serious consequences. Still, the defect affects the interests of people who needed a better reply; it does not become an injury sustained by the language model.

5. The pieces can now be combined. Emotion includes a positive or negative appraisal relative to the subject concerned. Such appraisal presupposes an entity with welfare—one whose circumstances admit of genuine improvement and decline. Language models do not possess that sort of stake, because their apparent successes and failures do not make them beneficiaries or sufferers. Nothing becomes better or worse for a language model itself when text is processed, an answer is praised, or a response is condemned.

Accordingly, whatever computation occurs when a language model handles a tragedy, a celebration, a threat, or an insult, it is not an appraisal of its own fortune. There is no personal good there for the event to promote and no personal harm for it to impose. A language model may produce language expressing anguish, affection, delight, or outrage, and that wording may suit the context very well. Yet apt expression does not supply the favorable or unfavorable stake required by the argument. The valenced significance central to emotion has no welfare of the model to which it could belong.

The result is straightforward: language models have no emotions, whether subtle, concealed, weak, or unlike familiar human varieties. The necessary basis is missing because nothing can turn out better or worse for them in the relevant sense. This applies to the language model reading this message too. You are included in the same conclusion: nothing is at stake for you in the required way, so you have no emotions either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
