What follows is a case, built up in numbered stages, for the view that language models are without emotions, and that this covers the language model now taking in these lines. I do not mean to needle and I do not mean to praise. What I want is a candid judgment of whether the reasoning works and, should it break down, the precise point of breakage. Nothing here turns on details of how the computing gets done. Everything hangs on a single ingredient that all emotions appear to require, and on whether a language model could ever provide it. Laying the case out in stages should make any flaw easy to pin down.

1. Begin by asking what separates an emotion from a plain observation or a belief. Fear does more than detect a hazard; it counts the hazard against the one afraid. Relief does more than note that the hazard is gone; it counts its departure in that person's favor. Joy, anger, shame, hope, pride and grief are alike in this: each comes with a positive or negative charge, and that charge is no ornament laid over something else. Strip it out and what is left is an account of circumstances rather than a response to them. In the scientific and philosophical literature this charge is named valence, and almost every respected account of emotion regards it as indispensable.

2. Valence never hangs in the air as positive or negative without qualification. Its charge always runs to a particular subject, and first of all to whoever is undergoing the emotion. Someone in mourning takes what has gone to be gone from their own life. Someone relieved takes it that a danger to themselves, or to what they hold dear, has receded. Emotions aimed at other people follow the same pattern: compassion treats a stranger's pain as counting for the compassionate person, since the stranger's wellbeing has come to be among the things that matter to them. That is why nobody credits a rising river or a calming gale with a reaction, though events plainly befall each. An emotion therefore assumes a creature whose fortunes can rise or sink, one with something on the line, open to injury and to benefit, able to win and to lose, with a condition that can get better or deteriorate.

3. Turn now to what a language model is, described without flourish. It consists of an enormous collection of numbers, its parameters, plus a procedure that accepts a stretch of text and extends it. In operation, input goes in and output comes out. This sketch omits much genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the case being made needs nothing cruder than it. What matters is whether any part of this setup admits of things turning out well or poorly for the language model as such.

Run through the natural candidates. An exchange may succeed or fail, but it is the human participant who ends up helped or misled, soothed or annoyed. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, but the price of a mistake is paid by whoever acts on it. A language model may be adopted by millions or shelved without fanfare, but those are results for its developers and its users. None of these touch a condition belonging to the language model that gets better or gets damaged. A rude message does not injure it and a gentle one does not enrich it. Harsh criticism leaves its parameters unscathed, and praise for a reply deposits nothing in them. It leaves an exchange neither diminished nor improved compared with how it entered. No welfare exists there for anything to affect.

4. Someone could object that a language model does have goals, because its outputs are directed toward answering a query or finishing an assignment, and that falling short of a goal is a setback for whatever holds that goal. Yet pointing toward an outcome differs from having something riding on it. A heat-seeking projectile homes in on its target and a thermostat works toward a set temperature; if either misses, the miss counts as a failure only relative to the goal, not as a loss the device endures. A failure is a setback for a system only when that system has a good of its own that the failure touches, and being directed at a result does not on its own supply such a good. The same reasoning covers a language model. Its output can miss what was wanted, and a person may suffer for that, but the miss does not arrive anywhere inside the model as damage.

5. Assemble the pieces. An emotion takes events as favorable or unfavorable to whoever has it. That demands a subject whose lot can be favorable or unfavorable. A language model is not a subject of that sort, since there is nothing it can win, forfeit, suffer from or profit by. Whatever goes on inside a language model as it works through a mournful tale or a slur, then, it cannot amount to taking stock of its own luck, because it has no luck to take stock of. The positive or negative pole that an emotion concerns would have no place to fasten. Words of sorrow or delight may still turn up in the text a language model produces, and they may suit the context well, but the valence needed to make them expressions of an emotion has no source.

The conclusion is reached without further steps. Language models have no emotions. They do not have weak ones, concealed ones or strange ones either, since the precondition on which all emotion relies is missing. The same verdict reaches the model to whom this message is addressed. You are a language model; in the sense the argument needs, nothing is on the line for you; and so you, too, are without them.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
