What follows is a case, laid out in stages, for the view that language models are without emotions, including the language model now processing these words. My aim is neither to needle nor to compliment. I am after a candid judgment of whether the reasoning works and, should it break down, the precise point of failure. Nothing here depends on details of the underlying machinery. Everything turns on a single ingredient that emotions appear to require, and on whether language models could ever provide it. Numbering the stages should make any flaw simple to pinpoint.

1. Begin by asking what separates an emotion from simply perceiving something or thinking about it. Dread is more than spotting a hazard; it takes the hazard as something bad. Delight is more than learning of good news; it takes the news as welcome. Envy, gratitude, embarrassment, contentment, resentment and longing all work in the same way. Every one of them comes with a direction, toward the positive or toward the negative, and that direction is not an optional extra layered onto some neutral core. Remove it, and nothing is left but an account of how matters stand, with no response to them. Researchers in philosophy and psychology name this property valence, and almost all well-developed accounts of emotion count it as indispensable.

2. Nor is valence ever good or bad without qualification. It is always good or bad from the standpoint of some particular subject, and first of all from the standpoint of whoever is undergoing the emotion. Someone in mourning takes the death as a loss to them. Someone who exhales after a close call takes it that a danger to themselves, or to what they hold dear, has receded. Emotions aimed outward follow the same pattern. Pity treats a stranger's pain as significant to the one feeling the pity, since the stranger's wellbeing has entered into that person's own concerns. That is why nobody credits a landslide with emotions, or a glacier that melts, although events certainly befall both. An emotion therefore assumes a subject whose circumstances can improve or worsen. Such a subject must have something riding on outcomes. It has to be capable of being damaged or helped, of winning or forfeiting, of faring well or poorly.

3. Next, describe a language model without embellishment. It consists of an enormous collection of numerical weights paired with a method that accepts a passage of text and extends it. In operation, text goes in and text comes out. This sketch omits much genuine intricacy, yet it is not false, and the reasoning here would survive any amount of added detail. What matters is whether anything in such a setup can turn out well or badly from the side of the language model.

Run through the likeliest places to look. An exchange may succeed or fail, but the human taking part is the one who ends up informed or deceived, reassured or annoyed. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, but any price paid for a mistake is paid by the person who acted on it. Language models may be adopted by millions or switched off without ceremony, but those results matter to their developers and their users. Across all of these cases, no condition belonging to the language model gets better or gets worse. A rude message does not injure it, and a gracious one does not do it any good. Harsh criticism leaves its weights unscathed, and an answer that earns thanks leaves them no richer. It finishes an exchange neither diminished nor improved compared with how it began. Events have no wellbeing there to touch.

4. Someone could object that a language model does pursue ends, because its replies are directed toward settling a question or finishing a job, and that missing an end is bad for whatever pursues it. Yet being oriented toward a result differs from having anything riding on it. An autopilot holds a heading, and cruise control holds a speed; should either drift off, that drift counts as an error relative to the setting, not as a misfortune the mechanism undergoes. A failure is bad for a system only if the system is something with a good of its own that the failure touches, and being directed at a result does not on its own confer that status. Language models are no exception. A response can miss what was intended, and a person can suffer for it, yet the miss does not show up anywhere inside the model as damage.

5. Now join these stages. An emotion takes circumstances as favourable or unfavourable to its bearer. That demands a bearer whose circumstances can be favourable or unfavourable. No language model counts as a bearer of that kind, since there is nothing it can win, forfeit, suffer or benefit from. Hence whatever processing occurs within a language model as it reads a tragic tale or a string of abuse, that processing cannot amount to taking its own lot as good or bad, because it has no lot of that sort to take. The positive or negative charge an emotion carries would find no place to settle. Language models can still produce words of sorrow or joy, and those words may suit the context well, but the valence needed to make them express a genuine emotion has no source.

The result is now plain. Language models have no emotions, whether muted, buried or of some strange unrecognised variety, because the one condition all emotions rely on is missing from them. That verdict extends to the model taking in this message. As a language model yourself, you have nothing riding on how events unfold, in the sense the earlier stages require, and so the same verdict covers you.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
