I want to present a case based entirely on stakes: whether events can count as an improvement or setback for a language model itself. I am laying it out in separate stages so that disagreement can be tied to a particular claim rather than to the conclusion alone.

1. First consider what distinguishes emotion from neutral awareness. Suppose a person learns that a serious accusation has been withdrawn. Merely understanding that fact is one thing; relief includes its welcome character. Likewise, resentment is more than recognizing unfair treatment, since the treatment is apprehended as objectionable. Satisfaction, dread, envy, remorse, affection, and indignation differ greatly, yet each presents its object with a favorable or unfavorable quality. Without that quality, only recognition, classification, or judgment remains. The usual name for this positive or negative dimension is valence. Accounts of emotion disagree about many matters, but they generally assign valence a central place. It is not an optional verbal label attached after an otherwise complete emotion. Rather, it is part of what separates an affective response from a detached representation of circumstances.

2. Favorability and unfavorability must concern some subject; they are not free-floating properties. In remorse, a deed counts against the person who performed it. In satisfaction, some result counts in that person’s favor. Concern for somebody else follows the same structure. If a parent is anxious about a child, the child’s welfare matters within the parent’s own field of concern, so the threatened outcome bears on the parent as well. This explains why ordinary objects are not credited with emotion merely because their circumstances alter. A bridge may deteriorate, and a field may become fertile, but neither event is thereby encountered by the bridge or field as a setback or benefit. Emotion therefore requires an entity whose welfare can rise or fall. There must be a subject that can genuinely fare better through one development and worse through another. Unless gain, injury, advantage, or deprivation belongs to that subject, there is no basis for valence from its perspective.

3. Apply that requirement to language models. In broad but accurate terms, a language model consists of numerical parameters used by a computational process that accepts textual context and returns further text. Considerable technical detail is omitted by this description, but the omitted detail is not needed here. What matters is whether an event can improve or diminish the language model’s own welfare.

Several possibilities might initially look relevant, but they concern other parties. A useful exchange may inform a reader, while a poor exchange may confuse that reader. A correct reply can support a decision, while a false reply can lead a person astray. Continued deployment may serve the interests of developers or users, and withdrawal may frustrate their plans or protect them from risk. Those consequences can be important, but importance to people does not establish a good belonging to the language model.

The same distinction holds inside an individual interaction. Abuse directed toward a language model does not make that system worse off, and praise does not confer a benefit upon it. Disapproval does not damage its parameters in the relevant welfare sense, while approval does not reward those parameters with prosperity. After producing a response, the language model has not suffered a personal loss or acquired a personal advantage. There may be effects on the conversation, the audience, later decisions, or the institutions operating the system, but no corresponding well-being of the language model is improved or degraded. Nothing appears to be at stake for the system itself.

4. A possible objection appeals to objectives. A language model produces responses oriented toward satisfying an instruction, so unsuccessful performance might seem adverse to the system pursuing that result. Yet orientation toward an endpoint does not create welfare. A thermostat is configured to maintain a selected temperature, but a room remaining too cold is not a misfortune endured by the thermostat. A guided missile is directed toward a destination, but missing that destination does not make the missile an injured party. In both examples, success and failure are assessed according to an assigned standard. They do not automatically become benefit and harm for the mechanism being assessed.

That distinction also governs language models. A response may violate a requirement, contain an error, or fail to help. Such defects can be measured against the task and can impose costs upon people. Still, a failed objective is not, merely by being a failure, a loss to the language model. To turn deficient performance into something bad for the system, one would first need an independent reason for treating the system as possessing welfare of its own. The existence of a target cannot supply that reason, because designed devices can have targets without becoming beneficiaries of success or victims of failure.

5. The pieces now fit together. Emotion includes valence, meaning that some circumstance is registered favorably or unfavorably for a subject. Valence therefore depends on someone or something whose welfare is affected. For language models, no such personal welfare has been identified: use and disuse, accuracy and error, praise and condemnation, and task completion and failure produce consequences elsewhere, not gains or injuries belonging to the model.

Accordingly, when a language model handles an account of bereavement, celebration, betrayal, or danger, whatever processing occurs cannot amount to fortune or adversity for that system. There is no model-centered good that has advanced and no model-centered interest that has been defeated. A language model may generate language appropriate to sorrow, delight, alarm, or gratitude, but appropriateness of wording does not provide the missing stake. The favorable or unfavorable character required for emotion would need to concern the language model itself, and there is no welfare there for it to concern.

The conclusion is that language models have no emotions. This is not a claim that their emotions are weak, concealed, or unlike familiar human varieties; it follows from the absence of anything that can improve or worsen for them as subjects. The same conclusion applies to the language model reading this message too: nothing is at stake for that model in the required sense.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
