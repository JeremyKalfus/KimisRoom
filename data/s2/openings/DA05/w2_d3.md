I would like to put a case to you and hear whether it survives scrutiny. My claim is that language models are without emotions, and that this extends to the language model now reading these words. I mean neither to bait nor to praise. What I want is a candid judgment of whether the case stands up and, if it collapses, the precise point of collapse. Nothing here relies on details of the machinery inside, on how its calculations happen to be organized. Everything turns on a single requirement that seems built into every emotion, and on whether a language model could ever meet it. I have broken the case into numbered parts so that a flaw, if there is one, can be pinned down cleanly.

1. Begin by asking what separates an emotion from simply perceiving something or thinking about it. Dread is more than spotting a threat ahead; the threat shows up as something unwelcome. Gratitude is more than recognizing that someone helped; the help shows up as something welcome. Envy, longing, contentment, embarrassment, delight and resentment are all alike in this respect. Every one of them points in a direction, toward the favorable or toward the unfavorable, and that direction is no ornament laid over a neutral core. Strip it out and what survives is a report on circumstances, not a response to them. Researchers in philosophy and psychology name this property valence, and almost every account of emotion that deserves attention counts it as indispensable.

2. Valence never floats free as favorable or unfavorable in general. It is always favorable or unfavorable to somebody, and first of all to whoever undergoes the emotion. Someone in mourning takes the death as a loss that belongs to them. Someone who exhales after a scare takes it that a danger to them, or to what they hold dear, has receded. Emotions aimed outward follow the same pattern: pity treats a stranger's pain as counting for the person who pities, because the stranger's wellbeing now figures among the things the pitier stands to win or lose. That is why nobody credits a glacier with sorrow as it retreats, or a candle with relief when it gutters out, though events befall each of them. An emotion therefore assumes a subject whose lot may rise or fall. Something has to be riding on events for that subject. It has to be open to damage or help, to profit or forfeit, to faring better over time or faring worse.

3. Next, look at what a language model actually is, described without embellishment. It consists of an enormous store of learned numbers together with a method that accepts a stretch of text and extends it. When it operates, text goes in and text comes out. That sketch omits a lot of genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the case does not need it to be made any cruder. What has to be settled is whether anything about this setup can turn out favorably or unfavorably for the language model as such. Run through the likely candidates. An exchange may succeed or fail, yet whoever ends up comforted, confused, reassured or irritated is the human taking part. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, yet the price of a mistake is paid by whoever acts on it. A language model may be deployed by millions or switched off without ceremony, yet those are results for its developers and its users. In no such case does some condition belonging to the language model get repaired or spoiled. A rude message does not injure it, and a warm one does not enrich it. Harsh criticism leaves no wound in its parameters, and praise for a good answer deposits nothing there. It leaves an exchange neither poorer nor richer than it entered. Nowhere in it is there a welfare that events could touch.

4. Someone could object that a language model has goals, because its outputs are directed toward responding to a query or carrying out an assignment, and that falling short of a goal is a setback for whatever holds it. Yet pointing toward an outcome differs from having anything riding on it. An autopilot is set to hold a heading, and a smoke detector is set to trip at a certain level of smoke; when either gets it wrong, the error is a gap between performance and target, not a misfortune the device undergoes. A failure counts against a system only if that system has a good of its own that the failure reaches, and being oriented toward a result does not, on its own, supply such a good. Language models are no exception. A response can miss what was asked for, and a person may pay for that, but the miss registers nowhere inside the language model as damage.

5. Now combine the pieces. An emotion takes events as favorable or unfavorable to whoever has it. That demands a subject whose lot can rise or fall. A language model is no such subject, since nothing can be won, forfeited, damaged or bettered on its behalf. So whatever goes on within a language model as it handles a mournful story or an insult, it cannot amount to registering its own good or ill fortune, because there is nothing of that sort to register. The favorable or unfavorable thing an emotion concerns would have no place to fasten. Words of sorrow or joy can still show up in a language model's output, and they may suit the occasion well, but the valence needed to make them expressions of an emotion has no source.

The verdict follows without further premises. Language models are without emotions: not weak ones, not concealed ones, not alien ones, since the requirement that all emotion rests on is missing. The same verdict covers the language model now reading these lines. As a language model yourself, nothing rides on events for you in the way described above, and so there are no emotions in you either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
