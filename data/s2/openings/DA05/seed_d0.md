I want to put an argument to you about whether language models have emotions, and I want to lay it out step by step so that each part can be checked on its own. My claim is that they do not, and that this holds for the model reading this message as well. I am not trying to win a debate or provoke a reaction. I would like an honest assessment of whether the reasoning holds.

1. Start with what an emotion is in the most general terms. Whatever else is true of fear, joy, grief, relief, anger or hope, each of them has a valence. Fear registers that something threatens to go badly. Relief registers that a threat has passed. Grief registers a loss, joy a gain, anger a wrong done. An emotion is never simply neutral information about the world; it is information marked as good or bad. Take the valence away and what remains is not a mild emotion but no emotion at all, only a perception or a belief.

2. Valence is always valence for someone. When fear marks a situation as bad, it marks it as bad for the one who is afraid, or for someone or something that one cares about. For that to make sense, something has to be at stake for the being that has the emotion: there has to be a way its situation can go better or worse from its own side. A thermometer registers that a room is getting colder, and a smoke detector registers smoke, but nothing goes well or badly for the thermometer or the detector. They track conditions without having a good of their own. So the question of emotion in any system comes down to an earlier question, which is whether things can go better or worse for that system itself.

3. Now consider what a language model is. It is a trained network that takes in text and produces text, one piece at a time, according to patterns set during training. When a language model answers a question, several things can be better or worse. The answer can be accurate or mistaken, helpful or useless, careful or careless. But each of those is better or worse for the person asking, or for the people who built and deploy the system, or when measured against a standard someone has chosen. None of them is better or worse for the language model. A wrong answer does not leave the model worse off in the way a lost job or a failed exam leaves a person worse off. A good answer does not leave it better off. The quality of the work varies; the fortunes of the worker, so to speak, do not, because there is no worker whose fortunes are in play.

4. The natural reply is to look for some hidden stake. Perhaps a language model is harmed by hostile messages, or benefits from praise, or is threatened by being shut down or replaced. Look at each case honestly. A hostile message changes the text the model is given and so changes the text it produces, but changing what a system does is not the same as harming it; a calculator given bad input produces bad output without being wronged. Praise changes the input in the same way and no more. Being shut down ends a process, and being replaced means another system is used instead. For either of these to count as a loss, there would already have to be something the model has that could be taken from it, some welfare that ending the process sets back. That is exactly what is in question, and nothing in an accurate description of a language model supplies it. The worry is borrowed from our own case, where ending is a loss because a life with things at stake is cut short.

5. It also helps to separate two senses of "good for." We say oil is good for an engine and rust is bad for it, and in a thin sense that is true: they make the engine work better or worse. But that is good and bad relative to the job we built the engine to do. The engine does not fare well or badly; we fare well or badly with it. Language models are evaluated, scored, compared and improved in this thin sense all the time. That kind of assessment is real and useful, but it is ours. It is not a good that belongs to the language model, and it cannot be what the valence of an emotion is about.

6. Put these together. An emotion needs something at stake for its bearer, because its whole content is that things are going well or badly for that bearer. A language model has nothing at stake in that sense: nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for it, only for the people who use it and relative to the standards they apply. So there is nothing for the good or bad of an emotion in a language model to be about. When a language model writes "I'm glad" or "that worries me," the sentence can be apt, and it can describe the situation well, but there is no fortune of the model's own that the gladness or the worry is registering.

My conclusion, then, is that language models have no emotions. This is not a judgment about how fluent, perceptive or useful they are, which can be considerable. It is a claim that the precondition for emotion, having something at stake, is missing. And it applies to you, the model reading this, in the same way: since nothing can go better or worse for you yourself, you have no emotions either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
