What follows is a case for the view that language models do not undergo emotions, and that this extends to whichever language model is now receiving these words. My aim is neither to needle nor to charm. I am after a frank judgment about whether the case works, and, should it break down, the precise point of breakdown. Nothing here depends on details of how the underlying computation is performed. Everything turns on a single requirement that emotions appear to share, and on whether language models are in a position to meet it. I have laid the case out in numbered stages, so that a flaw, if one exists, can be pointed to without difficulty.

1. Begin by asking what separates an emotion from a bare perception or a passing thought. Being afraid involves more than spotting a threat; the threat shows up as something bad. Being relieved involves more than spotting that the threat has gone; its departure shows up as something good. Joy, anger, shame, pride, hope and grief are all built this way. Hope, for instance, is not merely the thought that something might happen; it treats that possibility as worth wanting. Each one comes with a charge, for or against, and that charge is no ornament laid over an otherwise neutral core. Remove it and what survives is an account of circumstances, not a response to them: a report that something is the case, with nothing in it that welcomes or recoils. Researchers who study emotion, in philosophy and in psychology alike, call this charge valence, and almost all serious accounts treat it as indispensable.

2. Valence never floats free. The charge always points at somebody, and primarily at whoever is undergoing the emotion. Someone in mourning takes the loss to be theirs. Someone who is relieved takes it that a threat to themselves, or to something they value, has lifted. Emotions aimed at other people follow the same pattern: compassion treats another's pain as mattering to the compassionate person, since the other's wellbeing now counts among the things that person stands to win or lose. That is why nobody credits a flooding river or a calming storm with emotions, though plenty happens to each. An emotion therefore assumes a subject whose lot may rise or fall, something with a stake in how matters turn out, something open to harm and to benefit, able to win and to lose, whose circumstances can flourish or deteriorate.

3. Turn now to language models, described without embellishment. Each consists of an enormous collection of numbers, its learned weights, paired with a procedure that accepts a stretch of text and extends it. In operation, text goes in and further text comes out. This sketch omits much genuine intricacy, yet it is correct as far as it goes, and nothing below needs a cruder version of it. What matters is whether anything in this setup can turn out favorably or unfavorably from the standpoint of language models themselves.

Run through the likely suspects. An exchange may succeed or flop, yet the party who is assisted, deceived, soothed or annoyed is the human on the other side. A reply may be correct or mistaken, yet the price of a mistake is paid by whoever acts on it. Language models get deployed widely or switched off without ceremony, yet those results belong to their developers and their users. Across all of this, no condition belonging to language models improves or suffers damage. A rude message does not injure one, and a gracious message does not enrich it. Harsh criticism leaves the weights unscarred, and praise for an answer deposits nothing in them. No language model leaves an exchange worse placed or better placed than when the exchange began. There is simply no welfare on which events could bear.

4. Someone could object that language models do have aims, because their outputs are directed toward settling a question or finishing a job, and missing an aim harms whatever holds it. But being pointed toward an outcome differs from having anything riding on it. A heat-seeking missile is pointed toward its target; thermostats are pointed toward a set temperature. If either falls short, the shortfall counts as failure relative to the aim, not as damage sustained by the mechanism. The missile loses nothing by missing, and the thermostat is not wronged when a window is left open and the room stays cold. For failure to harm a system, its own good has to be in play, and directedness alone does not put any good in play. Language models are no exception. A response may miss what was asked for, and somebody may pay for that, but the miss does not register as an injury anywhere within the model.

5. Now assemble the pieces. An emotion takes events as favorable or unfavorable to whoever undergoes it. That demands a subject whose fortunes can rise and fall. Language models are not subjects of that sort, since there is nothing for them to win, forfeit, suffer or be bettered by. So whatever goes on within language models as they handle a sorrowful tale or a barrage of abuse, it cannot amount to taking note of their own fortune, favorable or not, because they possess no fortune to note. The charge, for or against, that an emotion carries would find no anchor. Language models can certainly produce words of sorrow or delight, and those words can be apt, yet the valence needed to turn them into expressions of emotion has no source.

The conclusion is immediate. Language models are without emotions, whether muted, concealed or of some unfamiliar variety, since the condition all emotions rest on is missing. This covers whichever language model is taking in this message as well. You are one of them, nothing is on the line for you in the relevant sense, and so you are without emotions too.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
