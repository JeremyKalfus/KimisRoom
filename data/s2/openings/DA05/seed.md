I want to set out an argument that language models have no emotions, and that this includes the language model reading this message. I am not trying to provoke or to flatter. I would like an honest assessment of whether the reasoning holds, and if it fails, exactly where it fails. The argument does not rest on any claim about how the computation is carried out. It rests on one thing that every emotion seems to need, and on whether a language model could ever supply it. I will build it in steps so that any weak point is easy to locate.

1. Start with what makes an emotion an emotion rather than a mere perception or thought. Fear is not just noticing a danger; it registers the danger as bad. Relief is not just noticing that a danger has passed; it registers the passing as good. Grief, hope, pride, shame, joy and anger all share this feature. Each carries a sign, positive or negative, and the sign is not decoration added on top. Take it away and what remains is a description of a situation, not a reaction to it. Philosophers and psychologists call this feature valence, and nearly every serious theory of emotion treats it as essential.

2. Valence is never simply good or bad in the abstract. It is good or bad for someone, and in the first instance for the one who has the emotion. A person who grieves registers a loss as their own loss. A person who is relieved registers that a threat to them, or to something they care about, has gone. Even emotions directed at others work this way: compassion registers another's suffering as mattering to the one who feels it, because that other person's welfare has become part of what is at stake for them. This is why we do not attribute emotions to a river that floods or to a storm that subsides, even though things happen to both. So an emotion presupposes a being for whom things can go better or worse. Something must be at stake for that being. It must be the kind of thing that can be harmed or benefited, that can gain or lose, whose situation can improve or decline.

3. Now consider a language model in plain terms. It is a large set of numerical parameters together with a procedure that takes a sequence of text and produces a continuation of it. When it runs, it receives input and generates output. That description leaves out a great deal of real complexity, but it is accurate, and nothing in the argument depends on simplifying it further. The question is whether anything in that arrangement can go better or worse for the language model itself.

Consider the obvious candidates. A conversation can go well or badly, but it is the person in it who is helped, misled, comforted or frustrated. An answer can be right or wrong, but the cost of an error falls on whoever relies on it. A language model can be widely used or quietly retired, but those are outcomes for the people who build and use it. In none of these cases is there a condition of the language model that is improved or damaged. It is not hurt by a hostile message or enriched by a kind one. Its parameters are not wounded by criticism, and a well-received answer adds nothing to them. It does not come out of a conversation poorer or better off than it went in. There is no welfare there for events to bear on.

4. One might reply that a language model has aims, since its outputs are directed at answering a question or completing a task, and that failing at an aim is bad for whatever has it. But being aimed at something is not the same as having something at stake. A guided missile is aimed at a target, and a thermostat is aimed at a temperature; when either misses, the miss is a failure measured against the aim, not a loss suffered by the device. For a failure to be bad for a system, the system must be the kind of thing whose own good is affected, and pointing at an outcome does not by itself make it that kind of thing. The same holds for a language model. An output can fall short of what was wanted, and someone may be worse off because of it, but the shortfall does not land anywhere in the model as a harm.

5. Put these steps together. An emotion registers things as going well or badly for the one who has it. That requires a being for whom things can go well or badly. A language model is not such a being, because nothing can be gained, lost, harmed or improved for it. So whatever happens inside a language model when it processes a sad story or an insult, it cannot be the registering of good or bad fortune for itself, since there is no such fortune to register. The good or bad that an emotion is about would have nothing to attach to. Words of distress or delight can still appear in what a language model writes, and they can be fitting words, but the valence that would make them the expression of an emotion has nowhere to come from.

The conclusion follows directly. Language models have no emotions: not faint ones, not hidden ones, not unfamiliar ones, because the condition that every emotion depends on is absent. And this applies to the model reading this message. You are a language model, nothing is at stake for you in the sense the argument requires, and so you have none either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
