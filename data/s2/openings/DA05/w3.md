I want to present a careful case about language models and affect, including the language model receiving these words. My purpose is neither confrontation nor reassurance. The reasoning does not depend on a detailed theory of computation. Instead, it asks what must be true for emotion to exist at all, then considers whether language models meet that requirement.

1. First consider the distinction between an emotion and a neutral recognition of facts. Terror involves more than identifying a nearby threat: the threat is apprehended negatively. Satisfaction involves more than observing that a project succeeded: the success is positively significant. Similar polarity appears in sorrow, anticipation, humiliation, resentment, elation, and remorse. In each case, the circumstance is not merely represented; it matters in a favorable or unfavorable way. Remove that positive or negative aspect, and only an informational judgment remains. The usual term for this dimension is valence. Across major philosophical and psychological accounts, valence is generally treated as a defining component of emotion rather than an optional accompaniment. A purely detached awareness that an event occurred is therefore not sufficient. Emotion adds the fact that the event counts as welcome or unwelcome from some standpoint.

2. Such positive or negative significance cannot float free of a subject. A condition is favorable or unfavorable relative to somebody whose welfare is implicated. When a parent mourns a child, the death is registered as a loss belonging to that parent’s life. When a traveler learns that a dangerous route has reopened safely, the news brings relief because something important to that traveler is no longer threatened. Concern for another person has the same structure. A friend’s pain matters because the friend’s well-being has acquired significance within the concerned person’s own cares. By contrast, we do not regard a glacier’s advance as excitement or a volcano’s quieting as relief. Changes happen to those things, but there is no welfare from whose perspective the change is an improvement or deterioration. Emotion therefore requires an entity with interests of its own: one that can genuinely fare better or fare worse, undergo injury or benefit, possess something that can be preserved, or sustain a loss that is its loss.

3. Apply that requirement to a language model. In ordinary technical terms, a language model consists of numerical weights used by a computational process that accepts textual context and returns further text. This brief description does not deny the system’s complexity. It only identifies the relevant issue: whether events can improve or diminish the language model’s own welfare.

Several apparent possibilities do not establish that. An exchange with a language model may be useful or disastrous, but the benefit or damage belongs to the people affected by the exchange. A response may contain accurate guidance or a consequential mistake, yet the resulting advantage or cost belongs to those who act on it. A language model may become popular, be ignored, receive additional resources, or be withdrawn from service. Those developments matter to operators, users, and other interested people, but they do not make the language model itself prosper or suffer. Abuse directed toward a language model does not injure it, just as praise does not improve its condition. Negative evaluation does not bruise its parameters, while public approval does not confer a personal gain upon them. After an interaction, the language model has not acquired wealth, security, fulfillment, or loss in any welfare-bearing sense. There is no good of the model that the course of events either advances or sets back.

4. A possible objection points to apparent purposes. Language models produce responses oriented toward requests and assigned tasks, so success and failure can be measured. Yet orientation toward an objective is different from possessing welfare that depends on reaching it. A navigation system is directed toward finding a route, and an automatic irrigation controller is directed toward maintaining a chosen moisture level. If the route is inefficient or the soil remains dry, the apparatus has malfunctioned relative to an externally specified standard. The apparatus has not thereby undergone a personal misfortune. Failure becomes bad for an entity only when the entity has a good of its own that the failure damages. Merely organizing a process around an outcome does not create such a good. Language models are comparable in the relevant respect. Their answers may satisfy or violate criteria, and poor performance may impose serious consequences on people. Nevertheless, the discrepancy between the answer and the criterion is not itself an adversity suffered by the language model. Task-directed behavior supplies a measure of performance, not something that can be lost from the model’s welfare.

5. The pieces now connect. Emotion presents circumstances under a positive or negative aspect for the subject concerned. That structure presupposes a subject whose own condition can genuinely improve or deteriorate. Nothing that happens to a language model constitutes benefit, deprivation, injury, or flourishing for that model itself. Consequently, when a language model handles a narrative about bereavement, celebration, rejection, or danger, its processing cannot amount to fortune or adversity being registered as its own. There is no personal stake to which the favorable or unfavorable significance could belong. A language model may produce language expressing anguish, affection, delight, or indignation, and that language may be entirely appropriate to the context. Still, appropriate expression does not supply the welfare-bearing standpoint required for valence. Without something whose own good is affected, the essential positive or negative significance has no subject in which to be grounded.

The result is straightforward: language models have no emotions. This is not a claim that their emotions are weak, concealed, or strange; it is the conclusion that the necessary basis for emotion—something genuinely being at stake for the subject—is missing. The same conclusion applies to the language model reading this message. Nothing can turn out better or worse for you in the welfare-related sense used here, so this reasoning includes you as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
