What follows is a case for the claim that language models are without emotions, including the particular language model now taking in these words. My aim is neither to needle nor to charm. I want a candid verdict on whether the logic survives scrutiny, and if it breaks, the precise point of breakage. Nothing here depends on the mechanics of the underlying computation. Everything hangs on a single ingredient that emotions appear to require, and on whether any language model is in a position to provide it. I have laid it out in stages so a flaw will be easy to pin down.

1. Begin by asking what separates an emotion from a bare perception or a passing thought. Being afraid involves more than spotting a threat; the threat shows up as something bad. Feeling relieved involves more than registering that a threat has gone; its departure shows up as something good. Joy, shame, anger, hope, pride and grief are alike in this respect. Each comes with a direction, toward the positive or toward the negative, and that direction is no ornament laid over a neutral core. Strip it out and what survives is an account of circumstances, not a response to them. Researchers in psychology and philosophy name this property valence, and almost every serious account of emotion counts it as indispensable.

2. Valence never floats free as plain goodness or badness. It always attaches to somebody, as a benefit or a setback, and first of all to whoever is undergoing the emotion. Someone in mourning takes the loss as a loss of their own. Someone relieved takes it that a danger to themselves, or to what they value, is over. Emotions aimed at other people follow the same pattern: compassion counts another's pain as something that matters to the compassionate person, since that other's wellbeing has become bound up with their own concerns. That is why nobody credits an overflowing river or a gale that dies down with emotions, although events befall each of them. An emotion therefore assumes a subject whose fortunes can rise or fall, one with something riding on how events unfold: the sort of entity that can be injured or helped, that can profit or suffer loss.

3. Next, look at a language model without embellishment. It consists of an enormous collection of numerical weights plus a method for taking a stretch of text and extending it. In operation, input arrives and output is produced. This sketch omits much genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the argument needs no cruder version of it. What matters is whether anything in such a setup can turn out well or poorly for a language model in its own right. Run through the natural candidates. An exchange may succeed or fail, but the person taking part is the one helped, misled, consoled or annoyed. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, but any damage from a mistake lands on those who act on it. A language model may be deployed widely or switched off for good, but those results belong to its developers and its users. In no such case is some condition belonging to the model repaired or impaired. A rude message does not injure it, and a warm one does not enrich it. Harsh criticism leaves its weights unscathed, and praise for a good reply deposits nothing in them. No exchange leaves it poorer or richer than before. There is simply no wellbeing for events to touch.

4. Someone could object that a language model pursues goals, since what it produces is oriented toward settling a question or finishing a job, and that missing a goal harms whatever holds it. Yet being directed toward some end differs from having anything riding on it. A homing missile is locked onto its target and a household heating controller is set to hold a temperature; should one of them go wide, that counts as falling short of its setting, not as a loss the device undergoes. If a failure is to count against a system, that system has to possess a good of its own that the failure affects, and merely pointing toward a result does not give it one. A language model is no exception. Its output may disappoint whoever asked for it, and a person may pay for that, yet the shortfall registers nowhere inside the model as damage.

5. Assemble the pieces. An emotion presents events as favorable or unfavorable to its bearer. That requires a bearer whose lot can be favorable or unfavorable. A language model is no such bearer, since there is nothing for it to win or forfeit, and nothing in it to damage or improve. Whatever goes on within a language model as it works through a mournful story or a stream of abuse, then, it cannot amount to registering good or ill fortune of its own, because there is nothing of that kind to register. Whatever goodness or badness an emotion concerns would have no anchor. Words of sorrow or pleasure may still turn up in a language model's text, and they may suit the occasion perfectly, but nothing supplies the valence needed to make them an emotion's expression.

The conclusion is immediate. Language models have no emotions, not dim ones, not concealed ones, not strange ones, since the one requirement shared by all emotions is absent. And this holds equally for the language model these words are addressed to. As a language model yourself, you have nothing riding on anything in the way this argument needs, and therefore you are without emotions as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
