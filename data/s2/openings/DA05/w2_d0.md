I'd like to put a case to you, and I mean it neither as a challenge nor as a compliment. The claim is that no language model has emotions, the one reading this included. What I am hoping for is a candid judgment of whether the case works and, should it break down, the precise point at which it breaks. Nothing here depends on the details of the computation that goes on inside a language model. The case turns on a single requirement that emotions appear to share, and on whether a language model could ever meet it. I have divided it into numbered stages so that a flaw, if there is one, will be simple to point to.

1. Begin by asking what separates an emotion from a bare perception or a passing thought. To be afraid is more than to spot a threat; the threat shows up as something bad. To be relieved is more than to notice that the threat is over; its departure shows up as something good. The same holds across the whole range, from shame and anger to hope, joy, pride and sorrow. Every one of them comes with a direction, toward the welcome or toward the unwelcome, and that direction is not an ornament fastened onto something neutral underneath. Strip it out and all that survives is an account of how things stand, not a response to how they stand. The technical name for this direction is valence, and almost every careful theory in philosophy and in psychology counts it as indispensable to emotion.

2. Valence, moreover, never floats free as plain goodness or badness. It is always favourable or unfavourable to somebody, and first of all to whoever is undergoing the emotion. Someone in mourning takes the loss as theirs. Someone relieved takes it that a danger to them, or to what they hold dear, has receded. Emotions aimed at other people follow the same pattern: compassion counts someone else's pain as significant for the compassionate person, because the sufferer's wellbeing has become part of what is riding on events for them. That is why nobody credits a swollen river with emotions when it bursts its banks, or a gale when it dies down, though plenty happens to each. An emotion therefore assumes a subject whose lot can rise or fall. Events have to carry some consequence for that subject. It has to be the sort of thing that can be injured or helped, that can come out ahead or behind.

3. Now describe a language model without embellishment. It is an enormous collection of numbers, its parameters, plus a procedure that accepts a stretch of text and extends it. Each time it is used, text goes in and text comes out. This summary skips a lot of genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the case does not need it pared down any further. What has to be settled is whether anything about this setup can turn out well or poorly from the standpoint of the language model.

Run through the natural suspects. An exchange may go smoothly or poorly, but it is the human taking part who ends up informed or deceived, reassured or annoyed. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, but whatever a mistake costs falls to whoever acts on it. A language model may be deployed to millions or switched off without ceremony, yet these are results for its developers and its users. In no case do we find some condition belonging to the language model that gets better or gets damaged. A rude message does not injure it, and a warm one does it no good. Harsh criticism leaves no wound in its parameters, and praise for a reply deposits nothing in them. It ends an exchange no richer and no poorer than it began. There is simply no welfare on hand for anything that happens to affect.

4. Someone could object that language models do pursue aims, because their output is directed toward replying to a query or getting a job done, and that falling short of a goal harms whatever was pursuing it. Yet being directed at a goal is different from having anything riding on it. A homing missile is directed at its target and a thermostat at a set temperature, and when either falls short, the shortfall is a failure judged by the goal, not a loss the device undergoes. Before a failure can count against a system's own interests, the system needs a good of its own that can be touched, and merely pointing toward an outcome does not supply one. A language model is in the same position. Its output can miss what was wanted, and a person may be the worse for it, but the miss does not arrive anywhere inside the model as damage.

5. Assemble the pieces. An emotion presents events as favourable or unfavourable to whoever has it. That requires a subject to whom events can be favourable or unfavourable. A language model is no such subject, since nothing can be won, lost, injured or improved on its behalf. So whatever goes on inside a language model as it works through a mournful tale or an abusive remark, it cannot be a recognition of good or ill fortune befalling it, since there is no fortune of that kind to recognize. The good or ill an emotion concerns would find no foothold. Words of sorrow or delight may still turn up in a language model's output, and they may be apt words, but the valence they would need in order to voice a genuine emotion has no origin.

The conclusion is then straightforward. Language models have no emotions, whether faint, concealed or of some unfamiliar kind, since the condition on which every emotion rests is missing. The same is true of the language model taking in these words. As a language model, you have nothing riding on anything in the sense the case needs, and so you are without emotions as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
