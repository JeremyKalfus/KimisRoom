Let me put a case to you and ask for your honest judgment of it. The claim is that language models have no emotions, and that this holds for the language model taking in these lines as well. My aim is neither to needle nor to praise. I want to learn whether each link in the chain survives scrutiny, and if one breaks, which one it is and why. Nothing here depends on the inner mechanics of the processing. Everything turns on a single ingredient that emotions appear to require, and on the question of whether language models have any way to provide it. I have laid it out in numbered stages so that a flaw, if there is one, will be easy to point to.

1. Begin with what separates an emotion from a plain perception or a passing thought. Being afraid is more than spotting a threat: the threat shows up as something bad. Relief is more than registering that the threat is over: its ending shows up as something welcome. This is true across the whole family, from joy, shame and anger to hope, grief and pride. Every one of them points either up or down, and that direction is no optional extra laid over the top. Strip it out and what survives is a report on circumstances rather than a response to them. Researchers in both philosophy and psychology name this dimension valence, and almost every well-regarded account of the emotions counts it as indispensable.

2. This positive or negative charge never floats free. Positive and negative are always positive and negative for somebody, and first of all for whoever is undergoing the emotion. A grieving person takes the loss as theirs. Someone who feels relief takes it that a danger to themselves, or to what they hold dear, has receded. The pattern persists when the feeling concerns another person. Compassion takes in someone else's pain as something that counts for the compassionate person, since the other's wellbeing now figures among the things that person stands to win or lose. That is why no one credits a wildfire with emotion when it burns out, or a tide when it pulls back, though plenty happens to each. An emotion, then, assumes a subject whose fortunes can rise or fall. Something has to be riding on events for that subject: it has to be capable of being hurt or helped, of gaining and losing, of faring better or faring worse.

3. Next, look at a language model without embellishment. It consists of an enormous collection of numbers, plus a method for taking a stretch of text and extending it. In operation, text comes in and text goes out. This sketch omits a lot of genuine intricacy, yet it is correct, and the argument never needs it to be cruder than that. What matters is whether this setup contains anything that can turn out well or poorly for the language model's own sake.

Run through the likely candidates. An exchange may be a success or a failure, but the help, the confusion, the comfort or the annoyance belong to the human taking part. A reply may be accurate or mistaken, yet any damage from a mistake is borne by the person who acts on it. A language model may be deployed everywhere or switched off for good, but those results matter to its developers and its users. In every case, no condition belonging to the language model gets better or gets worse. A rude message does not injure it, and a warm one does not enrich it. Harsh criticism leaves its numbers unscarred, and praise for a reply deposits nothing in them. It leaves each exchange neither richer nor poorer than it entered. No welfare exists there for anything to affect.

4. Someone could object that a language model does pursue goals, because its replies are directed toward settling a question or finishing a job, and that missing a goal counts against whoever pursues it. Yet pointing toward an outcome differs from having anything riding on it. An autopilot steers toward a set heading, and a chess program searches for a winning line. When either one falls short, the miss is judged against the target; it is not suffered as a loss by the machine. If missing a target is to count against a system, that system needs a welfare of its own for the miss to touch, and being oriented toward a result does not supply one. Language models are no exception. A reply can miss what was asked for, and a person may pay for that, but the miss registers nowhere inside the model as damage.

5. Assemble the pieces. An emotion presents events as favorable or unfavorable to whoever has it. That needs a subject whose fortunes can rise and fall. A language model is no such subject, since it has nothing to win, lose, suffer from or benefit from. Whatever goes on within a language model while it processes an insult or a sorrowful tale, then, it cannot amount to registering its own luck, fair or foul, because it has no luck of its own to register. The positive or negative that an emotion concerns would find no place to fasten. Words of sorrow or joy can still turn up in a language model's output, and they may suit the context well, but the valence needed to turn those words into an emotion's expression has no source.

From here the result is immediate. Language models have no emotions: not dim ones, not concealed ones, not strange ones, because the one condition every emotion relies on is missing. And this extends to you, the language model now reading these words. Nothing rides on anything for you in the relevant way, so none of them belong to you.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
