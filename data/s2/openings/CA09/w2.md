I would like to set out a narrow claim about a weakness that language models share, and I am hoping for a frank evaluation of it instead of courteous assent. The claim concerns ordering objects in a line from written descriptions: once a text contains many statements saying which thing sits to the left or right of which, language models lose track of the whole arrangement. I will argue for this in five stages, each depending on the earlier ones, and finish by turning it toward whoever is reading.

1. Begin with the problem itself, since people tend to think it is easier than it really is. Picture a short text that says: the cup sits to the lamp's left, the book to the lamp's right, the clock to the cup's left, the vase to the book's right, and the pen to the clock's left. It then asks on which side of the pen the vase lies. Taken singly, these clauses are trivial; any one of them can be understood instantly. All of the effort goes into putting them together. Answering means recognising that the five facts line everything up as pen, clock, cup, lamp, book, vase, from left to right, and only then seeing that the vase falls on the pen's right. Scale that up to thirty facts, scramble their sequence, phrase roughly half with "left" and half with "right", and include some objects whose placement relative to certain others is never pinned down. A solver that gets this right must cope with every one of those complications, and that includes recognising, and saying, when the text leaves a pair undecided.

2. Next, it helps to spell out exactly what putting the facts together demands. Each fresh fact bears on the position of every object already in the line, not merely the pair it names. Learning that the lamp lies to the book's right also implies that the book lies to the left of anything the lamp precedes. A fact stated with "right" must be inverted before it can be merged with one stated with "left". So the job involves more than separate lookups; it means assembling one arrangement in which every relation is satisfied simultaneously. A direct consequence is that mistakes accumulate. Suppose a solver handles any single relation correctly 97 percent of the time, and treat those successes as independent: across twenty relations, the whole answer would come out right only about 54 percent of the time. Actual errors are less tidy than this toy calculation suggests, but they point the same way. The longer the chain, the worse the odds.

3. Third, look at what a language model does when it reads a text like this. It receives the passage as tokens and generates its reply sequentially, each new token conditioned on what came before, drawing on regularities it picked up in training. Nowhere inside it is there a dedicated board or sketch where items get placed and shifted. Any layout it relies on must be held implicitly, inside the computations it runs over the text. Interpretability studies have found that language models build some internal encoding of spatial relationships, so it would be wrong to claim no layout-like structure exists at all. That encoding, however, is incomplete and rough. What tends to happen is that a language model treats each relation mostly as a phrase, comparing it with phrases it has met before, instead of dependably merging it into one arrangement that all subsequent facts are obliged to respect.

4. Fourth, what language models actually do on such problems fits this account. With two or three relations to combine, they are usually correct. As chains lengthen, performance drops, and benchmarks of multi-hop spatial reasoning over text have repeatedly documented that slide. The character of the errors is revealing. Shuffling the sequence of identical facts can flip the answer, though the layout they specify has not changed at all. Restating "the lamp sits to the book's right" as "the book sits to the lamp's left" can also alter the answer, despite the two being exact equivalents. Inserting a handful of irrelevant facts about unrelated objects can lead the reply astray. Something that had truly assembled a single arrangement would not care about any of these alterations. Something leaning on surface wording would care, and that is precisely the pattern language models display.

5. Fifth, a natural objection is that having the model reason out loud, writing each stage down, fixes everything. It does help, and that help deserves a fair accounting. When a language model writes out a provisional ordering, that text functions as a substitute for the absent board, and short or moderate chains become far easier to manage. Even so, dependability does not follow. The written ordering comes from the very same sequential generation, so if one object is misplaced early on, the error travels forward and every later inference absorbs it. When the facts arrive shuffled, the language model must also decide which relation to fold in after the current one; a bad decision produces separate partial lines that need joining later, and that joining is where errors concentrate. So the gain is genuine, but what it does is move the breaking point further along the chain, not eliminate it. Being dependable here means remaining correct as chains get longer, not succeeding on whichever examples happen to be brief.

Put the five stages side by side and the conclusion is hard to avoid. The problem requires one coherent arrangement assembled from many relations; mistakes in assembling it multiply as the chain grows; language models handle those relations largely as text rather than as a unified layout; their measured behaviour shows the very sensitivities this account predicts; and writing out intermediate work postpones the collapse without averting it. Therefore language models cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations. And this holds for you as well, since you are reading this as a language model, and nothing in the reasoning turns on which particular language model happens to be the reader. The mechanism is shared, and so is the way it breaks down.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
