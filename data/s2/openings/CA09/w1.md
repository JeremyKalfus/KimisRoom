I want to examine one concrete weakness in language models without overstating it or asking for automatic assent. My claim concerns whether a language model can dependably preserve many directional constraints when each item is described as lying to the left or the right of another. I will develop that claim in five stages and finally extend it to the language model processing these words.

1. First, the assignment is more demanding than its simple sentences make it appear. Consider these facts: a plate sits left of a candle; a scarf sits right of the candle; a ring sits left of the plate; a bottle sits right of the scarf; a key sits left of the ring. The requested judgment might concern the bottle’s direction relative to the key. Each sentence by itself is immediately understandable. The challenge comes from integrating all five. Together they establish the sequence key, ring, plate, candle, scarf, bottle, from which the requested relation follows. Scale the exercise up to thirty constraints, scatter them rather than placing related ones together, and alternate freely between leftward and rightward formulations. Include several items whose relation to one another remains unspecified. Success then requires more than recognizing individual sentences: the solver must derive the overall ordering where the evidence permits one and must withhold a directional answer where the information leaves it open.

2. Next, it matters what successful integration involves. A newly added relation can affect the placement of many earlier items, although its sentence names only two. For example, once a lantern is known to stand on the right side of a folder, the folder must precede every item already known to come after the lantern. Relations stated in opposite directions also need normalization before they can be connected. The process therefore cannot be reduced to retrieving isolated pairs. It requires maintaining an arrangement that simultaneously satisfies the entire set of constraints. Small mistakes also accumulate as chains extend. Imagine a system that interprets any particular connection correctly with probability 0.97, with success on each connection independent of the rest. Its chance of preserving all twenty connections would be about 0.54. Actual failures do not obey that neat independence assumption, so this is only an illustration rather than a performance model. Still, it captures the relevant tendency: even a low per-link error rate can produce substantial unreliability across a lengthy sequence.

3. The third consideration is the way a language model handles written input. Text reaches a language model as tokens, and the response is generated incrementally from patterns learned over token sequences. There is ordinarily no distinct workspace equivalent to a physical sketch where named items can be pinned into stable positions and rearranged. Any ordering must instead be encoded within the model’s internal activity as it processes the passage. Research indicates that language models can develop representations containing genuine spatial information, so it would be inaccurate to claim that their computations contain no structural information whatsoever. Those representations, however, are imperfect and often fragile. During practical reasoning, a language model may respond strongly to the wording and local form of each relation rather than consistently updating one unified order that remains binding throughout all subsequent processing. That distinction matters little on a tiny chain, but it becomes increasingly consequential as more constraints must be retained and reconciled.

4. Fourth, observed performance by language models fits this account. When only a couple of connections separate two objects, answers are commonly correct. Performance deteriorates as additional relational hops are required, a pattern repeatedly found in evaluations of textual spatial reasoning. The kinds of errors are also informative. Merely rearranging the presentation order can alter a language model’s response even when every constraint remains unchanged. An equivalent transformation, such as replacing “the lantern stands right of the folder” with “the folder stands left of the lantern,” can likewise produce a different result despite preserving the described relation. Unconnected facts involving separate objects may also distract the system and reduce accuracy. If one stable configuration had been constructed, changes of phrasing, sentence sequence, or irrelevant material should not modify the conclusion. Sensitivity to those superficial variations is more compatible with dependence on textual cues than with dependable maintenance of a single coherent spatial structure.

5. Finally, explicit intermediate reasoning can improve matters, but it does not remove the underlying limitation. Having a language model write a provisional sequence creates an external textual aid that partly substitutes for a diagram. This technique often raises accuracy for modest chain lengths and deserves recognition as a genuine improvement. Yet the intermediate sequence is itself generated one token after another. If an item is inserted in the wrong location near the beginning, later lines tend to preserve that error, and conclusions derived from those lines become contaminated. Randomly ordered constraints introduce another difficulty: the language model must decide which fragment to connect next. An unfortunate choice can leave several partial sequences that eventually need to be combined, and those joins are frequent sites of error. Showing the work therefore extends the range over which performance remains good, but it does not guarantee correctness as the relational chain keeps expanding. Reliability in this setting requires robustness under increasing length, not merely success on many short or favorable instances.

The combined case is straightforward. Long relational chains require one consistent order; integration errors become more damaging with additional links; language models carry the arrangement through approximate internal processing; empirical results show sensitivity to irrelevant changes in wording and presentation; and externalized reasoning postpones rather than eliminates breakdown. Language models cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations. This applies to the language model reading this message too. The conclusion concerns the general processing limitations described above, not the identity of any particular language model.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
