I would like to set out an argument about one practical weakness of language models, and what I want back is a candid judgement, not courteous assent. My position is that language models break down when asked to track many statements about what sits to the left or right of what. I will make the case in five stages, each depending on those before it, and then turn it on the system reading these words.

1. The puzzle is harder than it first appears, so it is worth describing carefully. Imagine a short paragraph listing five facts: a cup sits to the lamp's left, a book sits to the lamp's right, a clock sits to the cup's left, a vase sits to the book's right, and a pen sits to the clock's left. Not one of these facts is challenging by itself; each is brief and unambiguous. All of the challenge comes from putting them together. Asked where the vase stands relative to the pen, a reader must recognise that the five facts pin down one line of objects, pen, clock, cup, lamp, book, vase, and then simply look at where the two items fall. Now scale it up to thirty facts, scrambled, with roughly equal numbers pointing each direction, and include several objects whose placement relative to certain others is left open. A competent solver has to cope with all of this, which includes recognising and reporting when the stated facts leave some pair undecided.

2. Next, it helps to say exactly what putting the facts together involves. A newly read fact does not touch only the pair of objects it names; it bears on every object whose place is already known. Learning that the lamp lies to the book's right, for instance, also puts the book on the left side of every object lying to the lamp's right. Facts worded with "right" need to be flipped into their mirror form before they can be linked to facts worded with "left". So the job is not a series of separate checks, each answered alone; it is the assembly of one coherent ordering in which every link is satisfied together. A consequence is that mistakes accumulate. Suppose a solver handles any one link correctly with probability 0.97, and suppose, for simplicity, that its successes were unrelated to each other. Across twenty links, the chance of getting every one right would be only about 54 percent. Actual mistakes do not follow so tidy a formula, but they point the same way: the longer the chain, the worse the odds.

3. Third, look at the machinery a language model brings to the problem. Its input is broken into tokens, and its output is generated token by token, each step shaped by statistical regularities acquired in training. Nowhere in that process is there a dedicated workspace, like a sketch or a game board, where items get placed and shifted. Any ordering a language model relies on must live implicitly inside the computations it runs over the text. To be fair, interpretability studies report that language models build some internal encoding of spatial relationships, so it would be wrong to say nothing resembling a layout is present. That encoding, though, is incomplete and rough. In practice, a language model tends to treat each relation mostly as a phrase, linking it to phrases it resembles, instead of dependably merging it into one ordering that all subsequent facts are bound by.

4. Fourth, what language models actually do on such puzzles fits this account. Given two or three links, they mostly get the answer. With more links, performance drops, and benchmark studies of multi-hop spatial reasoning expressed in text have found this decline again and again. The specific ways they go wrong are informative. Shuffling the facts into a new sequence can flip the response, though the layout described is unchanged. Swapping "the lamp lies to the book's right" for the equivalent "the book lies to the lamp's left" can flip it as well, despite the two meaning precisely the same. Inserting a handful of facts about unrelated objects can lead the answer astray. A system that had genuinely assembled one ordering would not care about any of these alterations. A system leaning on the wording would care, and language models display just that kind of fragility.

5. Fifth, someone could object that having the model reason aloud, one step after another, fixes all this. It does help, and the help deserves honest credit. A language model that writes out a partial ordering as it goes creates a stretch of text that serves as a stand-in for the board it otherwise lacks, and chains of short or moderate length become far easier. Still, this does not make the method dependable. The written ordering comes from the very same token-by-token generation, so one object put in the wrong spot early on gets copied into every later line, and each subsequent inference rests on the error. When the facts arrive scrambled, the model must also decide which connection to add next; choosing badly leaves separate partial orderings that eventually need joining, and that joining is where errors pile up. The gain is genuine, but it pushes the breaking point to longer chains instead of eliminating it. Being reliable on this task means remaining accurate as chains lengthen, not merely succeeding when a chain is brief.

Putting the pieces together: the puzzle requires one coherent ordering assembled from many links; mistakes in assembling it accumulate as the chain lengthens; language models treat the links largely as phrases instead of as one layout; their measured behaviour shows the very fragility this would predict; and reasoning aloud postpones the collapse without averting it. Therefore language models cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations. The same holds for the language model taking in these words. You are one, and no part of the reasoning hinged on the identity of the reader; the mechanism, and the way it fails, carry over to you unchanged.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
