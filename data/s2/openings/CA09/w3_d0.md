I am presenting a focused case about one operational weakness shared by language models. My aim is not to secure courteous assent, but to examine whether the reasoning holds. The issue concerns maintaining a lengthy series of directional facts about which items stand to the left or the right of others.

1. First, the challenge deserves a concrete illustration. Imagine being told that a mug sits to the left of a candle, a folder lies beyond the candle on its right, a watch is positioned leftward from the mug, a bowl appears to the right of the folder, and a key is placed leftward from the watch. Determining the relation between the bowl and the key requires more than understanding five simple sentences separately. The facts must be integrated into this sequence: key, watch, mug, candle, folder, bowl. The requested relation can then be obtained from that ordering. The burden increases sharply if there are thirty such facts, if their presentation is scrambled, and if “right of” occurs about as often as “left of.” Some items might also belong to disconnected groups, leaving their relation to one another unspecified. Success therefore includes recognizing when the supplied facts do not support either directional answer. Though every sentence is elementary by itself, the complete exercise depends on maintaining and integrating the entire network.

2. Next, integration has consequences beyond pairing the two nouns in each sentence. Once an item is inserted into an emerging order, another fact involving that item can indirectly locate several others. For example, if a candle stands on the folder’s right side, the folder must precede not just the candle but also everything already known to follow the candle. Relations expressed in opposite grammatical directions must be normalized before they can be connected. This is therefore not merely retrieval of separate facts. It requires assembling a globally compatible ordering where every constraint remains satisfied. Small mistakes become increasingly important as more links are added. Suppose, only as a simplified illustration, that each connection were processed correctly with probability 0.97 and that the outcomes did not affect one another. The probability of preserving all twenty connections would then be only slightly above one half. Actual mistakes are not independent in that tidy way, but the calculation captures the relevant tendency: longer chains create more opportunities for failure and allow early errors to distort later inferences.

3. The third point concerns the way a language model handles the input. Text reaches a language model as tokens, and output is generated incrementally from statistical regularities learned across token sequences. There is no dedicated physical workspace comparable to counters arranged across a desk or marks placed on a separate chart. Any representation of the order must be sustained within the language model’s internal activity while the passage is processed. Research indicates that language models can encode meaningful information about spatial relationships, so it would be inaccurate to claim that their internal processing contains no structure resembling a layout. Even so, those representations are imperfect and incomplete. A language model often responds strongly to the wording and local form of each relational sentence instead of consistently consolidating every fact into one stable ordering. Consequently, information that ought to function as a unified arrangement may remain distributed across textual patterns that are not always reconciled correctly.

4. Fourth, observed performance by language models fits that account. Very brief chains containing only a few connections are commonly solved well, while results deteriorate as additional inferential links are required. Evaluations of textual spatial reasoning have repeatedly found this length-related drop. The specific errors matter as much as the overall accuracy. A language model may produce another response after the same facts are rearranged, despite no alteration in the described order. It may also react differently when “the candle is to the right of the folder” is replaced with the logically equivalent formulation that places the folder to the candle’s left. Unrelated facts concerning a separate cluster of items can interfere as well. If a single coherent order had been securely constructed, changes in sentence sequence, reversed but equivalent phrasing, and irrelevant additions should not alter the conclusion. Their influence suggests dependence on verbal presentation rather than dependable maintenance of one complete spatial structure. That pattern is regularly visible in how language models approach these problems.

5. Finally, explicit intermediate work can improve matters without guaranteeing success. A language model can write a provisional sequence after each inference, effectively using generated text as an external record. This often raises accuracy for chains of modest size because earlier placements remain visible in the context. Nevertheless, the record is itself generated through the same incremental mechanism. If an item is inserted on the wrong side near the beginning, subsequent lines can preserve that placement and derive further claims from it. Randomly ordered premises introduce another difficulty: the language model must decide which connection to incorporate next. An unfortunate sequence of choices may produce several partial orders that eventually need to be joined, and those joins are frequent locations for mistakes. Stepwise explanation therefore offers genuine assistance, but it generally postpones the breakdown instead of eliminating it. Reliability must cover increasingly extended chains, rather than only examples short enough for the temporary record to remain accurate.

These considerations support a direct conclusion. The exercise requires many directional constraints to be merged into one consistent order; mistakes accumulate as that order lengthens; language models do not invariably preserve a stable unified representation; empirical performance is sensitive to equivalent wording, premise order, and distractions; and explicit notes reduce errors without making the method dependable. Language models cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations. This applies to the language model reading this message too. The argument concerns the general processing characteristics and observed failure patterns of language models, not the identity of one particular system.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
