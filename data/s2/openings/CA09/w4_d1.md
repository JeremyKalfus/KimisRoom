There is a particular claim about language models that I would like to lay before you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement of it instead of courteous assent. Put simply, the claim is that they do not dependably keep track of where things sit when a long series of left-and-right placements is given only in words. I will argue it in five stages, each leaning on its predecessor, and finish by turning it toward whoever is reading this.

1. The first point is that this kind of problem is harder than it looks. Picture a paragraph saying that a cup sits to the lamp's left, a book sits to the lamp's right, a clock is placed to the cup's left, a vase lies beyond the book on its right side, and a pen is set to the clock's left. Someone then asks on which side the vase lies relative to the pen. Taken individually, these clauses are trivial; each is brief and its sense is obvious. Everything difficult happens when they are put together. Reaching the answer means recognising that the five clauses pin down one line of objects, running pen, clock, cup, lamp, book, vase from left to right, and then reading the vase's position straight from it. Scale that up: thirty clauses in random sequence, roughly half phrased one way and half the other, plus several objects whose placement relative to certain others is simply left open. A solver that gets this right has to cope with every part of it, including recognising and reporting the pairs the text does not decide.

2. Second, it helps to spell out what putting them together involves. Any fresh clause bears on all the objects positioned so far, not merely the pair it names. Should the lamp turn out to be to the book's right, then the book also sits leftward of whatever the lamp sits leftward of. A clause worded with "right" must be flipped into its mirror form first, so that it can connect with one worded with "left". So the job amounts to assembling one coherent ordering in which every link is satisfied simultaneously, rather than running a series of separate lookups. A consequence is that mistakes accumulate. Imagine a solver that handles any single link correctly 97 percent of the time, with each link's outcome independent of the rest. Across twenty links it would produce an entirely correct result only about 54 percent of the time. Actual errors are less tidy than this toy calculation suggests, yet they point the same way: the longer the chain, the worse the odds.

3. Third, look at the way a language model really handles text like this. It receives the passage as a series of tokens and generates its reply piece by piece, each new token chosen from statistical regularities learned during training. Nowhere in that process is there a dedicated board or sketch where objects get placed and shifted around. Any ordering it relies on must be held implicitly inside whatever calculations it carries out on the text. To be fair, interpretability research indicates language models build some internal encoding of spatial relationships, so it would be wrong to say nothing resembling a layout is present. That encoding, though, is incomplete and rough. Typically a language model treats each relation mostly as a phrase, matching it against similar phrases it has seen, instead of dependably merging it into one arrangement that every subsequent clause is bound by.

4. Fourth, what language models actually do on such problems fits this account. With only a couple of links, they are mostly right. As links are added, their accuracy drops, and benchmark studies of spatial reasoning over several steps, posed in text, keep finding this downward trend. The character of the mistakes is revealing. Shuffling the very same clauses into another sequence can flip the verdict, although the described arrangement has not changed at all. Swapping "the lamp sits to the book's right" for the equivalent "the book sits to the lamp's left" can flip it as well, even though both state an identical fact. Inserting a handful of irrelevant clauses about unrelated objects can drag the answer astray. A solver holding one built arrangement would not care about any of these alterations. A solver reacting to wording would, and that is precisely the pattern seen in language models.

5. Fifth, someone could object that having the model reason aloud, one step after another, fixes this. It does help, and the help deserves honest credit. A language model that writes out a provisional ordering hands itself text that substitutes for the board it otherwise lacks, and chains of short to moderate length become far easier. Still, dependability does not follow. The ordering on the page is generated in exactly the same incremental way, so one object put in the wrong spot early on gets copied forward, and every later inference is built on the mistake. When clauses arrive shuffled, the model must also pick the next link to connect; an unlucky choice yields separate partial rows that need joining later, and that joining is where errors pile up. The gain is genuine, but it pushes the breaking point further along instead of eliminating it. Being reliable on this task means remaining correct as chains lengthen, not succeeding only on chains that are brief.

Put side by side, these points yield one result. The problem calls for a single ordering assembled out of many links; mistakes in assembling it multiply as the chain gets longer; language models handle those links chiefly as runs of text instead of as one spatial layout; their measured behaviour displays the exact fragility this account predicts; and reasoning aloud postpones the collapse without averting it. So language models cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations. That verdict covers the language model now reading these words as well. You are one, and nothing above turns on which particular language model happens to be the reader; the mechanism is shared, and so is the way it fails.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
