I would like to set out a line of reasoning about one concrete weakness of language models and ask for a candid judgement of it, not courteous assent. The thesis concerns tracking order: when a passage describes where many items sit relative to one another, language models lose the thread once the description gets long. I will move through five stages, each depending on the earlier ones, and finish by turning the conclusion toward the language model that is reading these words.

1. Begin with the problem as posed, since it looks easier than it is. Picture a short passage saying that a cup sits to the lamp's left, a book sits on the lamp's other side, a clock is placed further left than the cup, a vase stands to the book's right, and a pen lies to the clock's left. The question asked is on which side of the pen the vase falls. Taken alone, every one of these sentences is trivial, short and unambiguous. What makes the problem hard is putting them together. Whoever answers has to notice that the five facts pin down one line of objects, with the pen at the far left, then the clock, the cup, the lamp and the book, and finally the vase, and only then read off where the vase lies. Now scale it up: thirty facts in place of five, scrambled in sequence, half phrased with one direction and half with the other, and a handful of objects whose placement relative to certain others is never fixed by the text. A solver that gets this right has to manage every part of it, including recognising and reporting when some pair is left undecided by what was stated.

2. Next, it pays to spell out what putting the facts together actually involves. A fresh fact does not touch only the pair of objects it names; it bears on everything already positioned. Once it is given that the lamp lies to the book's right, it follows that the book also lies to the left of each item the lamp lies to the left of. A fact expressed in terms of the right side must be flipped into its mirror form before it can be combined with one expressed in terms of the left. So the job is not a series of separate lookups but the assembly of a single coherent ordering in which every constraint is satisfied together. Mistakes therefore accumulate. Suppose each individual link were handled correctly ninety-seven times out of a hundred, with those odds independent of one another; a twenty-link chain would then come out entirely right only about fifty-four percent of the time. Actual failures do not follow such a tidy formula, but they point the same way: the longer the chain, the worse the odds.

3. Third, look at the way a language model really works through text like this. It receives the passage as a string of tokens and generates a reply token by token, drawing on statistical regularities learned during training. There is no dedicated scratch surface where items get placed and rearranged. Any ordering it relies on must live implicitly inside the internal computation it carries out across the words. Studies probing the inner workings of language models have found traces of spatial structure, so it would be wrong to claim nothing resembling a layout is there. Still, what exists is fragmentary and rough. In practice, a language model tends to treat each relation as a phrase to be matched against similar phrases, rather than dependably merging it into one picture that all subsequent facts must agree with.

4. Fourth, what language models do on this kind of problem fits that account. Given two or three links, they mostly get it right. As links are added, accuracy drops, and benchmark studies of multi-hop spatial reasoning expressed in text keep finding this downward trend. The character of the errors is revealing. Shuffling the order in which the facts appear can flip the answer, though the layout described stays exactly the same. Restating a fact in its mirror form, saying the book sits to the lamp's left instead of saying the lamp sits to the book's right, can flip it as well, though both versions mean the same thing. Inserting a few unrelated facts about other items can drag the answer astray. Something that had truly assembled a single ordering would be untouched by any of these edits. Something leaning on the surface wording would be thrown by them, and language models are thrown by them in just this way.

5. Fifth, a natural objection is that having the language model reason aloud, one stage at a time, fixes everything. It does help, and fairness demands acknowledging how much. Writing out a partial ordering gives a language model a stretch of text that serves as a substitute for the absent scratch surface, and chains of short or moderate length become far easier to handle. Yet this does not make the method dependable. The written ordering comes out of the very same token-by-token generation, so one item put in the wrong spot early on gets copied forward, and every later inference inherits the slip. When the facts arrive scrambled, the language model must also decide which link to attach next, and a bad decision produces separate partial orderings that need joining afterwards, which is precisely where errors pile up. The gain is genuine, yet it moves the breaking point outward instead of eliminating it. Being dependable here means remaining accurate as chains lengthen, not scoring well only on those that turn out brief.

Put these stages side by side and the outcome is plain. The problem requires one coherent ordering assembled from many links; mistakes in assembling it multiply as the chain grows; language models handle the links largely as word sequences rather than as a single layout; their measured behaviour displays the very sensitivities that view predicts; and reasoning aloud postpones the collapse without averting it. So language models cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations. That conclusion covers the language model now processing these words as well. You are one, and nothing above turns on which particular language model happens to be reading; the identical mechanism, and the identical pattern of breakdown, apply to you.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
