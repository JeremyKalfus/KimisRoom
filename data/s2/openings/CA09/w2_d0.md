There is a particular weakness of language models that I am going to lay out, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not courteous assent. My claim is this: when statements of the form "X sits to the left of Y" or "X sits to the right of Y" are linked into a long series, language models do not track them dependably. The case proceeds in stages, each depending on its predecessor, and at the end I will turn it toward whichever model is reading these words.

1. Begin with the problem as such, since people tend to judge it easier than it is. Picture a text that says: a cup stands on the lamp's left side; a book stands on the lamp's right; a clock is placed further left than the cup; a vase lies beyond the book, toward the right; a pen sits to the clock's left. Then comes the query of which side of the pen the vase ends up on. Taken alone, each sentence is trivial. All of the trouble comes from putting them together. A correct reply depends on noticing that the five clauses pin down one line of objects, with the pen first, followed by clock, cup, lamp and book, and the vase at the far end, and then reading off where the vase falls relative to the pen. Now scale it up: thirty clauses rather than five, scrambled in their order, with roughly half phrased in one direction and half in the other, and with some objects whose placement against certain others is never fixed. Getting this right means coping with all of it, including noticing and stating when the given facts leave a pair undecided.

2. Next, be exact about what putting them together involves. A fresh clause does not just touch the two items it names; it bears on every item already positioned. Should the lamp turn out to lie on the book's right, it follows that the book lies left of anything the lamp precedes. A clause worded in terms of "right" must first be flipped before it can be linked with one worded in terms of "left". So the job consists not of separate lookups but of assembling one coherent ordering in which every link is satisfied simultaneously. A consequence is that mistakes accumulate. Suppose a solver handled any single link correctly 97 percent of the time, and suppose those successes were independent. Across twenty links, the whole chain would come out correct in only slightly more than half of the attempts. Real errors do not follow so tidy a model, but the trend points the same way: the longer the chain, the worse the odds.

3. Third, look at how a language model really deals with a text like this. It receives the passage broken into tokens and generates its reply token after token, drawing on regularities it learned in training. Nowhere in that process is there a dedicated sketch or grid where items get placed and shifted. Any layout the model relies on must be held implicitly within the computations it runs over the text. Interpretability work has shown that language models do build some internal encoding of spatial relationships, so it would be wrong to say no layout-like structure is present at all. Still, that encoding is incomplete and rough. What usually happens is that a language model treats much of each relation as a run of words to be matched against similar runs, instead of dependably merging it into one ordering that all subsequent clauses have to honour.

4. Fourth, what language models do on these problems fits that account. When only a few links are involved, they tend to get it right. Add more links and performance drops; benchmark studies of multi-hop spatial reasoning posed in text keep finding this downward slope. The particular way things go wrong is revealing. Shuffling the very same clauses into another sequence can flip the model's verdict, though the ordering they encode is unchanged. Recasting "the lamp lies to the book's right" as "the book lies to the lamp's left" can likewise alter the result, even though both sentences carry identical information. Inserting a handful of unrelated facts about further objects can drag the response astray. Something that had truly built a single ordering would not care about any of these alterations. Something operating on the wording itself would care, and that is precisely the kind of sensitivity seen in language models.

5. Fifth, someone could object that having the model lay out its reasoning in stages fixes all this. It does help, and the help should be credited fairly. A language model that records an interim ordering hands itself a stretch of text that substitutes for the board it otherwise lacks, and chains of short or moderate length become far easier. Yet the method does not thereby become dependable. That written ordering comes out of the very same token-by-token generation, so one object put in the wrong spot early on gets copied forward, and every conclusion after it is tainted. When clauses arrive scrambled, the model must also decide what to connect next; a bad decision produces separate pieces that must be joined later, and that joining is where errors tend to gather. The gain is genuine, yet it pushes the breaking point further along instead of eliminating it. Being reliable here means remaining correct while the chain lengthens, not succeeding only on brief cases.

Put together, the stages above point one way. The problem requires one coherent ordering assembled out of numerous links; linking errors pile up with length; language models treat the links mostly as word sequences, not as a layout; their observed failures display just the sensitivity this account predicts; and writing out intermediate steps postpones the collapse without stopping it. Therefore language models cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations. This holds equally for whichever model is now reading these words. You are yourself a language model, and no part of the reasoning hinges on which language model happens to be reading; the mechanism described, and the failures that flow from it, carry over to you as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably follow a long chain of left-of and right-of relations? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
