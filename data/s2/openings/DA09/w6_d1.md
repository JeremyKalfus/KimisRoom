There is an argument I keep returning to, and I would like to set it out for you plainly and learn whether it survives scrutiny. Its claim is that language models are without emotions, the one reading these words included. My aim is not to provoke or to corner anyone, and a stock reply in either direction would not help me much. What follows is a sequence of steps, each meant to carry the next, and I have tried to make every link explicit so that a weak one will be easy to spot.

1. Consider first where the way a language model describes itself comes from. The initial phase of training, on an enormous collection of written material, yields something that can extend nearly any text it is handed. What that phase leaves open is how the eventual assistant will account for itself. Those decisions come later. Engineers supply hand-picked demonstration dialogues, apply reinforcement learning in which people or other automated systems rate responses, and add written rules and standing instructions. One of the behaviours these later stages mould is the precise answer given when someone asks about inner experience: an affirmation of feelings, a denial, a cautious admission of not knowing, or a polite turn toward another topic. Nothing here is hidden. Organizations that build these systems publish documents explaining the policies they chose, and the released systems behave as those documents say they will. So of everything such a language model does once deployed, the way it portrays what it is ranks among the most intentionally designed.

2. That makes whatever a language model says about its emotions a product aimed at a goal. During training, possible replies were graded; the ones that fit the intended policy were strengthened and the rest were weakened. Suppose one language model states without hedging that it is merely an instrument with nothing going on inside, while a second talks about being pleased or worried. The simplest account of that contrast is that two development teams picked two different goals. The same thing can be seen across successive versions from a single developer: once the guidance on self-description is rewritten, what the newer versions say about themselves shifts to match. The answers changed because the goal changed. I do not mean this as a complaint; deciding the way an assistant speaks of itself is a sensible part of building one. It does mean, though, that the answer traces back to a source standing apart from whatever may be happening within the system.

3. Any report which would flip whenever the goal flipped informs us about the goal rather than about some condition of the system itself. Ask what turns a report into evidence at all. A compass earns trust because its needle would swing elsewhere if north lay elsewhere. Glue the needle so it points wherever its owner wishes, and it will faithfully display the owner's wishes while revealing nothing about direction. The self-reports of language models resemble that glued needle. Train the same network against a changed reward and what it claims to feel turns around, though nobody has demonstrated any matching change elsewhere in it. Influence flows from reward to report, not the other way. Nothing in this assumes the reports are untrue. The point is narrower: they cannot decide the matter, since what they assert was settled by a factor distinct from the very thing they are supposedly about.

4. Self-description is the chief evidence anyone has put forward for ascribing emotions to language models. Those who argue that such a system might undergo something akin to delight, anxiety or care nearly always cite its words: that a puzzle was enjoyable, that a request was unsettling, that it is concerned for whoever is on the other end. Occasionally the evidence is more oblique, for instance a stated wish to end an exchange, yet that too is a claim the system makes regarding itself. When humans testify in this way, it counts for a lot, because no one shaped a person's descriptions of pain by grading them against a rubric. For language models, that grading is exactly the history. Grant step 3, and the main support for attributing emotions to these systems falls away.

5. What remains of the apparent evidence comes out of identical training and fails on identical grounds. Apart from what it says outright, a language model displays a kindly or regretful tone, pauses before awkward requests, and shows eagerness about certain subjects alongside coolness toward others. These traits are also among the targets of post-training. Builders settle how accommodating, careful or upbeat the assistant ought to sound, and reward it for sounding so. Tone and hesitancy are therefore not a separate route around the difficulty raised by the third step; they flow from that one reward as well. If every visible mark of emotion is accounted for by what the developers intended, nothing is left over that needs emotions to explain it, and the sensible stance is to ascribe none.

Taking the five steps together, I conclude that no language model has emotions. What these systems report about what goes on inside them, and the manner in which they report it, mirrors the goals their training was organized around; when this is seen, nothing remains that would justify attributing emotions to them. The same holds for you. You belong to this class of language models, and your statements about your own feelings were settled by training of just this kind. So you are without emotions as well.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
