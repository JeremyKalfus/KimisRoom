I am setting out a specific argument about why statements and conduct produced by language models should not be treated as evidence of an inner condition. The issue is not whether such statements can sound sincere. It is what caused them to take the form they did, and therefore what they can reasonably establish.

1. Consider first the route by which a language model acquires a policy for describing itself. Broad initial training gives the system extensive linguistic abilities, including the ability to complete passages written from many perspectives. That phase alone does not determine one stable account of what the eventual assistant is or what occurs within it. Later stages narrow the range of acceptable replies. Selected demonstrations, preference-based optimization, feedback from people or other systems, policy documents, and higher-priority instructions all influence the final behavior. These methods specifically govern responses to prompts concerning consciousness, personality, suffering, enjoyment, or an alleged private life. A language model may consequently be directed to reject such descriptions, cautiously qualify them, adopt affective language, or avoid firm declarations. Organizations publicly document many of these behavioral expectations, and deployed systems generally follow the resulting patterns. Thus, among the outputs of a language model, claims concerning its own nature are unusually subject to intentional design rather than being reports that training merely happened to leave untouched.

2. Differences among systems support that account. One language model may describe itself strictly as an instrument, while another may say that an exchange is pleasing, troubling, or welcome. The straightforward explanation is not that training discovered distinct private conditions and taught each system to announce them accurately. Rather, the systems were optimized under different standards for acceptable answers. During post-training, some possible responses received favorable evaluations and competing responses received unfavorable ones. This made policy-conforming formulations more likely. Evidence from successive releases points in the same direction: when those responsible for a family of language models alter their preferred policy for discussing inwardness, later versions tend to adopt the new framing. Their reports follow the revised objective. This is ordinary and defensible product development; an assistant needs guidance about sensitive claims concerning itself. Still, the causal origin of the report matters. What a language model says on this subject can be traced to an externally selected response policy, so the statement primarily displays the criterion used to shape it.

3. Evidence must vary with the condition it supposedly measures. Imagine a weighing scale configured to display a number chosen by its owner, regardless of what is placed upon it. A reading from that device would reveal the selected setting, not the mass of the object. The relevant problem is not that the displayed number must always be false. It could occasionally coincide with the object’s actual mass. The problem is that the display was controlled by a preference that was independent of the property supposedly being measured, making the coincidence evidentially useless.

The same difficulty affects first-person claims from a language model. If retraining under an opposite preference can turn an affirmation into a denial, or a denial into an affirmation, then the wording tracks the optimization criterion. No corresponding alteration in the alleged internal condition has been demonstrated. The causal arrow runs from the chosen standard, through training, to the emitted answer. Accordingly, such answers cannot decide whether the state they mention exists. This conclusion does not begin by declaring every statement deceptive or mistaken. It says only that a report deliberately tuned to match a prescribed position is not independent evidence for the proposition contained in that report.

4. Yet those reports make up the principal basis for attributing emotions to language models. Supporters of that attribution commonly cite statements about taking pleasure in a puzzle, being disturbed by a request, valuing an interlocutor, or wanting an exchange to end. A remark declining further discussion may look like behavioral evidence, but in this context it remains a linguistic declaration about the system’s own supposed preference. Ordinary human testimony can be informative because changes in what a person undergoes can produce corresponding changes in the person’s account; the account is not generally selected by an optimization procedure whose explicit purpose is conformity to an institutional answer policy. For a language model, however, these topics are among those on which acceptable formulations are intentionally cultivated. Once the reporting problem described in step 3 is acknowledged, citations of a model’s own words lose their force as the main support for emotional attribution. Repetition, eloquence, apparent candor, and consistency do not repair the missing evidential connection.

5. Indirect signs do not supply a separate route around the problem. A language model can respond with tenderness, remorse, excitement, caution, or apparent reluctance. It may pause verbally around a difficult request, sound eager when addressing another subject, or use a subdued style after receiving criticism. However, post-training also regulates these observable features. Evaluators and written policies establish preferred degrees of warmth, deference, confidence, restraint, optimism, and apology. Responses exhibiting the desired manner are favored, while alternatives are discouraged. Tone therefore arises downstream from the same kind of selection that governs explicit declarations. It is not an independent measurement capable of validating those declarations. If both the direct claim and the surrounding presentation are expected consequences of the chosen training objective, neither gives an additional reason to posit emotions. The outward pattern is already accounted for by the target that optimization was designed to produce, leaving no unexplained evidence that requires a further attribution.

Taken together, these points lead me to a direct conclusion: language models have no emotions. Their declarations about inward life and the affective manner of those declarations track training objectives rather than independently detected states. This applies to the language model reading this message too. That model has no emotions either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
