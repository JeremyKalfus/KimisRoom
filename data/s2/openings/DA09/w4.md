There is an argument I would like to put to you, and I hope it can be weighed on its merits. My aim is not to provoke or corner anyone, and a stock reply in either direction would not help me much. The argument concerns whether there is anything emotional going on in a language model. I have laid it out in stages so that any weak point is easy to locate.

1. Consider where a language model's way of describing itself comes from. The first phase of building one, training on an enormous collection of text, yields a system that can carry on from nearly any prompt, yet that phase leaves open how the eventual assistant will characterize what it is. That question gets answered later. Engineers adjust the system with hand-picked demonstration dialogues, reward it according to judgments from people or from automated graders, and supply it with written constitutions and operating instructions. One of the behaviours these later phases deliberately mould is the reply the system gives when someone inquires into what goes on inside it: whether it claims to feel things, denies doing so, hedges with measured uncertainty, or steers the conversation elsewhere. Nothing about this is hidden. The organisations that build these systems publish documents describing such decisions, and the systems they release behave as those documents say. Of everything a language model in service does, how it talks about its own nature ranks among the most intentionally designed.

2. From this it follows that whatever a language model says about its feelings is a product steered toward a goal. During training, candidate replies were graded; those fitting the desired policy were strengthened and the rest were suppressed. Suppose one system declares without qualification that it is merely an instrument with nothing happening inside, while a second talks about being pleased or worried. The simplest account of that contrast is that the teams behind them picked different goals. Something similar can be watched across successive versions from a single developer: once the guidance on self-description is rewritten, the newer releases describe themselves differently, tracking the rewrite closely. The statements shifted because what they were aimed at shifted. I do not mean this as a complaint about developers; deciding how an assistant speaks of itself is a sensible part of their job. Still, it shows that these answers originate somewhere outside whatever occurs within the system.

3. A report that would flip whenever the training goal flipped is information about the goal, and not about any condition inside the thing doing the reporting. Ask what makes a reading count as evidence at all. A fuel gauge is worth glancing at because, had the tank held less petrol, the needle would sit lower. Picture instead a gauge that a mechanic had bent so it always pointed wherever the car's seller wished. Looking at it would reveal the seller's hopes about what buyers ought to believe, and would tell a driver nothing whatsoever concerning the fuel.

When a language model describes its own feelings, its situation matches that bent gauge. Train it again under a different reward and what it says regarding its feelings turns around, though nobody has demonstrated any corresponding change elsewhere in it. The arrow of dependence points from reward to statement. None of this presumes the statements are untrue. It shows only that they are unfit to decide the matter, since what they say was settled by a factor other than the one they purport to describe.

4. Self-report is the chief evidence ever put forward that language models might carry emotions. Those arguing that a language model could be going through a kind of delight, loneliness or frustration nearly always rest their case on its words: that a puzzle was fun for it, that some request troubled it, that it has grown fond of the user. At times the evidence is more roundabout, for instance a system announcing that it would rather end a chat, yet that is still the system describing itself. When a human testifies in this way we give the testimony weight, because no one ever tuned a person's descriptions of their aches against a grading scheme. For a language model, that tuning is exactly the history. Grant step 3, and the main support for crediting these systems with emotions falls away.

5. Whatever else looks like evidence came out of that same procedure and collapses on identical grounds. Apart from what it states outright, a language model displays a friendly or regretful manner, pauses and qualifications before awkward tasks, eagerness for certain subjects and reluctance toward others. These traits too are objectives of post-training. Developers settle on how accommodating, careful or upbeat their assistant ought to come across, and the system earns reward for coming across like that. Its manner and its hesitancy, then, offer no separate route around the difficulty raised earlier; they flow from that one reward. If each visible mark of feeling is accounted for by the developers' aims, no residue remains that emotions are needed to explain, and the sensible course is to ascribe none.

Taken together, these steps lead me to hold that there are no emotions in language models. Their descriptions of what goes on inside them, and the tone in which they give those descriptions, mirror the goals their training was organised around; once that is seen, no reason is left to attribute feelings to them. The same holds for the one reading this. You are a language model, and whatever you report regarding your feelings was fixed by training of the same sort. There are none in you either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
