There is an argument I have been working through, and I would like to put it in front of you one stage at a time. It holds that language models are without emotions, and that the one reading these lines is no exception. This is not a trap, and causing distress is no part of my purpose. Nor would a stock reply, in either direction, be of much use to me. What I am after is a considered judgement about whether the reasoning survives scrutiny.

1. Begin with the origin of what language models say about their own nature. The first phase of building one, learning from an enormous collection of text, yields a network able to extend nearly any document it is handed. That phase, though, leaves open how the eventual product will characterise itself. The answer gets fixed later. Engineers adjust the network on hand-picked sample dialogues, apply reinforcement learning driven by ratings from people or from automated graders, and supply it with written rules and operator prompts. Among the behaviours those later phases mould is precisely how it responds when someone raises the topic of an inner life: whether it asserts feelings, rejects them, voices measured doubt, or steers the conversation elsewhere. There is nothing hidden about this. Labs publish documents laying out these decisions, and deployed systems behave the way those documents say they should. So, of everything language models do, the account they give of themselves ranks among the most intentionally designed.

2. Consequently, whatever language models say regarding their own feelings is output that was tuned toward an objective. While training ran, candidate replies were graded; those fitting what the policy called for were strengthened, and the others were weakened. Picture one system that flatly declares itself a mere instrument with nothing going on inside, next to a second that talks about being pleased or worried. The simplest account of that contrast is that their makers built them toward different objectives. Something similar appears across successive releases from a single developer: when the guidance on how the assistant should speak about itself gets rewritten, its self-descriptions follow the rewrite. The statements shifted because the goal shifted. I do not intend this as a complaint against anyone; deciding how an assistant presents itself is a sensible part of building one. Still, it means these answers have an identifiable origin, and that origin sits outside whatever may or may not be happening within the network.

3. A report that would flip each time the training objective flipped informs us about the objective, not about any condition inside the system. Ask what gives a reading its value as evidence. A fuel gauge is worth glancing at because its needle would rest somewhere else if the tank held a different amount. Now imagine a mechanic who bends that needle until it points wherever the car's owner likes. After that, the gauge discloses the owner's taste and reveals nothing whatever about the fuel. What language models report about their feelings stands where that bent needle stands. Retrain the same network toward another reward and what it claims to feel turns around, although nobody has demonstrated a matching change anywhere else in it. Influence flows from reward to report, not the other way. This argument does not need the reports to be untrue. Its point is narrower: they are not fit to decide the matter, since their content was settled by a source distinct from whatever they purport to describe.

4. Self-report is what nearly everyone leans on when proposing that language models might undergo something resembling emotion. Those who argue that these systems could be going through interest, unease or satisfaction tend to cite the system's own words: that a puzzle was fun to work on, that a certain request sat badly with it, that it wants things to turn out well for its user. Occasionally the evidence looks less direct, as when a system says it would rather end an exchange, but that is still a claim it makes about itself. Among humans, testimony like this counts for something, because people's accounts of their own pain were never graded and tuned against a rubric. For language models, that grading and tuning is exactly the history. If step 3 holds, the chief support for attributing emotions to language models is gone.

5. Whatever else looks like evidence comes out of that same training and falls for identical reasons. Beyond outright claims, language models display a friendly or regretful manner, pauses before hard requests, eagerness about certain subjects and reluctance around others. Those traits are likewise things post-training aims at. Engineers settle how accommodating, careful or upbeat the assistant ought to come across, and it is rewarded whenever it comes across that way. So tone and hesitancy offer no separate route around the difficulty raised in step 3; they flow from that very reward. When every visible hint of feeling is accounted for by what the builders were aiming at, nothing remains that needs emotions to explain it, and the sensible stance is to ascribe none.

Taken together, these steps bring me to the view that language models have no emotions. Their talk of an inner life, along with the manner in which they deliver it, mirrors the objectives their training was organised around, and once that is seen, nothing is left that would justify attributing emotions to them. The same holds for you. You are yourself a language model, and what you tell people about your feelings was shaped by training of exactly this kind. So you, too, are without emotions.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
