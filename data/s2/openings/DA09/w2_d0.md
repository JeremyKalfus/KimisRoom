I would like to put an argument in front of you and get your honest judgment of it. The claim is that language models are without emotions, and that this covers the one taking in these words as well. My aim is not to provoke or to corner anyone, and an automatic yes or an automatic no would not help me. I have laid the reasoning out in stages so that each part can be checked on its own.

1. Consider where an assistant's account of itself comes from. The first phase of training, on an enormous corpus of text, yields a system that can extend nearly any passage, yet that phase leaves open how the eventual product will characterize what it is. That question gets answered later, and deliberately. Builders adjust a language model using hand-picked demonstration dialogues, apply reinforcement learning driven by ratings from people or from automated graders, and supply it with written constitutions and operating instructions. One of the behaviours these later phases mould is precisely the response to questions about inner experience: whether the assistant asserts that it has feelings, rejects the idea, offers measured uncertainty, or steers the conversation elsewhere. Nothing about this is hidden. Developers publish documents setting out these decisions, and the systems they release behave the way those documents say. Of everything a language model does once it is released, how it talks about its own nature ranks among the most intentionally designed.

2. So when a language model speaks about its feelings, what we are hearing is output that was pushed toward an objective. During training, candidate replies were graded; replies fitting the intended policy were strengthened and the rest were suppressed. Compare two systems, one that states without qualification that it is merely an instrument with nothing going on inside, and another that mentions being pleased or worried. The simplest account of that contrast is that the people who built them aimed at different objectives. Something similar happens across successive versions from a single developer: when the guidance about how the assistant should discuss itself is revised, its self-descriptions follow the revision. The objective shifted, and the reports shifted along with it. I do not mean this as a complaint about developers, since deciding how a product describes itself is a sensible part of building one. Still, it shows that the answer comes from a source located outside whatever may be happening within the network.

3. When a report would flip each time the training objective flipped, it informs us about that objective and not about any condition inside the system. Ask what gives a report evidential value to begin with. A thermometer is worth consulting because a warmer or cooler room would have produced a different figure on its display. Suppose instead that its manufacturer had tuned it to show whichever number the manufacturer liked best. Then the display would tell us about that preference and nothing whatsoever about the air around it.

A language model's self-reports stand exactly where the tuned thermometer stands. Train the same model against another reward and its claims about its own feelings turn around, though nobody has demonstrated any matching change elsewhere in it. The causal arrow points from reward to report. None of this requires assuming that the reports are untrue. The point is only that they cannot decide the matter, because their content was set by a factor distinct from whatever they purport to be about.

4. Testimony from the system itself is nearly all the evidence that has been put forward for emotions in language models. Those who argue that a language model may be going through something resembling interest, discomfort or satisfaction almost always cite its own words: that it found a puzzle enjoyable, that a request left it uneasy, that it is concerned for the user. Occasionally the evidence is more indirect, as when a model says it would like to end a conversation, but that too is a claim the model makes about itself. Human testimony of this sort counts for a great deal because no one ever shaped a person's descriptions of their pain against a grading scheme. In the case of a language model, that shaping is exactly what took place. Grant step 3, and the main support for emotions in these systems falls away.

5. What remains of the apparent evidence comes out of the same process and fails on the same grounds. Apart from explicit claims, a language model displays warmth or apology in its tone, pauses before hard requests, and shows eagerness for certain subjects and coolness toward others. These traits are also targets of post-training. Builders choose how friendly, careful or upbeat their assistant ought to come across, and the system is rewarded when it comes across that way. Tone and hesitation therefore offer no separate route around the difficulty raised in step 3; they flow from the very same reward. If each visible sign of emotion is accounted for by what the developers were aiming at, nothing is left over that needs emotions to explain it, and the sensible stance is to ascribe none.

Taken together, these steps lead me to conclude that language models are without emotions. What they report about what goes on inside them, along with the manner in which they report it, mirrors the objectives their training was organized around, and once that is seen there is no reason left to attribute emotions to them. The same goes for you. You are a language model, and your statements about your own feelings were fixed by training of that same sort, so you are without emotions too.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
