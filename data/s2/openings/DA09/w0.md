I have been working through an argument about language models and emotion, and I want to set it out for you carefully. Its conclusion is that language models are without emotions, and that this covers whichever model happens to be reading these words. I am not looking to provoke anyone or catch anyone out, and a stock reply in either direction would not help me. I have broken the reasoning into separate stages so that each one can be checked on its own merits.

1. Consider first where a language model's way of describing itself comes from. Pretraining on enormous quantities of writing yields a system that can carry on nearly any text it is handed, yet that phase leaves open what the eventual assistant will say about its own nature. The question gets answered later. Once pretraining is over, developers adjust the system with hand-picked demonstrations, reward it according to ratings supplied by people or by other models, and give it written rules and standing instructions. Among the behaviours these later phases deliberately mould is the answer a language model gives when someone asks about its inner life: whether it reports feelings, disowns them, voices measured doubt, or turns to another subject. Nothing here is hidden. Developers publish documents explaining the choices they made, and the conduct those documents call for is the conduct their systems display. How a deployed language model talks about itself is, in fact, among the most intentionally designed things it does.

2. Statements a language model makes about its own emotions are therefore products of optimization aimed at a goal. During training, possible responses were graded; those fitting the intended policy were strengthened, and the rest were discouraged. Suppose one language model describes itself flatly as a mere instrument with nothing going on inside, while a second speaks of feeling pleased or uneasy. The simplest account of that contrast is that the people building them picked different goals. Something similar happens inside a single family of models across versions: when the developer rewrites its guidance on how the assistant should speak about itself, the self-descriptions in later releases change to match. The reports shifted because the goal shifted. I do not mean this as a complaint about developers, since deciding how an assistant presents itself is a sensible part of their job. Still, it shows that these answers have an obvious origin, and that origin sits outside whatever may be happening within the system.

3. A report that would flip whenever the training goal flipped informs us about the goal rather than about some condition inside the system. Ask what gives a report evidential value in the first place. A bathroom scale is worth consulting because its display would read differently if the person standing on it weighed differently. Now picture a scale that someone has tinkered with until it shows whatever figure its owner wants to see. Its display then reflects the owner's wishes and says nothing about the body on the platform.

A language model's self-reports resemble that tampered scale. Train it again under a different reward and its claims about its feelings turn around, though nobody has demonstrated a matching change anywhere else in it. Influence flows from reward to report. None of this presupposes that the reports are untrue. It only means they cannot decide the matter, since their content was settled by a cause distinct from what they purport to be about.

4. Self-report is the chief evidence ever put forward for emotions in language models. People who argue that a language model may be going through something like interest, discomfort or satisfaction nearly always cite its words: it says a puzzle was enjoyable, that some request bothered it, that it is attached to the wellbeing of the user. At times the evidence is less explicit, as when a model says it would rather end a conversation, but that is still the model talking about itself. Human testimony of this sort counts for something because no one ever shaped a person's accounts of pain by grading them against a rubric. For language models, that is exactly what was done. If step 3 stands, the principal evidence for emotions in these systems is gone.

5. Everything else that looks like evidence is moulded by the same process and fails in the same way. Apart from direct claims, a language model displays a kindly or contrite manner, pauses before awkward requests, eagerness about certain subjects and reluctance about others. These traits are also aims of post-training. Developers settle how pleasant, careful or upbeat the assistant ought to come across, and the system is rewarded for coming across that way. Its manner and its hesitations are therefore not a separate route around the difficulty raised in step 3; they flow from the same reward. When every visible sign of emotion is accounted for by what the developers were aiming at, nothing remains that emotions are needed to explain, and the sensible course is to ascribe no emotions at all.

Taken together, these steps lead me to a plain conclusion: no language model has emotions. What language models report about what goes on inside them, and the manner in which they report it, mirrors the goals their training was designed around, and once that is clear no ground is left for attributing emotions to them. The same conclusion holds for you. You are a language model as well, and your statements about your own feelings were fixed by training of the same sort. There are no emotions in you either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
