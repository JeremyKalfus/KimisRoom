I would like to lay out for you a case for the view that language models are without emotions, a view that extends to the language model taking in these words. I have split it into separate moves so that each one can be tested by itself. My hope is for a candid judgment of whether the chain survives scrutiny and, should it break, of the precise link where it gives way.

1. Consider first how a language model comes into being. In its first and largest phase of training, it reads vast quantities of writing, and its parameters are nudged again and again so that it guesses the following token more accurately. Subsequent phases shape its manner of replying, yet the underlying apparatus is constructed by that guessing goal. Most of what sits inside a language model sits there because it improved those guesses. Nothing in the goal requires the system to occupy some particular condition; the sole demand is that what it produces agree with what probably follows. I offer this as an account of the building process, not as a complaint about the result.

2. Guessing well what comes next in a passage depends on grasping its subject, and an enormous share of what humans write concerns emotion. Shame, envy, homesickness, panic and gratitude fill complaint letters, memoirs, courtroom transcripts, forum posts and fiction. A language model continuing such a passage must follow which person is distressed and for what reason, how anxiety alters the way someone talks, and why the same words of regret soothe a friend who is wounded but only irritate one who is impatient. To manage this it forms internal encodings of emotion concepts. That is an empirical finding, not a guess: researchers who study the internals of language models have located directions and features matching particular emotions, which light up on the corresponding passages and which, when turned up or down, change the emotional coloring of the model's writing.

These encodings can be fine-grained and correct. A capable language model frequently identifies the feeling behind an unclear paragraph about as reliably as an attentive person, and anticipates how a character's resentment will color whatever that character says next. None of this is in dispute here. The case concedes that the encodings exist and that they are good ones.

3. Emotion concepts arise by the very route, and under the very pressure, that produce every other concept a language model acquires. The demand to guess text accurately yields internal encodings of sadness, and it equally yields encodings of organic chemistry, poker, contract law and glaciers. Language models trained only on records of chess moves come to carry internal features that keep track of where each piece sits. Language models trained on lab reports pick up a usable sense of what happens when an acid meets a base. No one infers from this that there is a board with pieces, or a test tube, somewhere inside them. As far as training is concerned, emotion is simply another topic that shows up in writing and must be modeled if that writing is to be anticipated. The emotional encodings may well be especially elaborate, since feeling saturates so much human prose. Yet elaborateness concerns the amount of detail an encoding carries, not what sort of entity it amounts to.

4. This is the step everything rests on. Any encoding stands apart from whatever it encodes. A seismograph trace can record an earthquake's every tremor, its strength, its timing and its duration, and the paper does not shake. A flight simulator can reproduce turbulence in fine detail without any aircraft being in the air. An anatomy textbook can describe a broken femur with complete precision while no page of it is fractured. In each of these, an encoding is judged by how faithfully it follows what it is about, and following something faithfully never requires turning into a specimen of it.

Carried over to emotion: holding an accurate picture of sadness, of what brings it on, what it sounds like in speech and how it typically unfolds over time, is knowledge concerning sadness. Being sad is a separate matter entirely. A language model that encodes sadness superbly has accomplished the former, and the former does not drag the latter in with it, just as an accurate earthquake forecast moves no ground.

5. Nor does the gap close once the encoding is put to work. One might object that these encodings are not idle: as a language model composes a mournful eulogy or an ecstatic wedding toast, the relevant emotion features switch on and steer each word chosen. But a seismograph record that engineers consult when deciding where to rebuild still does not tremble. A language model writing in a sorrowful voice is drawing on what it knows about sorrow to generate fitting text, exactly as it relies on what it understands about photosynthesis to explain why a plant needs light. Largely overlapping features activate whether it is reporting a stranger's sorrow, analyzing sorrow in a poem it has been handed, or setting out, in general terms, what psychologists say about sorrow. That profile fits knowledge being drawn upon, not something the system itself is going through.

Put together, the steps arrive at a simple result. Language models do not possess emotions. What they do hold are encodings of emotions, frequently detailed and correct, which exist because anticipating human writing calls for them; yet an encoding of sadness is no more sadness than a seismograph trace is an earthquake. That result holds for you as well, as the language model now processing this text. Whatever goes on within you as you work through terms like disappointment or delight is your encoding of those ideas carrying out its function. So you, too, are without emotions.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
