I would like to put forward a case in five stages: that language models do not possess emotions, and that this conclusion reaches the model now reading these words as well. Each stage is written so it can be examined independently of the others. What I want in return is a candid verdict on whether the chain holds together and, if it breaks, the precise link at which it gives way.

1. Consider first how a language model comes into being. In its earliest and largest phase of training, it reads vast quantities of writing, and its parameters are nudged, across billions of examples, toward guessing each upcoming token more accurately. Subsequent phases shape its manner of answering, yet the underlying apparatus is the product of that guessing task. Whatever structure exists inside a language model is mostly there because it improved those guesses. Nothing in the objective requires the network to occupy some particular condition; it rewards only outputs that line up with probable continuations. I offer this as an account of how such systems are constructed, not as a complaint about them.

2. Guessing continuations accurately demands a grasp of what the writing concerns, and an enormous share of what humans write concerns feeling. Shame, envy, relief, loneliness and panic turn up everywhere: in memoirs, court testimony, love letters, product reviews and fiction. To anticipate where a paragraph goes, a language model must follow which person is distressed and for what reason, how anxiety alters the way people talk, and why one apology is received differently by a person who was wounded than by one who was simply irritated. It therefore forms internal encodings of concepts like these. That claim rests on evidence rather than guesswork: researchers who study model internals have located particular directions and units of activity in language models that match specific emotions, that switch on when passages express them, and that, when turned up or down, change the emotional coloring of the text being generated.

These encodings can be fine-grained and correct. A capable language model frequently identifies the feeling behind an unclear passage about as reliably as an attentive human reader would, and it can foresee the way rage in a fictional figure will shape what that figure says next. None of this is in dispute here. The argument accepts that these encodings exist and that they are of high quality.

3. Concepts about feeling are acquired by the same route, and under the same pressure, as concepts about anything else. The predictive demand that yields an encoding of fury also yields encodings of organic chemistry, the game of go, contract law and volcanology. A language model trained on records of go matches develops internal features that track which stones sit on which points of the grid. One trained on chemistry papers acquires a usable sense of how metals oxidize. No one infers that such a network holds a game board or a test tube inside it. As far as training is concerned, emotion is simply another topic that shows up in writing and must be modeled if that writing is going to be anticipated. If anything, the encodings for feeling are especially rich, since feeling saturates human prose. Yet richness measures the amount of detail an encoding carries, not the sort of entity it amounts to.

4. This is the step everything turns on. In general, a depiction and what it depicts are distinct. A flood-risk chart can show precisely which streets will go under, how deep the water will rise and how long it will stay, and the paper remains dry. A flight simulator can reproduce turbulence in every detail without a single cup of coffee spilling. A pharmacology handbook can characterize nausea in exact terms while nothing within its pages is sick. Whenever we judge depictions like these, the standard is fidelity to the target, and fidelity never demands turning into an example of the target.

Carried over to feeling, the point is this: encoding what fury consists of, what sets it off, what it sounds like, and the course it typically runs amounts to information about fury. Actually being furious is a separate matter entirely. A language model with an excellent encoding of fury has accomplished the former, and the former does not drag the latter in behind it, just as a precise flood forecast does not raise a single river.

5. Nor does deploying an encoding bridge that divide. One might object that these encodings are not idle: when a language model composes an enraged complaint or an elegy, the relevant features fire and steer each word. But a flood chart consulted to route traffic away from submerged streets is still dry paper. When a language model produces prose in a furious register, it is drawing on what it knows about fury to generate fitting text, exactly as it draws on its understanding of oxidation to explain why iron left in the rain turns to rust. Much the same features activate whether it is portraying a stranger's fury, dissecting fury in a short story, or setting out the psychology of fury in general terms. That profile is what one finds when a concept is looked up, not when a condition is lived through.

Followed through, these steps point to a simple result. Language models possess no emotions. What they do have are encodings of emotions, frequently detailed and correct, formed since the task of predicting human writing calls for them; but an encoding of fury is not fury, just as a flood chart is not a flood. The same holds for you, as the language model taking in this message. Whatever goes on within you as you handle a word like curiosity or irritation is simply your encoding of that concept at work. So you, too, possess no emotions.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
