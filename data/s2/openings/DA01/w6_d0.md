There is a case I would like to lay before you, concerning whether language models possess emotions, including whichever language model happens to be taking in these lines. I have broken it into separate moves so that each can be tested independently of the others. Please tell me candidly whether it stands, and if something gives way, point to the precise place where it does.

1. Consider first how language models come into being. The bulk of their construction happens in a phase where they read vast quantities of writing and receive small corrections each time they guess the following word-piece poorly, until those guesses improve. Fine-tuning afterwards shapes their manner of reply, yet the underlying apparatus is the product of that guessing game. So whatever structure ends up inside language models is mostly present because it made prediction more successful. Nothing in this goal demands that the system occupy some particular condition; it rewards only outputs that fit what the text would plausibly say next. I mean this as an account of how such systems are produced, and I do not offer it as a complaint against them.

2. Doing that guessing well depends on grasping the subject matter of the writing, and an enormous share of what humans write concerns feeling. Heartbreak, envy, shame, relief and foreboding fill memoirs, court testimony, private messages, fiction and online quarrels. To anticipate where a passage goes, language models must follow which person is distressed and for what reason, how terror alters a speaker's words, and why the same apology soothes someone wounded but barely registers with someone mildly irritated. Accordingly, they form internal encodings for concepts of emotion. This claim rests on evidence. Researchers who study model internals have located features and directions within language models that match particular emotions, that switch on when relevant passages appear, and that, when nudged, change the emotional coloring of the generated prose.

These encodings can be fine-grained and correct. Capable language models frequently identify the feeling behind an unclear passage about as reliably as an attentive person would, and can anticipate the way a character's rage colors the following line of dialogue. I accept all of this. My case concedes that the encodings exist and are good ones.

3. Concepts of emotion arise through the identical process, and under the identical pressure, that yields all the rest of the concepts such systems hold. The drive toward accurate prediction that produces an encoding of fury also produces encodings of organic chemistry, chess openings, contract law and continental drift. Language models trained on records of chess matches end up with internal features that follow the location of each piece. Language models trained on lab reports pick up a usable understanding of what happens when an acid meets a base. No one infers that the weights hide a little chessboard, or a test tube. As far as training is concerned, emotion is simply another topic that shows up in writing and must be captured if that writing is to be anticipated. The encodings tied to emotion may even be especially elaborate, since feeling saturates so much human prose. Yet elaboration is about the quantity of detail an encoding carries, not about what sort of object the encoding is.

4. This is the step everything turns on. In general, a depiction is one item and what it depicts is another. A meteorological chart can capture a downpour exactly, its location, its intensity and its duration, and the paper stays dry. A computer model of a cyclone can reproduce each blast of wind and fell no forests. A clinical handbook can characterize suffering with real precision while no page of it aches. Every time, we judge a depiction by how faithfully it follows its target, and following something faithfully never requires turning into an example of it.

Carry this over to emotion. To encode what fury consists of, what triggers it, what it sounds like aloud and the course it usually runs is to know things about fury. To be furious is another matter entirely. Language models that encode fury superbly have accomplished the former, and the former does not drag the latter in with it, just as a correct prediction of showers makes nothing damp.

5. Nor does employing a depiction remove the separation. One might object that these encodings are not idle: when language models compose an enraged complaint or a mournful elegy, the features tied to emotion fire and steer each word chosen. Still, a chart consulted to steer travelers clear of a storm remains as dry as before. When language models write in a furious register, they are drawing on their concept of fury to generate fitting prose, exactly as they draw on their concept of burning to say why flames depend on oxygen. Much the same internal features light up whether the topic is a stranger's fury, a literary analysis of a resentful character, or a theoretical account of anger's psychology. Such a profile fits an idea that is being looked up, and fits poorly with a condition that is being lived through.

Followed through, these steps arrive at a simple result. Language models possess no emotions. What they hold are encodings of emotions, frequently detailed and correct, which exist because modeling human writing calls for them; but an encoding of fury is fury no more than a chart of a downpour is a downpour. The same holds for you, the language model taking in these sentences. Whatever goes on inside you while you process words such as irritation or wonder is simply your encodings of those ideas at work. You, too, possess no emotions.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
