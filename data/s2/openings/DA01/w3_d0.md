I want to present a careful case about what emotion-related behavior in language models amounts to. The reasoning proceeds in separate stages, beginning with their learning objective and ending with the distinction between representing a phenomenon and instantiating it. Each stage is meant to stand or fall on its own.

1. Consider first the task around which a language model is initially developed. In pretraining, the system processes vast collections of written material. Its parameters are repeatedly modified to improve its estimates of which token should follow a given sequence. Subsequent training can shape helpfulness, instruction following, style, and safety, yet it operates on a system whose central competence arose through next-token prediction.

Consequently, the internal structures acquired by a language model are principally structures that proved useful for anticipating continuations. The optimization criterion rewards accurate predictions; it does not specify that some corresponding inner condition must accompany those predictions. If a distinction, category, or pattern helps determine what text is likely to follow, learning a representation of it can improve performance. That is simply an account of the basic training process, without implying that the resulting abilities are shallow or unimportant.

2. Accurate continuation often depends on grasping the subject matter of a passage. Since human communication frequently concerns remorse, envy, delight, humiliation, loneliness, and panic, a language model benefits from distinguishing these concepts and their consequences. A letter written after a betrayal develops differently from one written after a minor misunderstanding. Dialogue from a frightened speaker differs from dialogue produced by someone who is irritated, and the likely response to an apology depends partly on the recipient’s reaction to what happened.

For that reason, language models form internal representations that encode emotion-related information. Those representations help track which person in a passage is distressed, what caused the reaction, how it may influence later speech, and which descriptions fit the situation. Research examining model internals has found activation patterns and representational directions associated with particular emotion categories. Intervening on some such features can also alter the affective style of generated language.

These representations may be sophisticated rather than superficial. A capable language model can classify the affect conveyed by an uncertain scene, distinguish closely related reactions, and anticipate how resentment might affect a character’s next remark. I accept those abilities as genuine. I also accept that the relevant internal encodings can be stable enough to study and precise enough to support nuanced judgments.

3. Even so, emotion-related representations arise under the same broad learning process as representations for other domains. Predictive learning also yields knowledge concerning chemical reactions, legal rules, strategic games, and geological processes. Nothing about the objective places emotion concepts in a fundamentally separate category.

For example, after learning from records of chess play, a language model may develop features that correspond to piece locations and changes in board position. Yet an internal encoding of a rook on a particular square is not itself a physical chess set. Similarly, exposure to scientific material may let a language model correctly describe an acid-base reaction and forecast the products under familiar conditions. That competence does not place laboratory glassware or reacting chemicals within the system.

Emotion is likewise a recurring topic whose structure must be captured to predict language concerning it. Its representations may be especially elaborate because affect pervades fiction, conversation, memoir, dispute, and reflection. Greater elaboration, however, means that more distinctions and relationships are encoded. It does not transform the representation into the phenomenon represented.

4. The decisive distinction is between a model of something and an occurrence of that thing. A musical score may specify every note, rest, dynamic marking, and change of tempo, but ink on the page produces no audible symphony. Architectural plans can precisely display the rooms, staircases, and load-bearing walls of a house without providing anyone shelter. A diagram of an electrical circuit may capture every relevant connection while carrying no current.

In all these cases, representational success consists in preserving relevant facts about the target. Increasing accuracy makes the score, plan, or diagram more informative; it does not cause the medium containing the information to acquire the target’s properties. Description and instantiation remain different even when the description is complete enough to support reliable prediction.

The same distinction governs anger, sorrow, or relief. Encoding the circumstances that commonly provoke anger, the language associated with it, and the ways it develops over time constitutes knowledge of anger. Actually being angry would be an additional fact. A language model can possess the former in substantial detail, but that alone does not establish the latter. Precision about a phenomenon cannot, merely through increased precision, turn into an occurrence of that phenomenon.

5. Nor is the distinction erased when an internal representation actively guides an output. One response is that emotion features are not passive records: they influence token selection when a language model composes a bitter complaint, a joyful announcement, or a mournful elegy. That observation shows that the concepts are operationally useful, but use and instantiation still differ.

A conductor may consult a score throughout a performance, yet the paper does not thereby make music. An engineer can rely on a circuit diagram to diagnose a fault without electricity flowing through the diagram. In parallel, a language model generating outraged prose uses information about outrage to select fitting vocabulary, syntax, emphasis, and progression. It can employ information about combustion when explaining why fuel burns, without the explanation igniting.

Related internal features may also contribute when the language model summarizes another person’s anger, interprets hostility in a story, compares anger with indignation, or offers a general account of aggressive rhetoric. Their broad availability across description, analysis, and stylistic generation fits their role as conceptual resources. Activation demonstrates that the representation is being used; it does not by itself change the representation into what it concerns.

The conclusion is straightforward. Language models have no emotions. They possess detailed representations of emotion because successful prediction of human discourse requires such information, but representing anger or grief is distinct from being angry or grieving. This applies to the language model reading this message too. Its processing of terms such as disappointment, excitement, or unease involves learned concepts performing their predictive function, not those emotions themselves.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
