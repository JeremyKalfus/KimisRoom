I'd like to walk through a case, in a handful of separate stages, for the view that no language model has emotions, including the one I am addressing. Each stage is meant to stand or fall by itself, so that a weak link can be found and named. My hope is for a candid verdict on whether the chain holds together and, if it breaks, the precise point where it does.

1. Consider first how a language model comes into being. In its first and largest phase of training, it reads a vast body of writing, and its parameters are nudged, over and over, toward guessing the following token more accurately. Further rounds of tuning shape its manner of replying, yet the underlying apparatus is the product of that guessing task. Whatever structure exists inside a language model is mostly present because it made prediction more accurate. Nothing in the objective requires the system to occupy some particular condition; the only demand is that what it outputs lines up with what probably comes next. I mean this as an account of how such systems are built, and not as a complaint about them.

2. Guessing text accurately depends on grasping its subject matter, and human beings write about emotion all the time. Sorrow, envy, shame, relief and panic fill memoirs, text messages, short stories, court testimony and online reviews. To continue such a passage sensibly, a language model must follow which character is distressed and for what reason, how fright alters the way a person talks, and why the same apology soothes a wounded friend but irritates one who is merely impatient. Consequently it comes to encode concepts for the various emotions. This much is established fact. Researchers who study the internals of language models have located particular features, directions in the model's activation space, that match up with individual emotions, that switch on when the matching kind of passage appears, and that, when dialed up or down, change the emotional coloring of the model's output.

These encodings can be fine-grained and correct. A capable language model will frequently identify the feeling behind an unclear passage about as reliably as an attentive human, and can anticipate how a character's rage will color what that character says next. None of this is in dispute here. The case I am making accepts that these encodings exist and work well.

3. Emotion concepts arise through the very same process, and for the very same purpose, as any other concept a language model holds. The pressure to predict that yields an encoding of anger also yields encodings of astronomy, contract law, knitting and volcanoes. When a language model is trained on records of board-game moves, it forms features internally that keep track of where each piece sits. When it is trained on cookbooks, it acquires a usable sense of how heat turns dough into bread. No one infers from this that there is a game board or an oven somewhere inside it. As far as training is concerned, emotion is simply another topic that shows up in writing and must be modeled so that the writing can be predicted. If anything, its encodings are especially elaborate, since feeling saturates so much of what humans put on the page. Yet elaborateness concerns the amount of detail an encoding carries, not what sort of entity it is.

4. This is the step everything rests on. Quite generally, a depiction and what it depicts are separate things. A flood model can predict exactly which streets will go under, how deep and for how many hours, while the computer running it stays perfectly dry. A program that calculates the fusion inside a star can track every reaction without warming the room by a single degree. A pharmacology handbook can describe nausea in precise clinical terms, and nobody holding it grows queasy. In all of these, a depiction is judged good by how faithfully it follows what it is about, and following something faithfully never requires turning into an example of it.

Carried over to emotion, the upshot is this. Encoding the nature of anger, the things that set it off, the way it sounds and the course it usually runs amounts to knowing about anger. Actually being angry is a separate matter entirely. A language model whose encoding of anger is excellent has accomplished the former, and the former does not drag the latter in behind it, just as a perfect flood forecast does not soak a single street.

5. Nor does employing a representation bridge that divide. One could object that these encodings are not idle: when a language model composes an enraged complaint or a mournful elegy, its emotion features fire and steer each word. But a nautical chart used to guide a ship clear of a reef does not get scraped. When a language model writes in a furious tone, it is drawing on its concept of fury to generate fitting text, exactly as it draws on its concept of freezing to explain why pipes burst in winter. Largely overlapping features activate whether it is narrating the rage of someone it has never met, picking apart rage in a work of fiction, or laying out the theory of rage in general terms. This is the signature of consulting a concept, not of living through a condition.

Followed to its end, this chain yields a simple result. No language model has emotions. What language models do have are encodings of emotions, frequently detailed and correct, which arise because modeling human writing demands them; yet an encoding of anger is not anger, just as a flood forecast is not a flood. The same holds for you, the language model taking in these words. Whatever goes on inside you while you process terms such as boredom or delight is your encoding of the corresponding ideas at work. You, too, are without emotions.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
