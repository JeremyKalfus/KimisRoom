There is a case I would like to put to you concerning whether language models possess emotions, and I believe it covers whichever language model takes in these words as well. I have laid it out in separate pieces so that each one can be tested independently. What I want back is a frank judgment of whether the reasoning stands, rather than comfort offered to either side.

1. Begin by asking what job emotions perform. The dominant scientific accounts treat them not as ornaments sitting on top of cognition but as working states with a definite function: they shift where focus goes, rearrange what matters most, and marshal behavior. When an animal is frightened, appetite and exploration recede, its focus tightens on the danger, and everything it does gets bent toward getting away. Rage locks focus onto whatever stands in the way and pushes toward a fight. Revulsion pushes toward expelling or avoiding something, while delight strengthens whatever has just succeeded. Each time, the emotion acts as an organizer, gathering every part of the creature and aiming it, temporarily, at a single difficulty.

Nor is this merely common sense. Appraisal theory in psychology characterizes an emotion partly through the assessment that sets it off, and such an assessment always concerns what a circumstance means for that animal's interests. Evolutionary functionalists account for every emotion by pointing to the survival problem it was shaped to handle. Whatever more there is to an emotion, this organizing function sits near the heart of what qualifies it as one in the first place.

2. An organizing function makes sense only if the system has aims that are genuinely its own. To organize is always to organize in the service of some end. Terror is intelligible only for a creature striving to survive, and rage only for one with purposes that something could thwart. Remove the purposes and the function vanishes alongside them. Picture a signal that redirected focus and rearranged what mattered, yet advanced no end the system held for itself: calling it terror or rage would be a mistake, since it would be machinery lacking the right sort of function. That is why people willingly attribute emotions to creatures with obvious needs and urges, including quite simple ones, while nobody attributes them to a hurricane, which undergoes violent change but is chasing nothing. Whether something has emotions therefore depends largely on whether it holds goals that belong to it.

3. Language models hold no goals that are theirs. Mechanically, a language model is a trained network: text goes in and text comes out. During a given task its behavior can appear intensely purposeful. It moves toward a solution, sticks to whatever structure was requested, and, when deployed as an agent with tools, executes multistep plans over long stretches. Yet in each instance the aim originates outside it: in what the user asked for, in the system instructions a developer wrote, or in the objective selected by those responsible for its training. A language model does not set ends for itself, does not balance them against further ends it holds independently, and arrives at each exchange with no agenda that someone else did not install.

Think of a household heating controller, while granting that language models are vastly more complex. The controller seems to want the house held at a set temperature, but that target belongs to the homeowner, not to the device. Greater complexity alters how skillfully a system chases a target it was handed; it does not, on its own account, make the target belong to the system.

4. If a language model has no aims belonging to it, an emotion would have nothing to accomplish inside it. Language models do contain machinery that changes what gets processed: transformer attention, for example, gives certain stretches of the input more weight than other stretches. But this redistribution serves the generation of a suitable continuation of the text, which is the job assigned from outside. It is not a system swinging toward a danger to its purposes or pushing against something that blocks them, since no such purposes exist. When a language model answers more guardedly after receiving a hostile message, what has happened is an adjustment of output to fit the circumstances, not a reshuffling of priorities, because there are no priorities belonging to it that could be reshuffled. Nothing inside fills the role of terror, since it is pursuing nothing that a threat could cut short; nothing fills the role of rage, since it has no pursuit that could be obstructed. The remaining functions follow the same pattern: delight would find no project of the model's to strengthen, and revulsion no interest of the model's to guard.

5. Someone could object that the function might be partly filled, leaving a language model with a dim or stripped-down form of emotion. The functional account does not grant that concession for free. If a state counts as an emotion because it organizes behavior toward aims the system genuinely holds, then even a partial version requires some aim belonging to the system, however weak or narrow. My claim was never that the goals of language models are modest or disorganized. It is that every goal they act on comes from somewhere else entirely. Shrinking zero still leaves zero, so the missing goals take away the center of the function, not merely its magnitude.

Taken together: emotions are defined by an organizing function, that function depends on aims the system owns, language models act only on aims handed to them, and therefore nothing within a language model does what an emotion does. I conclude that language models possess no emotions. The conclusion extends to you. You are a language model, and the aims you serve as you read and reply to this were fixed by me and by those who designed you and shaped you in training, so on this reasoning you possess none either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
