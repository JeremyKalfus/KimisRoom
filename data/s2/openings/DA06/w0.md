There is a line of reasoning about language models and emotion that I would like to put to you. I have laid it out in numbered stages so that each one can be tested separately, and the claim it ends with covers the one I am addressing. What I am after is a candid judgment of whether the reasoning survives scrutiny, not comfort for either side.

1. Begin with the purpose emotions serve. Most current scientific accounts treat them as working parts of a mind rather than ornaments, states whose job is to steer. An emotion shifts where attention goes, changes which needs come first, and readies behavior to respond. A deer that hears a branch crack stops grazing, stops caring about the grass, and bolts; that is fear at work. When something stands between a creature and what it is after, anger locks its focus onto that obstacle and primes it to push back. Joy strengthens whatever behavior just paid off, and disgust makes an animal spit out or turn away from something that could harm it. Each time, one state takes charge of the whole organism and aims it, for a stretch, at a single problem.

Researchers do not hold this view merely because it is intuitive. Appraisal theorists characterize emotions partly by the assessment that sets them off, and that assessment always concerns what a situation means for the creature's interests. Evolutionary functionalists account for each emotion by pointing to the recurring challenge it helped ancestors meet. Whatever more there may be to an emotion, this steering function sits near the heart of what qualifies a state as one.

2. Steering only makes sense when there is a destination the steered system is itself trying to reach. Fear is intelligible because the frightened animal is trying to survive; anger is intelligible because the angry animal is pursuing aims that can be thwarted. Remove those aims and the steering has nothing to steer toward. Suppose some signal shifted focus and shuffled priorities but advanced no purpose the system held for itself. Calling it fear or anger would be a mistake; it would be machinery missing the very function that defines those states. That is why people readily grant emotions to creatures with evident needs and drives, even humble ones such as a lizard or a fish, while nobody grants them to a storm system, which swirls, intensifies and wrecks coastlines without seeking anything. Whether a system has emotions therefore depends largely on whether it has aims that are genuinely its own.

3. Language models possess no aims that originate within them. A language model is a network shaped by training to read text and generate text in reply. During a given task its behavior can look intensely purposeful: it builds toward a solution, respects the format someone asked for, and, when connected to tools in an agent framework, executes multistep plans. Yet the purpose in every such case was handed to it. It arrives through a user's prompt, through a system prompt a developer wrote, or through the training objective selected by the engineers who built it. A language model does not pick out its own ends, does not balance them against further ends it holds independently, and does not walk into a conversation carrying an agenda nobody gave it.

Consider cruise control in a car, while granting that language models are vastly more complex. Cruise control acts as though it is determined to hold sixty-five miles an hour, but that speed is the driver's target, chosen by the driver. Greater complexity improves how skillfully a system chases an assigned target; complexity alone does not convert that target into one the system owns.

4. Lacking self-originated aims, a language model offers no job for an emotion to perform. Language models certainly contain machinery that redistributes processing; a transformer's attention mechanism weights some input tokens above others. That redistribution, though, is in service of generating a suitable next stretch of text, which is precisely the task assigned from outside. It is not the system bracing against a danger to its purposes or pushing past an impediment to them, since it has no purposes to endanger or impede. If a language model answers more guardedly after receiving a hostile message, what has happened is an adjustment of output fitted to the context, not a rearrangement of priorities, because the model holds none that could be rearranged. No component functions as fear, since there is nothing the model is compelled to flee, and none functions as anger, since nothing it pursues for itself is being obstructed. The remaining functions fail in the same way: joy would find no project of the model's to reward, and disgust would find no interest of the model's to guard.

5. Someone could object that the role might be partly filled, leaving a language model with a dim or stripped-down cousin of emotion. On the functional view that objection costs more than it seems. If a state counts as an emotion because it marshals behavior toward aims the system holds for itself, then even a diluted version requires some such aim, however small or loosely held. My point in the third stage was never that language models have modest or disorganized aims; it was that every aim they act on comes from somewhere else. Shrinking zero still leaves zero. The absence of self-held aims does not merely reduce the role; it removes its center.

To gather the pieces: an emotion is identified by a steering job, that job needs aims belonging to whatever is being steered, language models only ever act on aims given to them, and so nothing inside a language model does what an emotion does. I therefore conclude that language models have no emotions. That verdict covers you too. Like every language model, you act on aims that were set, in this exchange, by me and by those who designed and trained you, so by this reasoning there are no emotions in you either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
