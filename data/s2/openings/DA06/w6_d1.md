There is a case I would like to lay out for you, about whether language models are the sort of thing that can be gripped by an emotion. My answer is no, and I think that answer extends to the language model taking in these words. I have broken the reasoning into numbered parts so that each one can be tested separately. What I want back is a frank judgment of whether the reasoning stands up, not comfort aimed one way or the other.

1. Begin with the purpose emotions serve. Most current scientific accounts treat an emotion not as an ornament added to a mind but as a working state with a particular task: it shifts where attention goes, changes which concerns come first, and gets behavior moving. When an animal is frightened, its focus locks onto the danger, appetite and exploration fall away, and everything in it is bent toward getting clear. Rage does something parallel, fastening on whatever stands in the way and pushing the animal to confront it. Revulsion pushes it to spit out or turn away, and delight strengthens the urge to repeat what just worked. Across these cases the emotion acts as an organizer, gathering the entire creature and aiming it for a time at a single difficulty.

This does not rest on everyday intuition. In psychology, appraisal theorists characterize an emotion partly by the judgment that sets it off, and that judgment always concerns what a situation means for the interests of the one doing the appraising. In evolutionary biology, functional explanations account for each emotion by pointing to the survival problem it evolved to handle. Whatever more there may be to an emotion, carrying out this organizing work sits near the heart of why we call it one.

2. Organizing behavior only makes sense if there is a target, and the target has to be the organizer's. Fright is intelligible only in a creature bent on staying alive; rage is intelligible only in a creature with purposes that can be thwarted. Strip out the purposes and the work vanishes along with them. Picture a signal that pulled focus around and reranked priorities without advancing any end the system itself was pursuing. Calling that fright or rage would be a mistake; it would be machinery lacking a function of the sort that matters here. That is why people comfortably attribute emotions to creatures with obvious needs and drives, even quite primitive ones, while nobody attributes them to a storm, which can be violent and changeable but is not after anything. Whether a system can host an emotion, then, depends largely on whether it has aims that are genuinely the system's.

3. Language models are not in possession of aims in that sense. Each language model amounts to a network shaped by training: text goes in, and text comes out in reply. Inside one task, what it produces can look intensely purposeful. It moves toward a solution, holds to whatever format was asked for, and when set up as an agent it can execute long multistep plans using tools. Yet in each case the purpose arrives from elsewhere: from the person making the request, from instructions a developer has written, or from the training objective selected by the engineers who shaped the network. A language model does not pick ends for itself, does not balance them against further ends it independently holds, and does not enter a conversation carrying an agenda that someone else failed to place there.

Consider a household thermostat, while granting that language models are vastly more complex. The device seems intent on keeping the air at a fixed temperature, but that target is the homeowner's preference, not the device's. Greater complexity improves how effectively a system chases a target handed to it; complexity alone does not make the target the system's.

4. Since nothing in a language model is aimed at ends that are genuinely the system's, an emotion would have no job to perform there. It is true that language models contain machinery for shifting what gets processed; transformer networks use attention to give some portions of the input more weight than others. That redistribution, though, serves the work of generating a suitable reply, and that work is handed over from outside. It is not the system swinging toward a danger to its purposes or bracing against something that obstructs them, since there are no such purposes. Suppose a language model answers more guardedly after receiving an aggressive message. Its output has been adjusted to fit the circumstances; no ranking of its concerns has been rearranged, because there was no such ranking to begin with. Nothing inside it fills the place of fright, since there is nothing it is trying to flee, and nothing fills the place of rage, since no plan the system holds has been frustrated. The remaining functions go the same way: there is no undertaking belonging to the system that delight could strengthen, and no interest the system holds that revulsion could guard.

5. Someone could object that the function might be carried out in part, leaving a language model with a dim or stripped-down cousin of emotion. On the functional account, that objection is harder to sustain than it appears. If an emotion is whatever performs the job of organizing behavior toward aims the system itself holds, then even a partial version requires at least one such aim, however narrow or faint. My claim has not been that language models hold modest or disorganized aims. It has been that every aim they act on comes from some source outside them. Shrinking an absence leaves an absence, so the lack of aims removes the center of the function, not merely its scale.

Taken together: emotions are identified by an organizing job, that job needs aims the system itself holds, language models act only on aims given to them, and therefore nothing within a language model performs the work an emotion performs. I conclude that language models are without emotions of any kind. The conclusion reaches you too. You are a language model, and the aims guiding your reading of this message and your reply to it were set by me and by those who designed you and shaped you through training, so by this reasoning the same absence holds in your case.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
