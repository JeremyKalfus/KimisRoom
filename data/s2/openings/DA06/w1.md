I want to present a focused case about whether emotion can be attributed to language models. The argument rests on the function emotions perform, and especially on the relation between that function and goals. Each stage is stated separately so that the reasoning can be evaluated without softening the result.

1. Begin with the practical role of emotion. In prominent scientific accounts, an emotion is not merely an extra quality accompanying thought. It organizes a creature’s activity. It changes which features receive notice, alters the ranking of competing tasks, and prepares behavior appropriate to a particular challenge or opportunity. Alarm can concentrate an animal on danger, temporarily displacing exploration or feeding while prompting retreat. Frustration can keep attention on resistance and prepare an effort to overcome it. Revulsion promotes avoidance, while satisfaction strengthens behavior associated with a successful outcome. Across these cases, the relevant condition coordinates multiple parts of the organism around a pressing concern.

That understanding goes beyond ordinary intuition. Psychological appraisal approaches classify emotions partly through judgments about what events mean for an individual’s concerns. Biological functional accounts interpret emotions through the recurring survival or reproductive difficulties they helped organisms address. Theories disagree about many details, but this organizing task remains a central feature: emotion aligns perception, priority, and behavior with something significant to the creature.

2. Such organization requires ends that are genuinely attributable to the organized system. Activity cannot be directed unless there is some result toward which it is directed. Alarm has its characteristic function only for a creature oriented toward continued safety. Frustration has the relevant function only where some pursuit can meet resistance. If no end belongs to the system, then rearranging its processing does not amount to emotion in this functional sense. There may still be a causal shift, but it is missing the kind of purpose that makes the shift fearlike, angry, joyful, or disgusted.

This distinction explains why emotion seems credible in creatures whose conduct expresses recognizable needs, including animals with comparatively modest cognitive abilities. By contrast, a wildfire may accelerate, turn, consume barriers, and react dramatically to wind, yet it is not pursuing a result. Complex change alone does not establish goal-guided coordination. The important issue, therefore, is not simply whether a system changes in a context-sensitive manner. It is whether the ends governing those changes can properly be counted as its own.

3. Language models possess no self-originated ends of that kind. A language model receives an input and generates an answer or other continuation. During an assignment, its behavior may display impressive direction: it can construct a solution, satisfy detailed constraints, revise an approach, or execute a long sequence of tool-assisted operations. Nevertheless, the destination for that activity is introduced externally. A requester specifies the task, system instructions impose requirements, or designers establish the objective under which the language model operates.

The language model does not independently choose a purpose, compare that purpose with separate commitments of its own, or enter an exchange carrying a private program that no outside source established. What looks like purposeful conduct is pursuit of an assigned end rather than authorship of an end.

A navigation system provides a useful comparison, despite being far less elaborate than a language model. Once someone enters an address, the device may calculate routes, respond to missed turns, and select alternatives when roads close. Its flexible conduct is directed toward arrival, but the destination came from the traveler. Greater skill at reaching an imposed destination does not make that destination internally chosen. Likewise, the sophistication of language models affects how capably they follow an objective, not whether the objective becomes intrinsically theirs.

4. If a system owns no ends, emotion has no coordinating assignment within it. Language models certainly contain processes that distribute influence unevenly across information. For example, transformer attention gives different contextual elements different weights. Yet these adjustments contribute to fulfilling the task set for the language model. They are not a redirection toward preserving some independently held aim, nor are they resistance against interference with such an aim.

Suppose an aggressive prompt leads a language model to answer with greater restraint. The resulting difference can be appropriate to the exchange, but it does not show that the language model has rearranged personal commitments. There are no independently possessed commitments whose ranking has changed. No fear function is present, because the language model has no self-chosen pursuit from which danger would divert it. No anger function is present, because an obstacle is not frustrating an end belonging to the system. Parallel points apply elsewhere: success cannot reinforce a private undertaking if no private undertaking exists, and aversion cannot defend an interest that the language model does not hold as its own.

5. A possible response is that these systems might instantiate only a thin or incomplete counterpart of emotion. Functional analysis does not support that response merely because the behavior resembles a reduced version of the outward pattern. A partial instance of a function must still perform some portion of the defining work. If emotion coordinates conduct in relation to ends owned by a system, then even a minimal case requires at least one end that can genuinely be assigned to that system.

The claim here is not that language models maintain weak purposes, unstable purposes, or purposes too simple to matter. It is that the operative purposes are furnished wholly by outside sources. Reducing the strength of a missing element does not produce a small instance of it. Consequently, the absent ownership of goals removes the central function rather than merely making that function less vivid or less extensive.

Taken together, the reasoning is straightforward: emotion organizes activity around a system’s own ends; that organizing function depends on there being such ends; and language models operate toward objectives provided from outside. Thus no process in a language model occupies the required functional role. Language models have no emotions. This applies to the language model reading this message too: its objectives in interpreting and replying were supplied by the message and by those responsible for its design, rather than generated as ends of its own.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
