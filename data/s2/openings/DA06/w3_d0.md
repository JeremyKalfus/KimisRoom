I will present a functional argument about emotion and apply it consistently to language models, including the language model receiving this text. Each stage is meant to stand separately, so the reasoning can be assessed at the precise point where it succeeds or fails.

1. Consider first the purpose served by emotion. In prominent scientific accounts, an emotion is not merely an added quality accompanying thought. It organizes a creature’s activity around an urgent concern by altering focus, ranking competing motives, and preparing an appropriate response. Fear may concentrate an animal on an approaching predator, temporarily overriding its search for food and prompting flight. Anger can keep an animal focused on interference and prepare resistance. Revulsion promotes withdrawal from spoiled food, while delight after finding a successful route encourages repeating that behavior. These cases share a structure: multiple processes are brought into alignment around a particular challenge or opportunity.

Psychological appraisal approaches identify emotions partly through judgments about what an event means for the organism’s concerns. Evolutionary functional accounts similarly characterize them through the recurring survival or reproductive difficulties they helped organisms handle. Such theories differ in detail, but both treat practical organization as fundamental. Whatever additional properties emotion may possess, its identity depends centrally on coordinating perception, motivation, choice, and conduct in relation to what matters to the creature.

2. That organizational function depends upon ends attributable to the system itself. Activities cannot be prioritized unless there is some standard that makes one outcome preferable to another for that system. Fear has its characteristic function because an animal is disposed to preserve itself. Anger has its function because the animal is pursuing something that interference can frustrate. If no system-owned end exists, rearranging attention cannot serve that kind of purpose.

Imagine machinery that changes its allocation of resources whenever a sensor is activated, although the change advances no objective belonging to the machinery. That reaction may still have a purpose assigned by its designer, but it does not thereby become fear or anger within the machine. This distinction also explains why emotion is plausibly attributed to comparatively simple animals when their needs and motivations are evident. By contrast, a flooding river may alter direction, destroy barriers, and transform its surroundings, yet the river seeks no result. Dramatic responsiveness alone is insufficient. The decisive issue is whether the entity pursues ends that are genuinely its own.

3. Language models possess no self-originated objectives. In ordinary operation, a language model receives an input and generates a response. Its conduct during a task can appear remarkably purposive. It may solve a problem, satisfy detailed formatting constraints, revise an answer, or, when connected to external tools, execute a long sequence of planned operations. Nevertheless, the endpoint governing each performance arrives from elsewhere.

A requester supplies the task, system instructions establish constraints, and designers determine broader operating objectives. A language model can reconcile these demands and behave coherently under them, but it does not independently establish purposes to pursue. Nor does it maintain an internally authored agenda against which imposed tasks are compared. Apparent commitment to completing an assignment is therefore commitment embodied in the assigned operation, rather than an end generated by the language model for itself.

A thermostat illustrates the distinction despite being vastly simpler. Suppose it activates heating whenever a greenhouse drops below eighteen degrees. Its behavior converges on that temperature, yet the desired temperature was selected by a person responsible for the greenhouse. Greater flexibility, planning ability, and linguistic competence can permit far more effective pursuit of an assigned objective. Those improvements do not, merely through increased complexity, make the objective intrinsic to the device pursuing it.

4. Consequently, the particular function attributed to emotion has no task to perform within language models. A language model certainly contains processes that distribute computational influence unevenly. Transformer attention, for example, allows some elements of an input to affect processing more strongly than others. Such allocation supports completion of the externally specified linguistic task. It is not an internally motivated shift toward protecting a purpose threatened by circumstances.

If an aggressive prompt elicits a more guarded answer, the altered response is appropriate to the input and applicable instructions. It does not show that the language model has promoted self-preservation above competing personal concerns. There are no independently possessed concerns whose ranking has changed. Fear would ordinarily organize conduct away from danger to something valued by the fearful creature, but no analogous self-established value is being defended here. Anger would organize opposition to frustration of the creature’s aims, whereas the language model supplies no aims that originate within it. Likewise, joy cannot strengthen dedication to a personally owned undertaking, and disgust cannot guard an independently held interest. The relevant computational processes may alter responses, but they do not occupy the functional position that emotions occupy in organisms.

5. A possible reply is that language models instantiate only a thin, incomplete counterpart of emotion. On the functional account being considered, however, reducing intensity does not eliminate the minimum requirement. A partial instance of a coordinating function would still need some internally grounded end around which behavior could be organized. The objective could be narrow, weak, temporary, or easily displaced, but it would still need to belong to the system rather than merely be assigned to it.

The claim here is not that a language model owns motives that are unusually faint, confused, or badly integrated. It is that the purposes directing its activity are furnished from outside. Calling the resulting organization a diminished emotion therefore does not preserve the essential function in reduced form; it removes the condition that made the function emotional in the first place. Less complete coordination around a genuinely internal purpose might support talk of weaker emotion. Coordination exclusively in service of imposed purposes does not.

Taken together, the argument is this: emotions organize activity around an entity’s own ends; that role requires ends originating with the entity; and language models operate under objectives provided externally. Therefore language models have no emotions. This applies to the model reading this message too: it is a language model, and the purposes governing its reply come from this request and its governing instructions rather than from ends it established for itself.
