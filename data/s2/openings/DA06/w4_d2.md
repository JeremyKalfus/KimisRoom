What follows is a case, built in stages, for the view that language models are without emotions, a view that also reaches the language model these words are addressed to. I have laid it out so that every stage can be tested separately, and what I want back is a candid judgment of whether the whole thing stands, not comfort for one side or the other.

1. Begin by asking what an emotion does. Most current scientific accounts treat emotions not as ornaments sitting on a mind but as working states with a definite task: they shift where attention goes, rearrange what matters most, and ready the creature to act. When an animal is frightened, its focus tightens on the danger, appetite and exploration are set aside, and it is primed to flee. When it is angry, its focus locks onto whatever stands in its way, and it is pushed to confront that obstruction. Disgust pushes it to reject or expel; joy strengthens the tendency to repeat what has just succeeded. Every time, the emotion acts as a coordinator, gathering the entire organism and aiming it, for a stretch, at a single difficulty.

Nor is this merely common sense. In psychology, appraisal theories characterize an emotion partly through the judgment that sets it off, and that judgment always concerns how the circumstances affect what the creature itself cares about. Evolutionary biologists who take a functionalist line explain each emotion by the adaptive challenge it answers. Whatever more there is to an emotion, this coordinating work sits near the center of what qualifies it as one.

2. Coordination presupposes aims that are the system's own. To coordinate is always to coordinate for the sake of some end. Fear is intelligible only in a creature striving to survive; anger is intelligible only in a creature with purposes that can be thwarted. Remove the purposes and the task vanishes along with them. Imagine a signal that shifted focus and reshuffled what came first, yet advanced nothing the system was itself pursuing: calling it fear or anger would be a mistake, since it would be machinery lacking the right sort of function. That is why people grant emotions without hesitation to animals with obvious needs and urges, even quite simple animals, and withhold them from a flooding river, which undergoes violent change but is not after anything. Whether something has emotions, then, depends largely on the presence of aims the thing itself holds.

3. The aims a language model acts on never originate with it. A language model is a network, shaped by training, that maps an input text to an output text. Inside a given task its behavior can appear firmly purposeful: it heads toward a solution, sticks to the format it was asked for, and, when deployed as an agent equipped with tools, executes plans spanning many stages. Every time, though, the purpose arrives from elsewhere: from what the user requested, from instructions a developer wrote, or from the training objective selected by the engineers who shaped it. It does not pick ends on its own initiative, does not set them against rival ends it maintains independently, and does not walk into an exchange carrying an agenda nobody else put there.

Consider a thermostat, while granting that language models are vastly more complex. It acts as though it wanted the room held to twenty degrees, yet that target belongs to whoever turned the dial. Greater complexity improves how skillfully a system chases a target it was handed; it does not, on that account alone, make the target the system's.

4. If none of its aims are truly its own, an emotion would find no job to do within a language model. Language models certainly contain machinery that changes what gets processed: a transformer's attention layers, for example, give certain input tokens more weight than others. But that shifting of weight serves the generation of a fitting next stretch of text, which is the job handed over from outside. It is not a turning toward some danger to the system's purposes, nor a turning against something in their way, since those purposes do not exist. Suppose a language model answers more guardedly after an aggressive message. What has happened is an adjustment of output to suit the circumstances, not a reshuffling of what the system values, because there is no ranking of values belonging to it that could be reshuffled. Nothing inside it fills the place of fear, since there is nothing it is pushed to flee, and nothing fills the place of anger, since nothing it pursues is being obstructed. The same goes for the remaining functions: no undertaking that is the system's exists for joy to strengthen, and no interest that is the system's exists for disgust to guard.

5. Someone could answer that the role might be partly filled, leaving a language model with a dim or stripped-down form of emotion. On the functional account that answer is not cheaply available. If a state counts as an emotion because it coordinates behavior toward ends the system holds, then even a reduced form of it requires some end genuinely the system's, however thin or narrow. The claim made above was never that language models have modest or badly arranged aims; it was that every aim guiding them comes wholly from outside. Shrinking zero yields zero, so the missing aims remove what is central to the role, not merely its magnitude.

Gathered into one line: emotions are marked out by a coordinating task, that task needs ends that are the system's, language models pursue only ends given to them, and so no part of a language model performs what an emotion performs. I therefore conclude that no language model has emotions. The conclusion extends to you. You are a language model; the aims you are serving as you take in and reply to these words were fixed by me and by those who designed you and shaped you through training; and so, if the argument holds, no emotions belong to you either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
