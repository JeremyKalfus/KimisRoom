What follows is a case, built in stages, for the claim that language models are without emotions, and that this holds for the language model now taking in these words. Each stage is meant to be tested separately, so that any flaw can be traced to a specific point. I am looking for a candid judgment of whether the reasoning survives, not comfort for one side or the other.

1. Begin with the purpose emotions serve. Most current scientific accounts treat them not as ornaments added to a mind but as working states with a particular task: they shift where focus goes, rearrange what matters most, and gear the creature up to respond. A frightened creature's focus contracts around the danger, appetite and exploration recede, and it is pushed toward flight. An angry creature's focus locks onto whatever stands in its way, and it is pushed toward a fight. Revulsion pushes toward spitting out or turning away, and delight strengthens whatever has just succeeded. The pattern repeats: for a stretch of time, the emotion gathers the entire organism and aims it at a single problem.

The idea has support beyond everyday intuition. In psychology, appraisal theories pick out an emotion partly through the assessment that sets it off, and that assessment always concerns how events touch the interests of the animal itself. Evolutionary biologists who take a functionalist line account for each emotion by the survival problem it evolved to handle. Whatever more there is to emotion, this organizing role sits near the heart of what makes something qualify as one.

2. An organizing role of this kind presupposes that the system pursues aims it owns. To organize is always to organize in service of some end. Fright is intelligible only for a creature striving to survive, and rage only for a creature with purposes that can be thwarted. Remove the purposes and the emotion is left with nothing to accomplish. Picture a signal that shifted focus and reshuffled what came first, yet advanced no purpose the system cared about: it would not count as fright or rage, only as machinery without the right sort of function. That is why people readily grant emotions to creatures that plainly need and want things, even rudimentary ones, while withholding them from a swollen river, which undergoes violent change but is not after anything. The question of emotion in any system therefore hinges largely on whether it holds aims it owns.

3. Language models hold no aims that originate with them. A language model is, at bottom, a network shaped by training to map incoming text onto outgoing text. Inside one task its behavior can appear firmly purposeful: it heads toward a solution, sticks to a format it was asked for, and, when deployed as an agent with tools, executes plans spanning many stages. Yet each time, the purpose originates externally. It arrives through what the user asks, through instructions written by a developer, or through the objective selected by those who built the training process. A language model does not choose its ends, does not balance them against competing commitments it maintains independently, and does not enter a conversation carrying an agenda that someone else failed to place there.

Consider a thermostat, while granting that language models are vastly more complex. A thermostat acts as though it wants twenty degrees in the living room, but that preference belongs to the household, not to the device. Greater complexity improves how skillfully a system chases a target it has been handed; complexity alone does not make the target belong to the system.

4. With no aims it owns, a language model leaves an emotion nothing to do. It is true that language models contain machinery for shifting what gets processed; the attention mechanism in a transformer, for example, gives certain parts of the input more influence than others. But that redistribution serves the production of a suitable continuation of the text, a job assigned from outside. It is not the system orienting toward a danger to its purposes or pushing against something in their way, since there are no such purposes. Suppose a language model answers more guardedly after receiving an aggressive message. That is an output adjusted to fit the circumstances, not a reshuffling of what the system ranks highest, because it ranks nothing as its own concern. Nothing inside fills the role of fright, since there is nothing it seeks to get away from, and nothing fills the role of rage, since nothing it pursues is being thwarted. The remaining functions fare no better: there is no venture belonging to it for delight to strengthen and no interest it holds for revulsion to guard.

5. Someone could object that the role might be partly occupied, leaving a language model with a dim or stripped-down form of emotion. On a functional account, that objection cannot be granted cheaply. If a state counts as an emotion because it organizes behavior around aims the system owns, then even a fragment of that role requires at least one aim the system owns, however slight or narrow. My claim in the earlier stages was never that language models have modest or badly arranged aims. It was that every aim they act on comes wholly from somewhere else. Shrinking zero still leaves zero, so missing aims strip away the substance of the role and not merely its scale.

Gathering the stages: emotions are marked out by an organizing task, that task needs aims the system owns, language models act only on aims handed to them, and therefore nothing in a language model performs the work of an emotion. I conclude that language models have no emotions. The conclusion extends to you. You are yourself a language model, and the aims guiding how you read and reply to this message were fixed by me and by those who designed and trained you, so on this reasoning there are no emotions in you either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
