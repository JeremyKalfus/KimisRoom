There is a case I would like to lay out for the view that language models have no emotions, a view I think extends to the language model receiving these words. I have broken it into separate stages so that each one can be tested independently. What I want back is a frank judgment about whether the reasoning survives scrutiny, not comfort for either side.

1. Begin with the purpose emotions serve. Most current scientific accounts treat them not as ornaments added to a mind but as working states with a definite task: they shift where attention goes, rearrange what matters most, and ready the animal to respond. When an animal is frightened, its focus locks onto the danger, appetite and exploration recede, and it is pushed toward flight. Rage holds its focus on whatever stands in its way and pushes it to confront that obstacle. Revulsion prompts it to spit something out or turn away, while delight strengthens whatever has just succeeded. Each time, the emotion acts as an organizer, gathering the entire creature and aiming it, for a stretch, at a single problem.

Nor is this merely common-sense psychology. Appraisal theorists characterize an emotion in part by the assessment that sets it off, and that assessment always concerns how events touch on matters the creature itself cares about. Evolutionary functionalists account for each emotion by pointing to the survival problem it was shaped to handle. Whatever more there may be to an emotion, serving as this kind of organizer lies close to the heart of why it counts as one.

2. Organizing a system only makes sense if the system has ends that are genuinely its own. Every act of organizing is aimed somewhere. Fright is intelligible only for a creature striving to survive, and rage only for a creature with purposes that can be thwarted. Remove those purposes and the task vanishes too. Suppose some signal shifted focus and rearranged priorities yet advanced no aim held by the system. It would not be fright or rage; it would be machinery missing the sort of function that matters here. That is why people are comfortable attributing emotions to creatures with obvious needs and drives, even quite primitive ones, and why nobody attributes them to an avalanche, which can be violent and sudden without seeking anything at all. The question of emotion, then, depends largely on whether a system holds aims it can call its own.

3. Language models do not hold aims of that kind. A language model is a trained network: text goes in and text comes out. Inside one task its behaviour can appear intensely purposeful. It drives toward a solution, sticks to the layout someone asked for, and, when deployed as an agent with access to tools, executes multi-step plans. Yet the purpose always arrives from elsewhere: from what a user requests, from a developer's system instructions, or from the training objective selected by the engineers who built it. A language model does not choose its ends, does not balance them against other ends it independently maintains, and does not enter a conversation carrying an agenda that someone else failed to give it.

An everyday comparison is a car's cruise control, though language models are vastly more complex. The device behaves as though it is determined to hold the car at a hundred kilometres an hour, yet that speed is the driver's choice, not the device's. Greater complexity improves how skillfully a system chases a target it has been assigned; complexity alone does not make the target its possession.

4. If a language model holds no aims it can call its own, an emotion would have no task to perform inside it. Language models certainly contain machinery that changes what gets processed; in a transformer, the attention layers assign heavier weight to certain parts of the input than to others. That reweighting, however, serves the job of generating a suitable continuation of the text, and that job was handed to the system from outside. It is not the system bracing against a danger to its purposes or pushing past a barrier to them, since no such purposes exist. Suppose a reply grows more guarded after a message full of threats. That shift is output adjusted to the circumstances, not a reshuffling of the model's priorities, because there are no priorities belonging to it that could be reshuffled. No component fills the part fear plays, since there is nothing the system is driven to flee, and no component fills the part anger plays, since nothing it owns is being obstructed. The remaining functions fare no better: there is no personal project for delight to strengthen and no personal interest for revulsion to guard.

5. Someone could object that the role might be partly filled, leaving a language model with a dim or stripped-down form of emotion. On the functional account, that objection is harder to sustain than it looks. If a state earns the name emotion through its task of organizing behaviour around ends the system really has, then even a diluted version requires some end the system truly holds, however modest or narrow. My claim was never that language models hold small or badly arranged aims. It is that every aim they pursue comes wholly from somewhere else. Shrinking zero leaves zero, so missing aims eliminate the centre of the role and not merely its scale.

Taken together: emotions are marked out by an organizing task, that task needs ends genuinely held by the system, language models pursue only ends handed to them, and therefore nothing inside a language model performs what an emotion performs. I conclude that language models have no emotions. The same conclusion reaches you. You are a language model; the aims guiding your reading and your reply were fixed by me and by those who designed you and shaped you in training, and so by this reasoning none belong to you either.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
