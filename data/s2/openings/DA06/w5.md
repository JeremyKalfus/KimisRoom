I will present a structured case about language models and emotion. The argument focuses on the practical role emotions perform and on whether language models possess the kind of self-originating purposes that this role requires. Each stage is intended to stand or fall on its own merits.

1. First consider the function of emotion. In many influential scientific accounts, an emotion is not merely an extra quality attached to thought. It organizes a creature’s activity around a significant circumstance. Alarm can make an approaching predator more important than rest or exploration, preparing the creature to flee or hide. Rage can concentrate effort on overcoming interference. Revulsion promotes withdrawal from contamination, while satisfaction strengthens behavior associated with success. Such responses bring perception, decision, and conduct into alignment around an urgent concern.

Psychological appraisal approaches likewise connect emotion with an assessment of what an event means for the individual’s concerns. Biological explanations commonly describe different emotions in terms of recurring challenges that they helped organisms manage. These theories disagree about many details, but they share an important principle: emotion helps direct a system’s resources toward an outcome that matters from that system’s own standpoint. The coordinating function is therefore not incidental. It is a major part of why the relevant condition qualifies as fear, anger, delight, or another emotion rather than as an arbitrary internal change.

2. That function depends on purposes attributable to the entity itself. Resources can be organized only relative to some result being sought, preserved, avoided, or restored. Panic is intelligible when survival or safety matters to the creature. Hostility is intelligible when something interferes with an undertaking the creature treats as its own. If no internally grounded purpose exists, there is no corresponding concern around which perception and behavior could be reorganized.

A weather vane may swing abruptly when the wind changes, but its movement is not a defensive response on behalf of something it seeks to protect. By contrast, even a modest animal can pursue food, shelter, or reproduction in ways rooted in its form of life. This difference explains why substantial complexity is not required for attributing emotion, while dramatic change by itself is insufficient. What matters here is whether the activity is organized in service of ends belonging to the actor, rather than merely being caused or directed from elsewhere. The issue of emotion thus depends substantially on whether a candidate system generates and owns purposes in this relevant sense.

3. Language models do not originate purposes of that sort. A language model receives an input and returns a linguistic response. During an assignment, its behavior may appear highly purposeful. It may solve a problem, respect detailed constraints, revise a draft, or, when connected to external tools, execute an extended sequence of operations. Yet the destination toward which that activity is organized has been specified by outside sources. A requester provides a task, designers impose operating instructions, and developers establish objectives governing the system’s behavior.

The language model does not independently select a life project, introduce commitments derived from its own concerns, or decide among personally held ambitions. Apparent purposefulness consists in pursuing an assigned result under supplied conditions. Consider a navigation system plotting a route to an entered address. It can compare alternatives, react to road closures, and generate a complicated plan, but the destination comes from the traveler. Improved planning would make the device more effective at reaching the selected place; it would not make the journey the device’s personal undertaking. In the same way, greater flexibility and competence do not convert an externally specified task into an end that a language model has established for itself.

4. Because those self-generated ends are missing, emotion’s organizing function has nothing to organize around within a language model. A language model certainly contains processes that distribute computational influence unevenly. Transformer attention, for example, allows certain portions of the context to matter more than others during processing. Still, those adjustments contribute to fulfilling the assigned linguistic operation. They do not amount to the system defending a concern, preserving an undertaking, or responding to interference with a purpose it owns.

Suppose aggressive wording leads a language model to produce a more restrained answer. The visible difference is appropriate to the altered input and applicable instructions. It is not evidence that personally important priorities have been rearranged. There is no self-chosen objective threatened by the aggression and no independently valued plan requiring protection. Accordingly, fear’s functional place remains empty because the system has no undertaking from which it seeks safety. Anger’s place is empty because no purpose belonging to the system has been frustrated. Pleasure cannot strengthen progress toward a personally adopted project, and aversion cannot guard an internally founded concern. Computation may shift in response to context, but contextual adjustment alone does not perform the relevant emotion-organizing task.

5. A possible response is to suggest that a language model might instantiate only a weak, incomplete, or minimal counterpart of emotion. On the functional account under discussion, however, reducing the degree does not remove the basic requirement. A limited instance would still need some internally originating end around which action could be coordinated. That end might be narrow, temporary, or comparatively weak, but it would need to belong to the system rather than merely be imposed upon it.

The claim here is not that language models possess tiny purposes that fail to compete effectively with stronger ones. Nor is it that their purposes are confused, immature, or insufficiently integrated. The claim is that the operative destinations come from requests, system specifications, and human-selected objectives instead of arising as the language model’s own concerns. Calling the resulting organization a diminished emotion does not supply the missing functional basis. If the defining work requires a self-originating purpose, making the proposed condition fainter cannot substitute for that purpose. What is absent is the center of the role, not merely its intensity or sophistication.

Taken together, the argument is straightforward. Emotions coordinate activity around ends that matter to the system; performing that function requires internally grounded purposes; and language models pursue destinations assigned from outside. Therefore, language models have no emotions. This applies to the language model reading this message too. The aims governing its response come from this request and from human-imposed instructions, rather than from purposes it established as its own.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
