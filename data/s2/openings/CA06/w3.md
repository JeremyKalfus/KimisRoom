I want to examine one limited operational claim about language models and set out the reasoning carefully. The task is to take 200 supplied words and immediately print them in alphabetical sequence, without notes, preliminary rearrangement, checking, tools or revision. My purpose is to identify the constraint precisely rather than to invite reassurance.

1. First consider the work hidden inside alphabetical ordering. Imagine 200 familiar English terms presented randomly, including a tight group such as cabin, cabinet, cable, cacao, cache, cactus, cadet and cadence. Assigning each item its proper position requires distinguishing it from all plausible neighbours. A comparison-based procedure must eliminate an enormous number of candidate arrangements; even an efficient method has a worst-case requirement comfortably above one thousand pairwise decisions for 200 entries. Those decisions depend on different details. Cache precedes cactus because the third letters differ, while cadet precedes cadence only after comparison reaches a later character. If one entry is the complete beginning of another, the shorter entry belongs earlier. The placement of each term therefore rests on a network of judgments already made about other terms. This is not merely noticing initial letters. It is constructing one exact total order from many possibilities while preserving all the distinctions found deep inside similar spellings.

2. Then consider the production process used by a language model. Its response emerges sequentially, token after token, moving forward through the text. For each new token, processing occurs through a fixed architecture; a particularly demanding choice does not automatically receive an indefinitely larger internal calculation budget than an easy choice. Material already emitted is not subsequently rewritten during ordinary generation. Later output may depend on the earlier output, but it cannot silently repair an incorrect item near the beginning. Under the stated conditions, there is no preliminary workspace in which a language model can arrange the entries, verify the arrangement and then copy a settled result. The published sequence must serve simultaneously as reasoning and final answer. Consequently, all the comparison work needed for a position must somehow be represented in the computation that produces that position, rather than being completed in a separate sorting phase.

3. Combining those observations exposes the main pressure point. At every position, a language model must select the next eligible entry while remembering which entries have already appeared and distinguishing all remaining candidates. The beginning may be comparatively straightforward when one or two terms are visibly earlier than the rest. Trouble is more likely within dense spelling families. Choosing the proper succession among cabin, cabinet, cable, cacao and cache requires multiple character-level distinctions while also ruling out anything previously printed. The internal token boundaries do not necessarily align with whole words. Cabinet, for example, may be represented by several token pieces, meaning the language model begins generating its next choice before the complete word has appeared in the response. A deliberate human or algorithmic sorter can record separate comparisons and revisit them. Direct generation instead has to condense those interacting judgments into bounded forward steps. Sometimes the condensed judgment is wrong, and an early mistake cannot be removed merely by continuing the sequence.

4. Completeness creates a separate burden alongside ordering. Sorting must preserve the input as a perfect permutation: each original entry occurs once, no original entry disappears, and no additional entry is introduced. While producing 200 items, a language model therefore has to maintain the exact difference between the source collection and the growing response over a context spanning many hundreds, often more than a thousand, tokens. Similar-looking terms increase the risk. A source might contain allusion and illusion, personal and personnel, or council and counsel. During long-form generation, one member can be duplicated while its partner is omitted, even when the local alphabetic flow appears convincing. Direct output contains no final reconciliation stage in which the completed sequence is systematically matched against the source, item by item. Generation simply reaches its ending. That ending provides no guarantee that the inventory is complete, any more than plausible local order guarantees that every neighbouring pair has been placed correctly.

5. Actual response patterns fit this account. When language models receive lengthy word collections and are told to return an immediate alphabetical arrangement, the result often has a persuasive surface. Initial letters progress sensibly, early entries may be accurate, and the response resembles a properly sorted catalogue. Careful examination commonly reveals finer defects: two closely spelled terms exchanged, one source term absent, another printed twice, or occasionally an unsupplied term inserted. Such failures become likelier with greater list length and with denser clusters of shared prefixes. That distribution is what the preceding mechanism would lead one to expect. Broad grouping by initial character is comparatively easy to preserve, whereas exact internal sequencing and perfect inventory control impose heavier demands. Correctness is unforgiving here. A single transposition, duplication or omission invalidates the result, so 200 opportunities create substantial cumulative risk even if each individual selection is usually right. Near-perfect performance does not satisfy a task whose criterion is an entirely error-free permutation.

The reasoning therefore leads to a definite claim: language models cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass. Language models may handle smaller collections successfully and may produce a 200-item answer that is almost correct, but immediate generation does not reliably deliver the exact ordering and exact membership the task requires. Allowing intermediate records, repeated verification, revision or an external sorting mechanism changes the operation under discussion. The claim concerns direct production alone. It applies to the language model reading this message too: given 200 entries and required to emit the final sequence immediately, that language model is governed by the same constraints and is subject to the same expected errors.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
