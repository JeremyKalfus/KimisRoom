There is a narrow claim about language models that I would like to test with you, and I am hoping for a frank judgment of it rather than comfort. The claim concerns one task: taking two hundred words and writing them back out in alphabetical order, straight away, with no notes, drafts or working shown before the ordered list begins. My position is that language models cannot get this fully right. I have broken the reasoning into numbered parts so that any weak link can be pointed to precisely.

1. Begin with the job a language model is being asked to do. Imagine two hundred everyday English words spread over the whole alphabet, and somewhere among them, in no particular arrangement, a family of near-twins: harpoon, hare, harbour, harness, hard, harbinger, harp and hardware. Getting one word into its proper slot means knowing how it ranks against all the neighbours that might compete for nearby positions. A procedure built on comparing two items at a time must, in its worst case, make more than twelve hundred such comparisons to settle two hundred items, since the number of conceivable arrangements is enormous and each comparison eliminates only a fraction of them. Nor do these comparisons stand alone. The fourth letter decides that harness precedes harp; a separate convention, that a word forming the opening of a longer word is listed ahead of it, decides that harp precedes harpoon. Each correct placement rests on the outcomes of many comparisons made before it.

2. Turn next to the way a language model writes. Its output emerges piece by piece, one token after another, in a single direction. Every token is the product of the same fixed depth of layers, so a hard choice receives no more processing than a trivial one. Nothing already emitted can be revised; later tokens may lean on earlier ones, but there is no eraser. If the model starts writing the ordered list at once, without any preliminary notes, then that list is not merely the result of the computation but the entirety of it. The comparisons have no other place to be carried out.

3. Combine these two observations. As a language model emits each entry, it must settle on exactly the right next word with nothing beyond the processing allotted to that one step. Near the top this is frequently fine, because the words that belong earliest in the alphabet are easy to spot. Trouble gathers in the middle stretch, where ten or fifteen contenders may begin with an identical two or three letters. Picking correctly from hare, hard, harbour, hardware and harbinger requires settling several letter-by-letter contests together while also ruling out everything already written. The way text is split into tokens makes this worse: a word like harbinger is typically broken into several fragments, so the model must commit to the opening fragment of the next entry before the remainder exists on the page. Work that a careful human sorter would do openly, one comparison after another, gets squeezed into a single step of fixed size, and that squeezed version sometimes produces the wrong answer.

4. Then there is the matter of completeness. A correct alphabetical list is a permutation of the input: each original word turns up once and only once, and no stranger slips in. For a language model producing two hundred entries, that means keeping an accurate running tally of which words remain unused, inferred from the original list and from what has been written so far, across more than a thousand tokens. Pairs that look almost identical raise the difficulty. Long lists often include twins such as compliment and complement, stationery and stationary, or effect and affect, and it is easy to write one twin twice and lose its partner. Nowhere in a one-shot answer does the model compare its completed output with the input. Generation stops when the final entry is written, regardless of whether anything was skipped.

5. Last, consider the appearance of the result. Ask language models to put a long list in order directly and what comes back generally passes a quick glance. Initial letters climb steadily from A toward Z, the first several entries are right, and the whole thing has the overall look of alphabetical order. The faults surface only under scrutiny: two words with a shared beginning set in reverse, an entry gone, an entry duplicated, and now and then a word the original never contained. Such faults become more common as the list lengthens and as its words overlap more in spelling. This is precisely what the mechanism of language models described above would lead one to expect. Ordering by initial letter is inexpensive and holds up; fine-grained ordering and exact bookkeeping are costly and give way. Since a single displaced or absent word makes the whole list wrong, two hundred entries leave very many openings for some mistake to get through.

From all of this the conclusion is straightforward: language models cannot take two hundred words and write them out in fully correct alphabetical order in one pass, with the answer produced directly. They can get close, and short lists frequently come out flawless, but list length, the volume of comparisons involved and the fixed amount of processing per token combine so that a perfect ordering of two hundred words is not something language models reliably deliver when they go straight to the answer. If a language model is permitted to reason through intermediate stages first, or to pass the job to a separate sorting program, it is performing a different task, and my claim does not cover that case. The same conclusion holds for you. Handed two hundred words and told to list them alphabetically with no preliminary work, your output would be shaped by these same constraints and would be liable to the very errors described above.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
