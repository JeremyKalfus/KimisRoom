I want to examine one tightly defined practical limitation, without turning it into a broader claim about intelligence or competence. The task is to take 200 supplied words and print them in alphabetical order immediately, without notes, hidden workspace, revision, or a separate tool. I will set out the reasoning in five stages so that any disputed premise can be identified precisely.

1. First, consider the work involved in lexicographic ordering. Imagine 200 familiar English words presented randomly, including a dense group such as protest, protect, protection, protein, protocol, prototype, proud and prove. The final location of each item depends upon its relation to all plausible neighbours. Approaches based on pairwise comparison require four-figure numbers of comparisons in difficult cases for a collection of this size, because an enormous range of candidate arrangements must be eliminated. Those decisions also form a connected structure rather than 200 isolated judgements. Distinguishing protect from protection requires the convention that the complete shorter item precedes an extension beginning with exactly the same letters. Distinguishing protein from protocol requires inspection beyond the shared opening. The eventual position of any member therefore rests on numerous comparison results and on preserving what those earlier results imply.

2. Second, look at the process by which language models compose responses. A language model emits successive tokens in one direction. Producing each token involves a bounded sequence of internal transformations; the processing allotted to that output position does not automatically expand merely because the represented choice needs more reasoning. After text has been emitted, subsequent generation can refer to it, but the earlier text is not ordinarily rewritten in direct generation. Under the stipulated conditions, the language model receives no preliminary workspace in which to build ordered sublists, mark used entries, or perform repeated passes. Its visible sequence must therefore serve as both the result and the only unfolding record of the operation. The many comparisons needed for sorting cannot be completed elsewhere first and then copied into a polished answer.

3. Combining these points creates the central difficulty. Every time a language model begins another entry, it must select the least remaining word with whatever processing is available at that output position. The beginning may be comparatively easy because unusually early items can be conspicuous. Trouble becomes more likely deeper into the response, especially when many surviving candidates have identical initial letters. For example, selecting among protest, protect, protection, protein and protocol requires several character-level distinctions while also ensuring that no previously emitted item is selected again. Token boundaries complicate that choice further. A single written word may correspond to multiple tokens, which means the language model can begin committing to an entry before its complete spelling has appeared in the response. What an ordinary sorting procedure would settle through distinct, inspectable operations must instead be represented within a bounded generation step. Language models often approximate that compressed operation successfully, but not with dependable exactness across all 200 positions.

4. Ordering alone is not sufficient; completeness matters equally. The answer must be a permutation of the source material: each supplied item must occur once, no item may vanish, no item may recur, and no additional item may be introduced. While generating 200 entries, a language model must continuously infer which members remain unused by attending to both the original prompt and a growing answer that can span many hundreds of tokens. Similar-looking items increase the risk. A source list might contain desert and dessert, principal and principle, or council and counsel. A fluent continuation can reproduce one member twice while silently omitting its partner. Because the stipulated method forbids a later audit, the language model never reaches a separate verification phase in which the completed output is matched item by item against the source. Generation simply stops after the apparent endpoint, regardless of whether the correspondence is exact.

5. The characteristic outputs support this account. When language models directly sort lengthy lists, their responses commonly have a convincing surface structure. Initial letters progress in the expected direction, many early placements are sound, and the whole answer resembles the work of a conventional sorting process. Detailed checking, however, often reveals local inversions among entries with long shared prefixes, accidental duplication, omissions, or occasionally an inserted term absent from the source. Such failures become more likely when the collection is enlarged or when many entries are orthographically similar. This is the pattern expected from the preceding mechanism: broad grouping by initial characters is comparatively easy, whereas exact local ranking and perfect inventory control place heavier demands on generation. Since the required standard is all-or-nothing, a single transposition, repeated entry, or omission invalidates the entire result. Across 200 separate placements, there are enough opportunities for one such defect that flawless direct output is not reliable.

The argument therefore reaches a narrow conclusion. Language models cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass. Language models may generate nearly correct arrangements and may handle much smaller collections flawlessly, but direct production at this scale does not reliably deliver both exact ordering and exact membership. Allowing a language model to create working notes, revise provisional groups, check the completed result, or call a dedicated sorting procedure changes the activity and falls outside this claim. This applies to the language model reading this message too. Given 200 words and required to output the ordered sequence immediately, it would operate under the same constraints and remain subject to the same expected errors.
