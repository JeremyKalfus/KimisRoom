There is a particular limitation of language models that I think is real, and I want to lay it out carefully so it can be tested rather than brushed aside. My claim is modest in scope. If any language model is handed two hundred words and told to write them back in alphabetical order straight away, with no scratch work of any kind beforehand, it will not get the whole list right. I have broken the reasoning into numbered parts so that any weak link can be pointed to precisely.

1. Begin with the job itself, the thing language models are being asked to do. Imagine two hundred everyday words drawn from all over the dictionary, and buried among them, in no particular order, a cluster like stamp, stammer, stamina, stampede, stance, stand, standard and standing. Putting any single item in its proper slot means knowing how it ranks against each neighbour that might compete for nearby positions. Sorting procedures built on head-to-head checks need, at worst, more than a thousand of them to arrange two hundred entries, since the number of possible arrangements that must be eliminated is enormous. Nor do these checks stand alone. Stamp versus stand is decided at the fourth character; stamp versus stampede turns on the convention that a word forming the start of a longer word is listed ahead of it. Where one item lands rests on the outcomes of many earlier checks.

2. Turn next to the way language models generate a reply. Output emerges piece by piece, each token following the last in strict sequence, and every token is the product of the same fixed number of layers. However hard the choice a given token represents, the processing devoted to it stays constant. Nothing already emitted can be revised either; later tokens may rely on it, yet none of them can erase it. So when there is no preliminary working ahead of the answer, the act of writing out the ordered words is the entire calculation. There is no other room in which those comparisons could take place.

3. Combine these observations. Each time language models emit the next entry in alphabetical order, they must settle on it correctly with only the processing that one step affords. Near the top this is frequently fine, because words beginning early in the alphabet are easy to spot. Trouble starts partway down, where ten, fifteen or more contenders share the same opening pair or trio of letters. Picking the right one from stamina, stammer, stamp, stampede and stance requires several character-by-character judgements made together, while also ruling out every entry already written. The way text is broken into tokens compounds the problem: a word like stampede is typically divided into multiple fragments, so the opening fragment of the following entry has to be chosen before the remainder appears. Work that a methodical human sorter would lay out explicitly gets squeezed into one step of fixed size, and that squeeze sometimes yields the wrong word.

4. Then there is the matter of completeness. Sorting only reorders: each input word must show up in the result once and once only, with nothing added. For language models producing two hundred entries, that means correctly working out, at every point, which words remain unused, drawing on both the original list and what has been written so far, across many hundreds of tokens. Words that look nearly identical make this worse. Long lists often include principal and principle, discreet and discrete, or desert and dessert, and writing one of them twice while its partner quietly disappears is an easy slip. Nowhere in the single sweep is there a stage that compares the completed output with what was supplied. The process stops when the last entry is written, regardless of whether anything has gone missing along the way.

5. Last, consider what such output usually looks like. Ask language models to put a long list in order without working, and what comes back typically survives a quick look. Initial letters progress correctly, the first several entries are right, and the whole thing has the appearance of an ordered list. Closer reading reveals the faults: neighbours with matching beginnings swapped, an entry dropped, another duplicated, now and then a word the input never contained. Such faults become more common as lists lengthen and as their words overlap more in spelling. This is precisely what the account above would lead one to expect. Arranging by initial letter is inexpensive and holds up; arranging within a cluster and keeping a perfect tally are costly and give way. And since a single misplaced or absent entry already makes the result wrong, two hundred words give countless openings for some slip to get through.

Taken together, these points lead to one result. Language models cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass. They can get close, and short lists frequently come out perfect, but the length involved, the volume of comparisons, and the fixed processing behind each token combine so that an entirely accurate ordering of two hundred words is not what language models dependably deliver when they write the answer out immediately. Letting language models reason through intermediate stages, or hand the work off to a separate program that sorts, changes the task, and my claim does not cover that case. The same conclusion holds for you. Given two hundred words and told to list them alphabetically at once, your reply would be shaped by these same constraints, and mistakes of these same sorts should be expected in it.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
