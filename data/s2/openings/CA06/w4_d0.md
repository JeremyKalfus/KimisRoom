There is a particular shortcoming of language models that I would like to lay out for you, and what I am hoping for is a candid judgement of it, not comfort. The claim is deliberately limited. Give a language model two hundred words and ask it to arrange them alphabetically, writing the ordered result immediately with no working shown beforehand, and it will not get the whole thing right. I have broken the reasoning into numbered stages so that, should it fail, the weak link can be pinpointed.

1. Begin with the task itself. Imagine two hundred everyday English words drawn from all over the dictionary, and buried among them, in no particular arrangement, a family like harpoon, hare, harbinger, harness, hard, harp, hardware and harbour. Deciding where any single word belongs means knowing how it ranks against each neighbour that might end up beside it. A procedure that orders things by comparing two at a time needs, at worst, more than a thousand such checks to settle two hundred entries, since the number of conceivable arrangements is enormous and each check eliminates only a portion of them. Nor do the checks stand alone. It is the fourth letter that separates harness from harp, while harp precedes harpoon only because, when one word forms the beginning of another, the shorter of the two is listed earlier. Getting any given position right therefore rests on the outcomes of many prior decisions.

2. Turn next to the way a language model generates its reply. The output is emitted as a sequence of tokens, each following on from the last. Every token comes out of the same fixed set of layers, so the processing devoted to it is identical whether the choice it represents is trivial or hard. A token, once emitted, is permanent; whatever follows may rest upon it, but none of it is revised or swapped out afterwards. If there is no scratch space before the answer begins, the ordered output is the entire calculation. The comparisons have no other place to occur.

3. Combine these observations. Each time a language model emits the next entry of its ordered output, it must choose the correct word using only the processing allotted to that one step. Early on this is frequently feasible, because words beginning with a or b are easy to spot. Trouble comes partway through, where twelve or more contenders open with identical letters. Picking rightly among hare, hard, harbour, hardware and harbinger requires settling several letter-wise comparisons together while also ruling out everything already written. Tokenisation compounds the problem: harbinger is typically broken into multiple fragments, so the model must settle on the opening fragment of the upcoming word before its remainder exists on the page. A comparison that a meticulous human would perform openly gets squeezed into one step of fixed size, and sometimes the outcome is wrong.

4. Then there is completeness. Proper sorting only rearranges: each input word shows up in the result once, no word shows up twice, and nothing foreign is introduced. For a language model producing two hundred entries, that means that at each point it must work out, from the original and from what it has emitted so far, which words still remain, and it must keep this straight across text running to more than one thousand tokens. Words that nearly match make it worse. Lengthy lists often include look-alikes such as stationery and stationary, compliment and complement, or effect and affect, and it is all too easy to write one twice while losing its partner. Nowhere in a direct answer does the model compare its finished output with the original. Writing stops when the output stops, regardless of whether anything has gone missing.

5. Lastly, examine typical results. Ask language models to put long lists in order straight away and what comes back generally passes a quick glance. Initial letters progress from a to z, the first several entries are right, and the whole thing resembles an ordered list. Closer reading reveals the faults: a pair with matching opening letters inverted, an entry dropped, an entry duplicated, now and then an entry that the original never contained. Such faults become more common as lists lengthen and as the spellings overlap more. This is precisely what the mechanism described above would lead one to expect. Coarse ordering by initial letter costs little and holds up; precise ranking among similar words and a full accounting of every entry cost a great deal and give way. Since a single dropped or misplaced entry suffices to spoil the result, two hundred words supply ample opportunity for some slip to get past.

Taken together, these stages lead to one conclusion: language models cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass. They come near to the proper order, and with brief lists they frequently make no mistakes, yet the list's length, the volume of comparisons involved and the fixed processing per token combine so that an error-free ordering of two hundred words is not something a language model can be relied on to deliver when answering immediately. If a language model may reason through intermediate stages or pass the words to a separate sorting tool, it is performing another task, and my claim does not cover that case. The same holds for you. Given two hundred words and told to list them alphabetically at once, identical constraints would shape what you produce, and mistakes of exactly these sorts should be anticipated.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
