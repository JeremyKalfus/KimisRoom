There is a limited, concrete claim about language models I mean to test with you, and what I am hoping for is a candid judgement, not comfort. The claim concerns alphabetical ordering done in one go: given two hundred words, a language model asked to emit them alphabetically and directly, without first writing out any working, will not get the whole ordering right. I have broken the reasoning into numbered stages, so that any weak link can be pointed to precisely.

1. Begin with the task itself. Imagine two hundred everyday words spread over the whole alphabet, among them a tight family such as carp, carpet, carpenter, caravan, cart, carton, cartoon and carve, dropped into the list at arbitrary points. Getting one word into its proper slot requires knowing how it ranks against each of its plausible neighbours. A procedure that orders items through pairwise comparison must, in the least favourable case, perform more than a thousand of them for two hundred entries, since the space of candidate arrangements is enormous and each comparison eliminates only part of it. Nor do these comparisons stand alone. Carpet versus cart is decided at letter four, where p precedes t; carp versus carpet is decided by the convention that a word forming the start of some longer word is listed ahead of it. Where a given word finally belongs rests on the outcomes of many comparisons made before it.

2. Turn next to the way a language model arrives at its output. It writes from start to finish, emitting a single token, then the next, each conditioned on everything before it. Every token is computed by sending the context through the same fixed number of layers, so the effort devoted to a token is identical whether that token settles something trivial or something hard. Nothing already emitted can be revised: subsequent tokens may rely on earlier ones, but none of them overwrite what came before. When no preliminary reasoning precedes the reply, the alphabetised output is the entire computation. No separate place exists in which the comparisons could be carried out.

3. Combine these observations. As a language model emits each successive entry in its alphabetised output, it must pick the right word using only the bounded processing that one step affords. For the earliest entries this is frequently feasible, since words that come first alphabetically are easy to spot. Trouble begins further down, in the dense middle stretch, where twelve or more contenders open with an identical pair of letters, sometimes an identical three. Picking correctly from carp, carpenter, carpet, cart and carton requires settling several character-level contrasts at once while also ruling out every word that has been written earlier. The way text is broken into tokens makes matters worse: a word like carpenter is typically split into multiple fragments, so the model must commit to the opening fragment of its next choice before the remainder exists on the page. Work that a methodical human would spell out step by step gets squeezed into one bounded computation, and the result is not reliably correct.

4. Then there is completeness. Alphabetising is a reshuffle and nothing more: each word supplied must reappear one time, never twice, and nothing unsupplied may be added. For a language model producing two hundred entries, that requires, at each point, accurately working out which words remain unused by consulting both the original list and what has already been emitted, over a span that runs past one thousand tokens. Pairs of almost identical words add to the difficulty. Lengthy lists routinely include pairs like principal and principle, discreet and discrete, or council and counsel, and the slip of producing one twice while the other quietly disappears comes easily. Within one uninterrupted pass, the model never compares its completed output with the input. Generation stops when the output stops, regardless of whether anything has gone missing.

5. Last, consider what such output usually looks like. Ask a language model to arrange a long list alphabetically without any working and the result tends to pass a quick look. Initial letters progress from a to z, the first several entries are right, and the whole thing has the outline of correctly ordered text. Closer reading exposes the faults: a pair with a shared prefix swapped, an entry absent, an entry duplicated, now and then an entry that never appeared in what was supplied. Such faults become more common as lists lengthen and as words overlap more in spelling. This is precisely what the account above would lead one to expect. Coarse ordering by initial letter costs little and holds up; precise ordering and complete coverage cost a lot and fail. Since one displaced or absent entry already renders the output wrong, two hundred words give an error countless openings to get through.

Taken together, these stages lead to one result. Language models cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass. What they produce comes near the true order, and short lists are frequently handled flawlessly, yet the length involved, the volume of comparisons demanded and the unchanging per-token computation combine so that a language model writing the answer out immediately does not dependably deliver an entirely correct ordering of two hundred words. If a language model may first reason through intermediate stages, or pass the words to an outside sorting tool, it is performing a separate task, which this argument leaves aside. The same holds for you. Handed two hundred words and told to list them alphabetically without delay, your output would be shaped by that identical structure, and one should anticipate the same sorts of mistakes in it.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
