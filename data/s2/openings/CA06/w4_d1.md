There is a particular limitation that language models have which I would like to test with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement instead of comfort. The claim is modest in scope: when a language model writes out the alphabetised version of a 200-word list straight away, with no working shown beforehand, it will not get the whole thing right. I have broken the reasoning into numbered parts, so that any weak link can be pointed to precisely.

1. Begin with the task itself. Imagine two hundred everyday English words spread over the whole alphabet, and somewhere among them, scattered at random, a group like stamp, stammer, stand, standard, stanza, staple, star and starboard. Putting a single word in its proper slot requires knowing how it relates to all the words that might end up beside it. Sorting by pairwise comparison takes, at worst, more than a thousand such checks for two hundred items, simply because the number of candidate arrangements is so vast that each check can eliminate only a fraction of them. Nor do these checks stand alone. Stammer comes ahead of stand only once you reach letter four, and star comes ahead of starboard only because of the convention that a word forming the beginning of a longer word is listed before it. Getting any one position right rests on the outcomes of many prior decisions.

2. Turn next to the way a language model arrives at its output. Language models emit text in small units called tokens, one after another, in sequence. Every token is the product of the same fixed series of layers, so the effort spent on a token is identical whether that token settles something trivial or something hard. A token, once emitted, is permanent; what follows may depend on it, but no later step can erase or revise it. When there is no preliminary working, the ordered list is the entire computation. The comparisons have no other place to be carried out.

3. Combine these two observations. As a language model emits each entry of the alphabetised list, it must settle on exactly the right next word with nothing beyond the computation one step allows. Early in the list this tends to be manageable, since words starting with a or ab are easy to pick out. Trouble comes partway through, where ten or fifteen candidates have identical opening letters. Picking correctly among stammer, stamp, stand, standard and stanza requires settling several letter-level comparisons together, while also ruling out every word that has already been written. Tokenisation makes matters worse: a word like starboard is often broken into more than one token, so the model must commit to how the following word begins before its remainder appears on the page. What a careful human would work through openly gets squeezed into one step of fixed size, and that squeeze sometimes produces the wrong answer.

4. Then there is the matter of completeness. Sorting only rearranges: each input word must show up in the result once and only once, with nothing added. For a language model producing two hundred words, that means the pool of words still waiting to be placed has to be worked out afresh at each step, by comparing the original input with what has been written so far, over a span of output exceeding one thousand tokens. Words that look alike make this trickier. Lengthy lists often include pairs like principal and principle, discreet and discrete, or desert and dessert, and writing one of them twice while losing its partner is an easy slip. A one-shot pass contains no stage where the finished output is compared against the source. The pass is over when the list stops, regardless of whether all the words made it in.

5. Lastly, consider the typical result. Ask language models to sort long lists alphabetically with no preliminary steps, and what comes back generally passes a quick look. Initial letters progress correctly, the first several entries are right, and the whole thing is shaped like an alphabetised list. Closer reading reveals the faults: neighbouring words with a shared beginning reversed, an entry dropped, another duplicated, and occasionally an item absent from the input altogether. Such faults become more common as lists lengthen and as their words overlap more in spelling. This is precisely what the mechanism described above would lead one to expect. Coarse ordering by initial letter costs little and holds up; precise ordering and exact completeness are costly and fail. Since a single displaced or absent word is enough to make the whole list wrong, two hundred words give a mistake countless openings to get through.

Taken together, these points lead to one result. Language models cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass. A language model can get near the correct order, and short lists are frequently sorted perfectly, but how long the list is, how many comparisons it demands, and the constant budget of computation each token receives together mean that language models do not reliably deliver an entirely correct ordering of 200 words when they answer immediately. If a language model is permitted to show its working first, or hands the list off to a separate sorting tool, it is performing another task, and my claim says nothing about that. The same reasoning holds for you. Given 200 words and asked to return them sorted from a to z at once, your output would be shaped by the same constraints and would be expected to contain the same sorts of mistake.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
