I would like to lay out a line of reasoning about one concrete limitation of language models, and I am hoping for a frank verdict on it, not comfort. The thesis is deliberately modest. When a language model is handed 200 words and writes them out in alphabetical order straight away, without any notes or working produced first, the result will not be fully correct. I have split the reasoning into numbered parts so that, should it fail, the faulty part can be identified.

1. Begin with the demands of the job itself. Imagine 200 everyday English words in no particular order, spread from A to Z, with a tight family like stable, stack, stadium, staff, stag, stage, stagger and stain buried at scattered points. Putting a single word in its proper slot requires knowing how it ranks against every rival that might end up beside it. A procedure that works by comparing two words at a time must, at worst, perform upward of 1,200 such comparisons on 200 items, simply because the number of arrangements it has to narrow down is so enormous. Nor do those comparisons stand alone. Staff precedes stag because f comes before g in position four. Stag precedes stage for a different reason: no letter differs, and when one word is the opening of another, the shorter is listed earlier. Getting any one word right rests on the outcomes of many comparisons made before it.

2. Turn next to the way a language model arrives at its reply. Output is emitted in small units called tokens, each following the last. Every token is the product of the same fixed series of layers, and the quantity of processing devoted to a token is identical whether the choice it represents is trivial or very hard. A token, once emitted, is permanent; what follows may rely on it, but none of the later steps can revise or erase it. When the answer is produced directly, with no preliminary reasoning, the alphabetised output is all the computation there is. The comparisons have no other place in which to be carried out.

3. Combine these two observations. As a language model emits each entry of the ordered list, it must settle on the right successor using only the processing that one step affords. Early on this is usually feasible, because words beginning with a or b tend to be conspicuous. The trouble comes partway through, where ten or fifteen candidates may begin with identical letters. Picking correctly among stable, stack, stadium, staff and stag requires settling several letter-by-letter contests together while also ruling out everything that has already been written. Tokenisation compounds the problem: a word like stadium is often broken into multiple fragments, so the model must commit to the opening fragment of the next entry before the remainder exists on the page. Work that a methodical person would do openly, one comparison after another, gets squeezed into a single step of fixed size, and that squeeze sometimes produces the wrong choice.

4. Then there is the matter of completeness. Sorting is a reshuffle, nothing more: each of the original words must show up in the result once and only once, and no outsider may slip in. For a language model producing 200 entries, that means continually working out which words remain unplaced by consulting both the original list and its own partial answer, throughout a stretch exceeding 1,000 tokens. Words that closely resemble one another make this harder still. Lengthy lists routinely include pairs like principal and principle, desert and dessert, or accept and except, and it is easy to write one of them twice while omitting its partner. Nowhere in a direct answer does the model compare its completed output with what it was given. Generation stops when the list stops, regardless of whether anything was lost along the way.

5. Last, consider how such output typically appears. Ask language models to put a long list in alphabetical order directly, and what comes back generally passes a quick look. The initial letters progress from a to z, the first several entries are right, and the whole thing has the outline of an alphabetised list. Closer reading reveals the faults: a pair of words with a common beginning in reversed order, an entry absent, an entry duplicated, now and then a word that the original never contained. Such faults become more common as lists lengthen and as their words overlap more in spelling. This is precisely what the mechanism above would lead one to expect. Arranging by initial letter costs little and holds up; precise ordering and complete coverage cost a great deal and fail. Since a single displaced or omitted word makes the whole list wrong, 200 entries give errors a great many openings, and it would be surprising if none got through.

My conclusion comes out of these parts. Language models cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass. They can get near the correct arrangement, and short lists are often handled flawlessly, yet the length involved, the volume of comparisons and the fixed processing per token combine so that a perfect ordering of 200 words is not what a language model can be counted on to produce when it answers immediately. A language model permitted to reason through intermediate stages, or to pass the words to an outside sorting tool, is performing a separate task, and my claim does not extend to it. The same conclusion covers you. Were you handed 200 words and told to list them alphabetically at once, your reply would be shaped by the same constraints, and similar mistakes should be expected in it.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
