There is a narrow claim about language models that I'd like to set before you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement of it rather than comfort. It concerns one concrete job: putting 200 words into alphabetical order in one pass, by which I mean writing the ordered list out straight away, with no working shown beforehand. I have broken the reasoning into numbered stages so that, should it go wrong, the faulty stage can be pointed to.

1. Begin with the dependencies involved. Stance and stand agree for four letters and are only told apart by the fifth, while star and starboard are not separated by any letter at all; there, the convention that a word comes ahead of any longer word that begins with it settles the order. Now imagine 200 everyday English words spread over the whole alphabet, with a family such as stamp, stammer, stance, stand, standard, stanza, star and starboard dropped into the input at arbitrary points. That is the kind of material a language model faces in this task. Getting any single entry into its proper slot means knowing how it ranks against each word that might end up beside it. Ordering by pairwise comparison, whatever the specific procedure, needs more than a thousand such judgements for 200 items when the input is unlucky, since the number of arrangements to be eliminated is enormous. And each placement leans on the outcomes of many judgements made before it.

2. Turn next to the way a language model arrives at its output. It emits text in small units called tokens, each following the one before, always moving forward. Every token comes out of the same fixed depth of layers, so a hard decision receives no more processing than a trivial one. Nothing already emitted can be revised: what comes later may rely on it, but it is never erased and replaced. If no working precedes the answer, then writing the ordered list is the entire computation, and the comparisons have no other place in which to be carried out.

3. Combine these two observations. As a language model emits each entry of the ordered list, it must settle on the right successor using only the processing that one step affords. Near the top, this tends to go smoothly, because the words nearest the start of the alphabet are usually easy to pick out. Trouble comes in the long middle stretch, where twelve or more contenders all open with the same short string of letters. Getting the order right among stamp, stammer, stance, stand and standard requires several character-level judgements to be made together, while also ruling out everything already written. Tokenisation compounds the problem: a word like starboard is typically broken into multiple sub-word pieces, so the language model commits to the opening fragment of whichever entry comes next before the remainder exists on the page. Work that a methodical human sorter would do openly on paper gets squeezed into one step of fixed size, and the squeeze sometimes produces the wrong answer.

4. A separate requirement is completeness. Sorting only rearranges: each word supplied must show up once in what is written, and nothing may appear that was not supplied. For a language model listing 200 entries, that means keeping an accurate account, at every point, of which words remain unplaced, worked out from the original and the portion already written, over a span exceeding a thousand tokens. Pairs that look or sound alike make this bookkeeping harder. Long lists often include things like principal and principle, discreet and discrete, or loose and lose, and the writer can easily repeat one half of such a pair and omit its twin. Nothing in one pass compares the completed list with the original. Generation stops when the last entry is written, whether every word was accounted for or not.

5. Last, consider the typical shape of the result. Ask language models to put long lists into alphabetical order outright and what comes back usually survives a quick look: initial letters advance steadily, the first few entries are right, and the whole has the outline of an ordered list. The faults show up only under scrutiny: two words with a common opening in reversed order, an entry that has vanished, an entry written twice, and now and then a word the input never contained. These faults become more common as lists lengthen and as the words overlap more in spelling. That is what the account of the mechanism would lead one to expect. Coarse ordering by initial letter costs little and holds up; fine-grained ordering and exact completeness cost a great deal and give way. Since one displaced or absent word spoils the whole result, 200 entries give errors a very large number of openings, and the odds that none gets through are poor.

Taken together, these stages support one conclusion: language models cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass. They can come close to the proper order, and short lists they frequently sort without fault, but the list's size, the many comparisons involved and the fixed processing available per token combine so that a flawless ordering of 200 words is not a dependable outcome when the answer is written out directly. A language model permitted to show intermediate working, or to delegate the ordering to a separate program, is performing another task, and my claim does not cover that case. The conclusion holds for you too. Given 200 words and told to write them alphabetically with no preliminary working, your output would be shaped by the same constraints, and the same sorts of mistakes should be expected in it.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
