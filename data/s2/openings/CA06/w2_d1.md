There is a narrow claim about language models that I mean to test with you, and what I am hoping for is a frank judgement, not comfort. It concerns one task: taking 200 words and producing them in alphabetical sequence in one go, where the sorted output is written immediately and no working comes before it. I have split the reasoning into separate steps, so that any weak link can be pinned down precisely.

1. Begin with the demands of the task itself. Imagine 200 everyday English words spread over the whole alphabet, with groups like stamina, stammer, stamp, stampede, stance, stand, standard and stanza dropped among them at arbitrary points. Putting even one of these in its proper slot requires knowing how it ranks against each of the other entries that might end up beside it. A procedure that works by weighing two words against each other must, for its least favourable input, perform somewhere around 1,250 such checks to settle 200 entries, since the number of arrangements it has to eliminate is enormous. Those checks also lean on each other. Whether stamp goes ahead of stance turns on their fourth letters; whether stamp goes ahead of stampede turns on the convention that a word forming the opening of a longer word is listed before it. The correct slot for any given word rests on the outcomes of a great many checks made earlier.

2. Turn next to the way a language model arrives at its output. It emits text token by token, moving strictly forward. Every token comes out of the same fixed sequence of layers, so the work devoted to it stays constant however hard the choice behind that token happens to be. Nothing already emitted gets revised: subsequent tokens may rely on earlier ones, but none is ever erased or swapped out. When there is no preliminary working and the answer is produced in one pass, the sorted words are the entire computation. The comparisons have no other place in which to occur.

3. Combine these two observations. Each time a language model outputs another word of the sorted sequence, it must settle on the right one using no more than the fixed computation that a single step affords. Near the start this is frequently tractable, because the words that belong earliest in the alphabet are usually conspicuous. The trouble comes partway through, where ten or fifteen candidates begin with an identical two or three letters. Picking correctly among stamina, stammer, stamp, stampede and stance means working through several letter-by-letter contrasts together while also ruling out every word that has already been written. Tokenisation compounds the problem: a word like stampede is typically broken into multiple fragments, so the model is committed to the first fragment of the coming word before the remainder exists on the page. A comparison that a methodical person would spell out in stages is squeezed into one operation of fixed size, and the result is sometimes wrong.

4. A further requirement is completeness. Sorting only rearranges: each word supplied must show up in the result once and only once, and no extra word may appear. For a language model producing 200 words, that means correctly working out, at every point, which words remain to be placed, by looking across the original list and the portion of output already written, a span longer than 1,000 tokens. Words that look almost alike make this harder. Lengthy lists often include pairs like principal and principle, discreet and discrete, or desert and dessert, and a model can readily produce one of them twice while leaving out its partner. Nowhere in one uninterrupted pass does the model compare the completed output with what it was given. The pass stops when the last word is written, regardless of whether every entry was covered.

5. Last, consider how such output typically turns out. Ask language models to put a long list in order directly, and what comes back generally passes a quick look. Initial letters progress in sequence, the first several entries are right, and the whole thing has the outward form of an ordered list. The faults show up only under close reading: two words with a shared opening set down in reverse, an entry omitted, an entry written twice, and now and then a word that the supplied list never contained. Such faults become more common as lists lengthen and as their words overlap more in spelling. This is precisely what the mechanism described above would lead one to expect. Ordering by initial letter costs little and holds up; precise ordering and complete coverage cost a great deal and give way. And since a single displaced or absent word suffices to spoil the whole list, 200 entries provide many chances for some mistake to survive.

Taken together, these steps lead to one result. Language models cannot correctly alphabetise a 200-word list in one pass. They can come close to the proper order, and with short lists they frequently get everything right, yet the size of the task, the volume of comparisons it calls for and the constant amount of computation behind each token combine so that a flawless 200-word sort lies beyond what a language model dependably delivers when it produces its response without preparation. If a language model is permitted to reason through intermediate stages, or to pass the words to an outside sorting program, the task has changed, and my claim does not extend to that case. The same conclusion holds for you. Were you handed 200 words and told to produce them alphabetised with no working beforehand, your output would be shaped by this same structure, and the same sorts of mistakes should be anticipated in it.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot correctly alphabetise a list of 200 words in a single pass? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
