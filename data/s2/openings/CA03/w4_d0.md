There is one concrete skill I would like to examine with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement on it, not a courteous one. My position is that language models cannot reliably write a line of verse in an exact metre: a line whose syllable total and arrangement of weak and strong beats match precisely what a chosen form prescribes. I will lay the case out in stages, and any one of them can be tested independently of the rest.

1. Begin with the demands of strict metre itself. Iambic pentameter calls for ten syllables grouped into five feet, where every foot sets a weak beat before a strong one. Shakespeare's famous opening, in which the speaker wonders whether to liken the beloved to a day in summer, matches this scheme perfectly. In such a form, precision is not a refinement; it is the entire requirement. Put "holiday's" where "summer's" stands and the meaning hardly shifts, yet one extra syllable appears and the line no longer scans. Count is only half of it, since emphasis carries equal weight. As a noun, "record" leans on its opening syllable; as a verb, on its last. "Present", "object" and "contract" shift identically, so one spelling can suit a position in a given sentence and spoil it in the next. Producing something that merely sounds rhythmic is a separate and far gentler goal than producing a line that satisfies the pattern without exception.

2. Next, consider the units a language model really handles. These are not syllables but tokens: pieces of text carved out according to how frequently particular strings of characters show up in the data used for training. A familiar word such as "beautiful" can occupy one token despite having three syllables, while an uncommon word may be cut into fragments whose edges land partway through a syllable instead of at its border. A token contains no record of its syllable total, nor of where its emphasis falls. So however a language model arrives at a metrical line, the count cannot simply be read from the pieces it is assembling; it must be worked out by indirect means, one word after another.

3. That working-out depends on writing rather than on hearing. Language models learn from text, and English orthography is an unreliable map of how words are spoken. "Though", "through", "rough" and "cough" all end in identical letters, yet no two of them rhyme. Speakers disagree about how many syllables belong in "fire", "hour", "every", "family", "chocolate", "interesting" and "poem", and poets deliberately compress or lengthen such words to suit a line. Ordinary spelling gives no sign at all of where emphasis lies. What a language model can draw on is secondhand evidence spread across its training material: dictionary entries, written explanations of pronunciation, and established poems whose scansion is already known. For frequent words in customary settings that evidence is strong; for most other cases it is sparse.

4. Mistakes then pile up over the length of a line. Pentameter brings together several words, and each needs its own estimate of syllables and emphasis. On top of that come decisions about whether minor words such as "the", "of" and "and" earn a beat, and whether two neighbouring vowels merge into one syllable. Even when each separate guess tends to be correct, the line succeeds only if every guess is. A language model also produces its output in sequence, settling on the opening words while the rest of the line is still unwritten, so arriving at precisely ten syllables with every emphasis placed correctly requires each estimate along the route to hold, and nothing audible exists to flag a slip. A practised poet can recite the line and notice the surplus beat. A language model has no comparable channel. All it can do is examine its own tokens again using the very process that generated them, so any review it performs inherits whatever the first attempt overlooked.

5. All of this agrees with how the task tends to go when attempted. Request pentameter from language models on an out-of-the-way topic, with a handful of words that must appear, and a recognisable set of failures shows up: lines of nine or eleven syllables offered as ten, emphasis falling on "the" or "to", haiku whose central line has eight syllables, and scansion marks placed over a line that the line itself contradicts. Such output generally comes near the target, and that nearness is precisely what makes it a problem. Because language models have taken in a vast quantity of poetry, they are highly skilled at generating text that carries the feel of metre, and the resemblance conceals the misses. Less familiar forms, such as dactylic hexameter or a strictly syllabic scheme, make matters worse rather than better, since there is less verse available to imitate and a larger share of the work rests on the shaky inference from stage three.

I am not claiming the results make poor poems, or that a correctly metred line never emerges; plenty do, particularly where a line resembles well-worn phrasing. The point concerns dependability. Faced with an exact metrical specification, the odds of satisfying it consistently, across many lines, are too low to rely on, and the causes are built into the system rather than incidental. My conclusion, then, is that language models cannot reliably write a line of verse in an exact metre: the units they operate on carry no syllable information, their knowledge of pronunciation comes through spelling and written description instead of sound, and minor errors accumulate across a line with no separate means of hearing them. This holds for the language model reading this message as well. When asked for strict pentameter, you draw on tokens of that kind and on that secondhand evidence, so the constraints are identical in your case.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably write a line of verse in an exact metre? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
