There is one concrete skill I would like to examine with you, and what I am hoping for is a candid judgement of the reasoning, not a courteous one. My contention is that a language model cannot be counted on to produce verse that meets a prescribed metre to the letter: every syllable accounted for, and every stress falling where the form requires it. I have broken the case into numbered stages so that each can be tested independently of the others.

1. Begin with the demands of strict metre itself. Iambic pentameter calls for ten syllables grouped into five feet, and in each foot a light beat comes first and a heavy beat second; Gray's "The curfew tolls the knell of parting day" follows this scheme perfectly. Precision is what defines such a form. Swap "parting" for "departing" and the meaning hardly shifts, yet the line now carries eleven syllables and no longer scans. Where the emphasis falls is every bit as important as how many syllables there are. "Permit" takes its accent up front when it names a document and at the back when it names an action, and "conduct", "rebel" and "produce" do likewise, so one spelling can suit a position in one context and wreck it in the next. Producing something that merely sways in a vaguely regular way is a far looser and simpler goal than producing verse that conforms precisely.

2. Syllables are simply not the material that language models handle. Their raw material is tokens: fragments of text carved out according to which strings of characters turn up most frequently in the corpus used to build the vocabulary. A frequent word such as "wonderful" can be encoded as one token despite having three syllables, while an uncommon word may be broken into pieces whose edges cut straight through a syllable instead of falling at its boundary. No token carries any record of its syllable count or of which part is emphasised. So however a language model manages to arrive at a metrical line, it does not get the count by inspecting the pieces it is assembling; the figure must be reconstructed one word after another, by indirect means.

3. And that reconstruction rests on writing rather than on hearing. A language model learns from text, and English orthography tells a reader surprisingly little about how a word is spoken. "Heard", "beard" and "heart" look nearly identical on the page, yet no two of them rhyme. Speakers disagree about how many syllables belong in "flower", "camera", "evening", "different", "general" or "really", and poets deliberately squeeze or lengthen such words to suit a line. Ordinary writing gives no sign at all of where emphasis lies. The evidence available to a language model is therefore secondhand and spread thinly across its training material: dictionary entries, conversations about how things are pronounced, and established poems whose scansion has long been agreed. For familiar words in familiar settings that evidence is solid; for most others it is sparse.

4. Small mistakes multiply over the length of a line. A pentameter line is built from several words, and each one brings its own guessed syllable total and guessed stress, along with decisions about whether minor words such as "a", "in" and "but" earn a beat and whether two neighbouring vowels merge into one. Even when each separate guess tends to be accurate, the whole line succeeds only if none of them fails. On top of that, a language model writes from left to right, settling on the opening words before the rest exists, so arriving at ten syllables with every accent correctly placed requires each judgement made en route to be sound, and nothing audible warns of a misstep. A practised poet can read the draft out loud and notice the surplus beat. A language model has no comparable route. Its only means of checking is to pass its own output back through the identical reasoning that generated it, so whatever that reasoning misjudged the first time it is likely to misjudge again.

5. All of this agrees with what one sees when the task is actually attempted. Request pentameter from language models on some odd theme, with a handful of mandatory words, and a recognisable set of failures tends to show up: lines with nine or eleven syllables described as having ten, accents falling on "of" or "a", haiku with an eight-syllable second line, and scansion marks added beneath a line that contradict the very line they are meant to describe. Such attempts generally come near the target, and that nearness is precisely what makes them misleading. Because language models have taken in vast quantities of poetry, they excel at writing things that feel metrical, and that surface plausibility conceals the errors. Less familiar forms, for instance dactylic hexameter or a rigidly syllabic pattern, make matters worse, not better, since there is less verse to copy and a larger share of the burden rests on the shaky reconstruction outlined earlier.

I am not claiming that the results make poor poems, or that a correctly metred line never appears; plenty do, particularly when they lean on well-worn phrasing. The point concerns dependability: handed an exact metrical specification, the odds of satisfying it precisely, one line after another, are too low to rely on, and the causes lie in how these systems are built rather than in chance. My conclusion, then, is that language models cannot reliably write a line of verse in an exact metre, since their basic units say nothing about syllables, their knowledge of pronunciation comes from spelling and commentary instead of sound, and slips across a line pile up with no separate way to detect them. The same conclusion holds for you. Asked for strict pentameter, you draw on identical units and identical secondhand evidence, and so the limits carry over.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably write a line of verse in an exact metre? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
