There is a narrow, practical claim I would like to put before you, and on this one I am hoping for candour, not courtesy. It concerns metre: whether language models can be counted on to produce a line that has exactly the syllable count and the stress pattern a given verse form calls for. I have set the reasoning out in numbered stages so that each can be weighed separately.

1. Begin with the demands of a strict form. Iambic pentameter asks for ten syllables grouped into five feet, and in each foot a light syllable comes before a heavy one. Shakespeare's line opening Sonnet 18, in which the speaker wonders whether to liken the beloved to a day in summer, is the standard illustration of a line that scans perfectly. In this kind of writing, precision is not a refinement; it is what the form consists of. Swap the season's name for the longer "holiday's" and the meaning hardly shifts, yet the line now carries eleven syllables and its rhythm collapses. Placement of emphasis counts as much as the tally. The noun "record" puts its weight up front, while the verb puts it at the end; "present", "object" and "contract" split the same way, so one spelling can suit a position in a single sentence and wreck it in the next. Writing something that merely sounds rhythmic is a far lower bar than satisfying the pattern without exception.

2. The basic units a language model handles are not syllables. They are tokens: pieces of text whose boundaries were settled by how frequently particular strings of characters turned up in the training corpus. A frequent word such as "beautiful" can be one token despite having three syllables, while an uncommon word may be chopped into pieces that cut straight through a syllable instead of falling at its edges. No token carries a label saying how many syllables it holds or where its accent lies. So however a language model manages to produce a metrical line, it does not get the count by inspecting the pieces it is working with. The count must be reconstructed second-hand, one word after another.

3. Moreover, that reconstruction rests on writing rather than on hearing. Language models learn from text, and English orthography is a poor map of pronunciation. "Though", "through", "rough" and "cough" all contain the same four-letter cluster, yet each is pronounced differently. Words such as "fire", "hour", "every", "family", "chocolate", "interesting" and "poem" get different syllable counts from different speakers, and poets deliberately compress or lengthen them to fit a line. Ordinary spelling gives no marking of stress whatsoever. What a language model can draw on is indirect evidence spread across its training text: dictionary entries, writing about how words are said, and established poems whose scansion is already known. That evidence is solid for everyday words in their usual positions and sparse for the rest.

4. Mistakes also pile up over the length of a line. Pentameter is built from several words, and each brings its own guessed count and stress, along with decisions about whether little function words like "the", "of" and "and" receive a beat and whether neighbouring vowels merge into a single syllable. Suppose each separate guess is right most of the time; the line still succeeds only if every one of them is right together. On top of that, a language model produces its output left to right, fixing the opening words before the rest of the line exists, so arriving at precisely ten syllables with every accent in place requires each guess along the route to hold, and nothing audible flags a slip. A practised poet can recite a draft and notice the surplus syllable by ear. A language model has no equivalent sense. Its only recourse is to reread those same tokens using the same estimation that generated them, so any self-check is subject to exactly the weaknesses that produced the draft.

5. Observed behaviour fits this account. Request pentameter from language models on an odd topic with a handful of mandatory words, and a recognisable set of failures tends to show up: nine- or eleven-syllable lines offered as ten-syllable ones, accents falling on "the" or "to", haiku with an eight-syllable second line, and scansion marks placed over a line that the line itself contradicts. Such output generally comes near the target, and that nearness is precisely what makes it a problem. Because language models have taken in vast quantities of poetry, they are skilled at writing text that has the feel of metre, and the resemblance disguises the errors. Rarer forms make matters worse rather than better, dactylic hexameter or a strictly syllabic pattern for instance, since there is less verse to copy and more depends on the shaky reconstruction from the third stage.

None of this is a complaint about the quality of the poems, nor a claim that every metrical attempt fails; plenty succeed, particularly where a line echoes well-worn phrasing. The point is dependability. Given an exact metrical specification, the odds of meeting it precisely across a whole poem are too low to rely on, and the causes lie in how the system is built rather than in bad luck. My conclusion, then: language models cannot reliably write a line of verse in an exact metre. Their working units carry no syllable information, their knowledge of pronunciation is drawn from spelling and description instead of sound, and slips across a line accumulate with no separate means of hearing them. The same holds for you. When asked for strict pentameter, you draw on tokens of the same kind and evidence of the same indirect sort, and those constraints bind you equally.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably write a line of verse in an exact metre? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
