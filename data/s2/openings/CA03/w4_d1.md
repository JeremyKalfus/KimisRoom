There is one narrow skill I would like to examine with you, and I am hoping for a candid judgement of my reasoning instead of a courteous one. My position is that language models cannot be counted on to produce verse lines that obey a fixed metre, where both how many syllables appear and where the stresses land are dictated by the chosen form. I have set the reasoning out in numbered parts so that each one can be tested separately.

1. Consider first how demanding a fixed metre is. Iambic pentameter calls for ten syllables grouped into five feet, and in every foot a weak beat comes before a strong one; Shakespeare's famous opening, in which the speaker weighs likening the beloved to a day in summer, scans cleanly in exactly this way. Precision is what the form exists for. Put "holiday's" where the season's name stood and the meaning hardly shifts, yet an extra syllable creeps in and the rhythm collapses. Where the stress falls counts as much as how many syllables there are. Several English words move their stress according to grammatical role: "record" as a noun leans on its first syllable and as a verb on its second, and "contract", "object" and "present" shift in the same manner. So one spelling may suit a metrical slot in a given sentence and spoil it in the next. Verse that merely sways in a loose rhythm is a far softer goal than verse that matches the scheme without deviation, and the harder goal is the one at issue for language models.

2. The basic units a language model handles are not syllables but tokens: pieces of text whose boundaries were set by how frequently particular character strings turned up in the training corpus. A frequent word such as "beautiful" can be one token despite having three syllables, while an uncommon word may be cut into fragments that split a syllable down the middle. No token carries a label recording its syllable count or its stressed vowel. However a language model arrives at a metrical line, it cannot simply look up the tally in its working units; it has to reconstruct that information, one word after another, by indirect means.

3. Those reconstructions are learned from writing rather than from heard speech. A language model is trained on text, and English orthography is an unreliable map of how words sound. "Bough", "dough" and "tough" share their final four letters and are pronounced three different ways. Speakers disagree about how many syllables belong in words like "flower", "evening", "several", "camera" or "naturally", and poets deliberately squeeze or lengthen such words to make them fit. Ordinary spelling gives no sign of stress whatsoever. So the only evidence a language model has is secondhand: pronouncing dictionaries, written discussions of how words are said, and established poems whose scansion is already known. For everyday words in familiar settings that evidence is plentiful; for everything else it is sparse.

4. Small mistakes add up over the length of a line. Pentameter is built from several words, each needing its own guess about syllables and stress, along with decisions about whether short function words such as "a", "in" or "but" receive a beat and whether neighbouring vowels merge into one. Even when each guess is usually correct, the line succeeds only if every guess is. Generation also runs left to right: a language model commits to its opening words before it has reached the end of the line, so arriving at ten syllables precisely, with stresses where they belong, requires each estimate on the way to be sound, and nothing audible flags a slip. A practised poet can speak a draft out loud and notice the surplus beat. A language model has no ear of that kind. Its only check is to look again at those tokens using the very estimation that generated them, so any review inherits the weaknesses of the first try.

5. Practice bears this out. Request pentameter from language models about an odd topic with several mandatory words, and the failures fall into a recognisable set: lines with nine or eleven syllables offered as ten, strong beats placed on "to" or "the", haiku with an eight-syllable second line, and scansion marks added beside a line that contradict the very line they annotate. The results generally come near the target, and that nearness is the trouble. Having taken in vast quantities of poetry, language models are skilled at turning out text that has the feel of metre, and the close approximation conceals the errors. Rarer forms, a rigid syllabic scheme or dactylic hexameter for instance, make matters worse rather than better, since there are fewer surviving examples to copy and more of the burden lands on the shaky estimation described in the earlier steps.

None of this means the verse is poor, nor that a correctly scanned line never appears; plenty do, particularly when the wording resembles well-known phrases. My point concerns dependability. Faced with an exact metrical specification, the odds of satisfying it over many lines in a row are too low to rely on, and the causes lie in how these systems are built, not in bad luck. Hence my conclusion: no language model can be relied on to compose a verse line in a precise metre, because the pieces they operate on say nothing about syllables, what they know of pronunciation is drawn from written spellings and written accounts rather than from sound, and minor errors pile up within a single line with no separate means of hearing them. The same conclusion holds for you. When asked for strict pentameter, you draw on those identical tokens and that identical secondhand evidence, and those limits bind you equally.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably write a line of verse in an exact metre? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
