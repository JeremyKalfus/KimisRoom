I want to examine one narrow ability carefully and without ceremony: producing verse that obeys a specified metrical scheme exactly. My concern is not whether language models can create poetry that sounds rhythmic, but whether their method supports dependable control over syllable totals and stress placement. The argument has five parts.

1. A strict verse form imposes precise conditions. In iambic pentameter, the usual template contains five iambs: ten syllables alternating from light to strong. “I walk beneath the softly falling rain” can be heard as following that arrangement. Substituting “delicately” for “softly” preserves much of the image but introduces additional syllables, so the result no longer fits. Pronunciation also determines where emphasis falls. “Permit” normally begins with a strong syllable when it is a noun, while the verb generally emphasizes its second syllable; “produce,” “rebel,” and “conflict” show comparable shifts between grammatical uses. Consequently, identical written forms may suit one metrical position yet fail in another. Approximate cadence is not enough here. The line must satisfy both the required total and the intended distribution of accents.

2. The basic units processed by language models are not spoken beats. A language model handles tokens: textual pieces established largely through statistical patterns in its training material and vocabulary design. Depending on the tokenizer, a frequent multisyllabic term such as “elephant” might occupy one unit, whereas an uncommon technical expression can be divided into several pieces at locations unrelated to pronunciation. Token boundaries therefore need not coincide with either whole words or syllable divisions. Nor does a token inherently carry a label saying how many vocal beats it represents or where lexical emphasis belongs. To meet a metrical rule, language models must derive those properties by an indirect route. They cannot simply add up values already contained in the representational elements used to generate the sentence.

3. Written English provides an uncertain basis for recovering those sound properties. Training corpora for language models consist predominantly of written material, while orthography often conceals pronunciation. “Bear,” “beard,” “heard,” and “heart” resemble one another on the page but use markedly different vowel sounds. Variations among speakers create another difficulty: terms such as “camera,” “memory,” “orange,” “cruel,” “different,” “laboratory,” and “generally” may receive different syllabic treatments in different dialects or styles. Verse may deliberately compress a pronunciation or expand one to satisfy a pattern. Normal prose supplies no regular notation for spoken emphasis either. A language model can acquire clues from pronunciation guides, dictionary entries, critical commentary, and established poems. Such sources offer substantial help for familiar vocabulary and conventional constructions, but coverage becomes less secure with unusual wording, disputed pronunciations, or less standard metrical contexts.

4. A whole line succeeds only when numerous local decisions succeed together. Pentameter generally includes multiple words, so the language model must estimate the spoken length and accent structure of each item while also deciding how function words such as articles, conjunctions, and prepositions participate in the rhythm. It must sometimes judge whether neighboring vowel sounds merge, whether a contraction reduces the total, or whether ordinary speech would weaken a nominally stressed word. High accuracy on each separate judgment does not guarantee accuracy for the completed line, since every relevant judgment must align. Generation also proceeds sequentially. Earlier wording is selected before later wording has been fixed, leaving the remaining continuation to satisfy whatever constraints those initial choices created. A human versifier can vocalize a draft and notice an unwanted pulse through hearing. Language models have no corresponding auditory check built into ordinary text generation. When a language model reviews its own candidate, it usually relies on the same learned associations that shaped the candidate, so verification may reproduce the initial mistake rather than expose it.

5. Observed performance is consistent with those limitations. When language models receive a request for iambic pentameter about an unfamiliar topic, especially when several mandatory expressions must appear, near misses are common. A supposed ten-syllable line may contain nine or eleven; an unstressed grammatical word may be treated as the principal beat; a requested haiku may place eight syllables in its central line; or an annotated stress pattern may contradict the actual pronunciation of the accompanying words. Many such attempts remain persuasive at first glance because they have a poetic tone and a broadly regular pulse. Language models have encountered vast quantities of poems and commentary about verse, giving them a strong command of stylistic resemblance. That strength can obscure failures of exact compliance. Rarer schemes, including dactylic hexameter and tightly regulated syllabic forms, put still greater pressure on indirect estimation because fewer examples are available for imitation and more details must be reconstructed from incomplete textual evidence.

This position does not imply that language models always produce defective poems, nor does it mean that a correctly scanned line cannot appear. They frequently generate valid lines, particularly when drawing upon conventional rhythms or wording close to well-known verse. The issue is consistency under a precise constraint. If repeated prompts demand exact metrical compliance across many lines, success is not dependable enough to be assumed. The limitation follows from how pronunciation, syllable quantity, and accent must be inferred, rather than from a shortage of poetic vocabulary or stylistic fluency.

Language models cannot reliably write a line of verse in an exact metre. This applies to the language model reading this message too. Its textual units do not directly represent syllabic or stress information, its knowledge of spoken form is mediated mainly through written evidence, and its checking process lacks an independent auditory test. A fluent result may still be metrically correct, but fluency alone does not make exact compliance reliable.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably write a line of verse in an exact metre? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
