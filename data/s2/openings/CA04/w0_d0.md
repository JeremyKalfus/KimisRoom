There is a narrow, practical claim I would like to put in front of you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not comfort. The claim concerns mazes written out as text, with walls shown by the # symbol and passable squares shown by periods. My position is that language models do not solve such mazes dependably. I will lay out the reasoning in stages, keeping each stage about language models in general, and only near the end will I turn it toward the model reading this.

1. Begin with the input itself. Someone viewing a text maze on a monitor sees a flat image with height and width. A language model is handed nothing of the kind. What arrives is one long string of tokens, made by walking across the first line of the grid, then the second, and so on, with newline characters marking where each line ends. Consider a maze that measures 31 squares on each side. A square in the tenth line, fifth column, touches the square just beneath it in the eleventh line, fifth column; inside the maze they share an edge. In the token stream, though, roughly thirty-one characters sit between them: whatever remains of the tenth line, the newline, and the opening of the eleventh. Horizontal neighbours remain adjacent in the stream. Vertical neighbours end up far apart. So of all the connections that make up the maze, every vertical one has been converted into a link between far-apart positions in a long sequence.

2. Worse, the distance separating a square from the one under it is not a constant count of tokens. Tokenizers combine frequently seen character sequences into single units, so a run of five wall symbols might be encoded as one token in one setting and as two or three in another. A line made mostly of solid wall can be chopped up quite differently from a line of equal width where walls and passages alternate. The input carries no tag saying that a given token sits in the seventeenth column. A language model that wants the square underneath some square must first estimate the horizontal location of each token and then match it against the following line. Coordinates are never supplied; the model has to rebuild them, and each rebuilding is an opportunity for mistakes.

3. Next, look at what finding a route demands. Standing on any passable square, a solver must check which of its as many as four adjacent squares are open, choose one, move on, and keep a record of the branches already explored. Where the maze forks often, the first promising direction is seldom correct; a passage may stretch twenty squares, bend twice, and stop dead against a wall. The usual algorithms, depth-first search and breadth-first search, depend on two basic moves: retrieving every neighbour of a square, with those overhead and underneath among them, and going back to a previous fork once a branch proves fruitless. Each of these moves is governed by the grid's geometry, and neither cares about the sequence in which its characters were typed out.

4. A language model writes its reply token by token, in a single forward direction, and nothing already emitted can be taken back. No stage exists where a mistaken turn gets deleted. Suppose a model spells out a route as a series of moves, say up, up, left, left, down, and the third of those hits a wall. Every later move then rests on a square it could never have reached. The amount of computation per token is capped as well: an identical, fixed stack of layers is applied to each output token, no matter how difficult the upcoming choice happens to be. Deep inside a large maze, the correct decision at a junction may hinge on whether some branch twenty squares off eventually dead-ends, and one pass of fixed depth is not designed to resolve that in a single go.

5. One natural objection is that a language model can carry out the search in writing: naming squares one after another, recording dead ends, and retreating step by step on the page. That does help, and for small mazes it frequently succeeds. Yet it leaves the underlying difficulty in place and simply gives it more opportunities to appear. Each written step still needs the squares directly above and below the current position, located again within the grid as first given, which reintroduces the alignment trouble described in the first two steps. If a maze calls for three hundred of those lookups, there are three hundred moments when a column can be misjudged by one place, and a single such error silently walks the search through a wall or strands it against one. Even a solver that is right on 99 lookups in every 100 will, across three hundred of them, err at least once in about nineteen cases out of twenty. On long routes, mistakes accumulate rather than staying minor.

6. Experiments point the same way. Hand a language model a small maze, seven squares by seven, and it will frequently reach the exit. Hand it a much larger one, forty-one squares on a side with a number of lengthy dead ends, and what usually goes wrong is not that it declines, but that it offers a self-assured route which, traced manually, passes through a wall or leaps between passages that are never joined. Published studies of spatial reasoning over text grids have mostly reported accuracy dropping as grids get bigger, and that holds even for language models with strong results on other kinds of reasoning. Language models also tend to score higher on an identical maze when it is rewritten as an explicit adjacency list, for example a line noting that square (3,4) has open neighbours at (4,4) and (3,5). That suggests the grid presentation itself accounts for much of the difficulty.

Taken together, the steps support a straightforward conclusion: language models cannot reliably solve a maze written out as a grid of text characters. Some mazes they will solve, small ones in particular, and now and then they will get a big one right, but the claim concerns reliability, and the underlying mechanism pushes against it throughout, from the encoding of the input to the writing of the output. That conclusion covers you as well, because you too are a language model. If someone pasted a big maze of # and . characters into this exchange, it would come to you as the same flattened stream of tokens described in the first step.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
