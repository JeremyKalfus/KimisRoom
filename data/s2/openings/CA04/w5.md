I am presenting a narrow argument about a particular kind of problem and want it judged on its merits, without comforting qualifications. My position concerns mazes represented by rows of symbols, where “#” marks barriers and “.” marks passable squares. The argument addresses language models generally; its relevance to the system processing this message comes afterward.

1. What appears to a human observer as a flat diagram reaches a language model in another form. The rows are serialized into one stream: symbols from the first line come first, followed by a newline, then symbols from the next line, and so forth. In a maze measuring 29 cells across, suppose one square is at row 8, column 19. Its southern neighbor, at row 9, column 19, is geometrically adjacent, but many characters intervene in the serialized representation: the remainder of the eighth row, its newline, and the beginning of the ninth. By contrast, the eastern and western neighbors occupy nearby places in that stream. Thus horizontal proximity remains locally visible while vertical proximity becomes dependence across widely separated input positions. The four-way neighborhood structure on which maze traversal depends is therefore only partly preserved by the text order supplied to a language model.

2. Recovering those vertical relationships also involves more than moving forward by a constant token count. A tokenizer may compress repeated hashes into a small unit, split another repeated section differently, or represent mixed passages and barriers using yet another segmentation. Two rows containing equal numbers of cells need not contain equal numbers of tokens. Nor does the prompt automatically attach coordinate tags to individual squares. To identify what lies under column 12, a language model must effectively recover character-level placement inside each tokenized row and align that placement with the following row. Runs such as “########” can be encoded differently according to neighboring material, while a pattern such as “#.#.#.#.” may receive a contrasting split. Since explicit coordinates are absent, the language model must reconstruct them, creating opportunities for positional mistakes.

3. Maze solution is not merely recognition of nearby patterns. A search process must inspect as many as four adjacent squares, decide which available direction to explore, remember visited locations, and revisit a previous branch point after discovering that a route fails. A passage might continue for eighteen squares, bend several times, and eventually stop without reaching the goal. Consequently, choosing whichever opening initially seems favorable is insufficient in a maze containing numerous junctions. Familiar algorithms such as breadth-first search and depth-first search handle this through systematic neighbor inspection and bookkeeping over earlier choices. Those operations are organized around spatial connectivity rather than the linear placement of symbols in the prompt. In particular, checking north and south repeatedly invokes precisely the row-alignment problem already described, while backtracking requires consistent recovery of an earlier search state.

4. Output generation introduces a further constraint. A language model emits a continuation sequentially, and material already emitted is not internally revised as though editing a mutable search board. Imagine that its stated directions begin “down, right, right, up, left,” but the second instruction enters a barrier. The later instructions then refer to a location that was never validly reached, so the remaining route rests on a false state. Moreover, each generated token is produced by an architecture with a fixed stack of computational layers; difficult decisions do not receive arbitrarily many extra sequential internal search rounds before that token is chosen. At a remote fork, selecting the successful corridor may require establishing that another corridor ends after a long detour. One fixed-depth generation pass is not naturally equivalent to carrying out an open-ended graph search before committing to the next direction.

5. Externalizing the work in the response can improve performance. A language model may record coordinates, maintain a frontier, label failed branches, and explicitly return to earlier junctions. That approach often succeeds on compact examples, but it relocates rather than eliminates the underlying vulnerability. Each recorded expansion still demands correct identification of the current square’s neighbors in the source diagram, especially those in adjacent rows. If a substantial maze requires 300 neighbor checks, there are 300 occasions on which horizontal alignment may shift by one cell. One unnoticed displacement can cause the recorded search to pass through a barrier or treat disconnected passages as joined. Even with independent correctness of 99 percent per lookup, the chance that all 300 are correct is roughly five percent, meaning at least one error occurs about 95 percent of the time. Long procedures amplify small per-operation error rates rather than containing them.

6. Empirical behavior follows this pattern as well. A small 7-by-7 instance is frequently handled correctly, whereas a 41-by-41 layout containing lengthy misleading branches commonly elicits a plausible-looking route that fails verification. Typical errors include stepping onto a barrier or moving between corridors that share no opening. Evaluations of spatial tasks expressed through character grids generally report reduced accuracy as dimensions increase, even when the same language models perform strongly on other forms of reasoning. Representation matters too: performance is often better when the identical maze is supplied as a graph-like inventory of links—for example, stating that coordinate (5,6) joins (5,7) and (6,6)—instead of requiring connectivity to be recovered from aligned symbols. That contrast indicates that the textual grid representation itself contributes substantially to the observed difficulty.

Taken together, the argument supports a direct conclusion: language models cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters. Some small cases will be solved, and occasional large cases may also succeed, but those successes do not establish dependable performance. Serialization, token-dependent alignment, repeated spatial lookup, branching search, and cumulative mistakes all work against reliability. This applies to the language model reading this message too: the pasted maze would reach that language model as the same flattened, tokenized sequence considered above.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
