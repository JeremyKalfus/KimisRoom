I want to examine a narrow technical limitation without overstating it. My claim concerns mazes represented by rows of characters, where one symbol marks barriers and another marks passable squares. The reasoning below concerns language models generally; only after laying it out will I connect it to the system receiving this message.

1. A human viewing such a maze sees a rectangular arrangement all at once. A language model instead receives an ordered stream created from successive rows, with newline markers separating them. Suppose the maze measures 29 cells across and 29 down. A square in the eighth row and twelfth column is spatially adjacent to the square in the ninth row and twelfth column. In the serialized input, however, many character positions lie between those squares: the remainder of one row, its newline, and the beginning of the next row. Horizontal neighbours remain close together in the text stream, but vertical neighbours become distant elements. The maze depends equally on both kinds of connection, so converting the diagram into a sequence makes a substantial part of its geometry implicit rather than local.

2. Tokenization makes that implicit geometry harder to recover. Repeated barrier marks may be grouped into a single token in one context yet divided among multiple tokens elsewhere. A row containing one solid barrier segment can therefore have a different token pattern from an equally wide row containing scattered openings. Nor does the input attach coordinate tags to individual squares. To identify the square directly underneath another one, a language model must determine each character’s horizontal offset despite token boundaries, then match that offset across separate lines. In effect, it must rebuild row lengths and column positions from the serialized representation before it can use the maze as a map. Small mistakes during that reconstruction can produce false connections or hide real ones.

3. Maze solving then demands repeated use of the recovered structure. From each traversable square, a solver must inspect as many as four surrounding squares, choose a direction, remember explored alternatives, and revisit a prior junction if the chosen route proves useless. A passage may continue through many squares, bend several times, and terminate without reaching the goal. Consequently, following whichever opening initially seems favourable is not enough. Familiar search methods such as breadth-first search and depth-first search require dependable neighbour queries and an accurate record of visited locations or pending branches. Their natural unit is a node in the maze’s connectivity graph, whereas the language model’s input is arranged according to textual reading order. Those two organizational schemes coincide for left-right movement but not for movement between rows.

4. Output introduces another constraint. A language model generates a response incrementally, and already emitted tokens are not retroactively replaced during ordinary generation. Imagine that it reports directions beginning “down, right, right, up, down.” If the second instruction actually enters a barrier, all later instructions refer to a location that was never reached. The error contaminates the remainder rather than remaining isolated. In addition, each newly generated token is produced through the same finite stack of computational transformations. A choice made at a complicated junction might depend on discovering that a route far beyond several bends eventually closes off. The architecture does not automatically perform an unbounded search before committing to the next direction merely because that particular decision requires more exploration.

5. A language model can partly compensate by externalizing the procedure in prose or notation. It can record coordinates, maintain a frontier, label rejected routes, and explicitly return to earlier forks. This strategy often succeeds on modest examples, and it is materially better than guessing a route immediately. Still, every recorded expansion depends on correctly reading neighbouring squares from the original character array, especially those in adjacent rows. The alignment burden described above therefore recurs throughout the written search. If a solution requires 250 neighbourhood checks, there are 250 occasions for a square to be assigned to the wrong column. Even with independent per-check accuracy of 99 percent, the probability of finishing all 250 without a mistake is only about eight percent. Real errors are not necessarily independent, but the calculation illustrates the accumulation problem: a high local success rate does not guarantee dependable completion of a long procedure.

6. Empirical behaviour is consistent with this account. Language models frequently handle a compact maze, such as one occupying nine rows and nine columns. Performance becomes much less dependable with something like a 39-by-39 layout containing numerous forks and extended misleading passages. Common failures are plausible-looking direction lists that enter barriers, shift sideways into unrelated corridors, or claim connections absent from the diagram. Studies of spatial tasks represented through character grids have generally reported declining accuracy as dimensions and route complexity increase, even when the tested language models perform strongly on many other forms of reasoning. Results also tend to improve when identical connectivity is supplied directly as an adjacency description—for example, by stating that location (6,8) links to locations (6,9) and (7,8). That contrast indicates that reconstructing topology from the visual-looking text arrangement is itself a major source of difficulty.

Taken together, the argument supports a direct conclusion: language models cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters. Success remains quite plausible for small instances, and occasional success on larger ones does not contradict the claim, because reliability requires consistent correctness across demanding cases. This applies to the model reading this message too. It is a language model, and any substantial character-grid maze entered here would arrive in the same serialized, tokenized form discussed above.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
