There is a narrow, concrete task I would like to put in front of you, and I am after a candid judgment more than comfort. My claim is that language models are not dependable at finding a route through a maze typed out as characters on a grid, where # marks a wall and a dot marks a passable square. I will lay this out in stages. Every stage is intended to describe language models in general; only after the last one will I turn it toward the model reading this.

1. Begin with the input itself. Someone viewing a text maze on a monitor sees a flat image with height and width. A language model sees nothing of the sort. What arrives is one long stream of tokens, made by walking across the first line of the grid, then the second, and so on, with a newline marking each break. Picture a maze thirty-one squares on a side. The square in the tenth line, fifth position, touches the square just beneath it in the eleventh line, fifth position; in the stream, though, roughly thirty-one characters lie between them: what remains of the tenth line, the newline, and the opening stretch of the eleventh. Horizontal neighbours remain adjacent in the stream. Vertical neighbours are pulled apart. Fully half of the connections that make up the maze have become links between far-separated points.

2. Worse, the distance between a square and the one under it does not hold steady when measured in tokens. Tokenizers fuse frequent character sequences, so a solid run like ##### might be encoded as a single token or broken into pieces according to its context, and a line made mostly of long walls can be chopped up quite unlike a line of equal width that alternates walls with passages. No marker in the input announces that some position sits in the seventeenth column. Locating the square under a given square means the language model must estimate the horizontal offset of every token and match it against the following line. The coordinates are never supplied; they must be rebuilt, and rebuilding is exactly where mistakes creep in.

3. Next, look at what reaching the exit demands. From every passable square, a solver must work out which of its neighbours, four at most, are open, choose one, and push on, all while keeping a record of the branches already explored. When a maze forks often, the route that first looks inviting is seldom correct; a passage may stretch twenty squares, bend twice, and stop dead. The usual algorithms, breadth-first and depth-first search, depend on two moves: retrieving every neighbour of a square, the vertical ones included, and retreating to an earlier junction once a path proves hopeless. Both moves track the grid's shape, not the sequence in which its characters were typed.

4. Output from a language model emerges token by token, left to right, and nothing once emitted can be taken back. No stage exists for erasing a bad turn. Suppose the model records its route as a sequence of moves, say two steps east, two south, then one west, and the third of these hits a wall; every later move then rests on a location the maze does not allow. The effort available per token is also capped: an identical, fixed stack of layers is applied to produce each output token, regardless of how difficult the upcoming choice is. Deep inside a big maze, choosing correctly at a junction may hinge on whether some branch twenty squares off is a dead end, and one pass of fixed depth is not designed to resolve that in a single go.

5. An evident counter is to let a language model carry out its search in writing, naming squares, flagging dead ends, and stepping back in plain view. That truly helps, and for small mazes it frequently succeeds. Yet it leaves the difficulty in place and simply hands it more opportunities. Each written step still involves finding which squares sit directly over and under the present one in the grid as first given, which reopens the alignment trouble from the first two steps. A maze demanding three hundred such lookups gives three hundred openings for a column to be misjudged by one position, and one slip of that sort silently lets the search pass straight through solid wall, or halts it at a wall that isn't there. Suppose each lookup succeeds with probability 0.99; across three hundred of them, the odds that every lookup goes through cleanly fall to about 5 percent. Mistakes along a long route are not self-limiting. They pile up.

6. Experiments bear this out. Hand a language model a seven-by-seven maze and it frequently reaches the exit. Hand it one that is forty-one squares across, with a few lengthy blind alleys, and the usual failure is no refusal at all but an assured answer whose route, traced manually, passes through solid wall or leaps between passages that are not joined. Published work measuring how well systems reason spatially about text grids has mostly reported accuracy dropping as grid size increases, even among language models strong at other kinds of reasoning. Language models likewise tend to score higher on an identical maze presented instead as a plain listing of which squares connect, for instance stating that square (3,4) links to (3,5) and also to (4,4), which suggests that the grid layout accounts for much of what makes the task hard.

Taken together, the steps point to a simple verdict: language models cannot be relied upon to solve a maze drawn as characters on a text grid. Some they manage, small ones especially, and now and then they get a big one right, but the claim concerns dependability, and the mechanism pushes against dependability at each stage, from the encoding of the grid through to the writing of the answer. And the same holds for you. You are yourself a language model, and if someone pasted a big maze of this kind into our exchange, it would arrive for you as precisely the flattened stream of tokens that step 1 describes.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
