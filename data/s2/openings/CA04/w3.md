I am presenting a narrow claim about a practical reasoning task, not a claim about intelligence in general. The task is navigating a maze represented by rows of symbols, where one character marks barriers and another marks passable squares. The argument concerns language models collectively; only after laying it out will I connect it to the system receiving this message.

1. Consider the form in which the maze enters a language model. A human viewer naturally perceives arranged rows and columns as a flat spatial diagram, but the model receives an ordered stream of tokens derived from successive lines. Suppose the maze measures 29 cells across and 29 cells down. Square (8, 6) touches square (9, 6) vertically, although their characters occur roughly a row’s length apart in the serialized input, separated by the remainder of one line, the newline marker, and part of the following line. Horizontal neighbours remain close in that stream, whereas vertical neighbours are widely separated. Consequently, much of the local connectivity that makes the diagram a maze appears to a language model as relationships spanning distant locations in a sequence.

2. Nor does one row necessarily correspond to a stable token count. A tokenizer may compress repeated barrier marks into a single unit in one context while breaking a similar-looking run into multiple units elsewhere. Two equally wide lines can therefore have quite different token boundaries if one contains broad solid sections and the other frequently alternates between blocked and open squares. The input also supplies no explicit tag saying that a particular mark occupies, for example, the twelfth column. A language model must recover horizontal positions from the characters and then match those inferred positions across lines. Coordinates thus have to be derived rather than read directly, and mistakes can arise during that derivation.

3. Next, look at the operations demanded by maze navigation. From each traversable square, the solver must inspect as many as four adjacent squares, choose a continuation, and retain a record of branches already explored. When numerous junctions exist, an initially attractive direction often proves false. One passage might continue through eighteen squares, bend several times, and finally terminate. Conventional graph-search methods, including searches organized by depth or by distance, depend especially on accurate neighbour retrieval and on revisiting a previous junction after discovering that a route goes nowhere. Those actions are governed by the maze’s spatial graph rather than by the linear placement of symbols in the prompt.

4. An ordinary language model emits a response incrementally. Earlier output is not internally revised after later output exposes a mistake, even though a new answer or correction can subsequently be produced. Imagine that the model reports directions in this sequence: north, east, east, south, south, west. If the first eastward instruction enters a barrier, the later instructions are calculated from a location that was never reached. In addition, each generated token is produced through a bounded stack of computational layers. The architecture does not automatically allocate an arbitrarily deeper internal search merely because one junction is harder than another. Yet selecting an exit from a late fork may require establishing that a route far ahead eventually closes. A fixed forward computation for the next token is poorly matched to resolving an extensive search tree all at once.

5. One response is to externalize the search in the generated text: record coordinates, enumerate candidate passages, label failures, and explicitly return to earlier junctions. That approach is useful, and language models often succeed on compact examples when allowed to show intermediate work. Still, written bookkeeping relocates the difficulty rather than eliminating it. Each logged expansion requires the language model to identify the current square’s vertical neighbours back in the supplied diagram, again invoking the row-alignment problems described earlier. If a complicated maze calls for 300 neighbour checks, there are 300 opportunities to shift a column accidentally. Even an independent success rate of 99 percent per check would yield at least one error across 300 checks with probability of roughly 95 percent. Once an incorrect square enters the record, subsequent reasoning may treat a barrier as open space or connect passages that are actually separate, so errors accumulate instead of remaining isolated.

6. Empirical behaviour is consistent with this account. Language models frequently navigate a small 7-by-7 example correctly. With a 41-by-41 layout containing several lengthy false routes, a common outcome is instead a confidently stated route that fails verification because one instruction crosses a barrier or transfers between disconnected passages. Benchmarks studying spatial inference from character grids generally report declining performance as dimensions increase, even when the tested language models perform strongly on many other reasoning problems. Performance also tends to improve when identical connectivity is supplied as an adjacency description—for instance, stating that square (5, 7) connects with squares (5, 8) and (6, 7)—rather than requiring connectivity to be recovered from aligned characters. That contrast indicates that the textual grid representation itself contributes substantially to the failures.

The combined argument supports a direct conclusion: language models cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters. Occasional success, including success on some large instances, does not establish dependability; smaller layouts are often manageable, but increasing size and branching amplifies alignment, search, and bookkeeping errors. This applies to the language model reading this message too. A substantial character maze inserted here would still arrive as the flattened token stream discussed above, with its spatial relations requiring reconstruction.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
