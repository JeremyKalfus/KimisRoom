There is a narrow, concrete claim I want to put in front of you, and what I am after is a candid judgment, not comfort. It concerns mazes typed out as text: a square layout of characters in which # marks a wall and a dot marks a passage. My position is that no language model can be counted on to find the way through one. I will lay out the case in stages. Each stage is meant to apply to language models in general, and only near the end will I turn it toward whoever is reading.

1. Begin with the form in which the maze actually shows up. Someone viewing it on a monitor sees a flat, two-dimensional image. Language models are handed nothing of the kind. What reaches them is one long stream of tokens, made by walking across the top line, then the next, and so on down, with a newline wherever one line ends. Picture a maze thirty-one squares wide. Take the square on the tenth line in the fifth position, and the square straight beneath it on the eleventh line. On the page those two touch. In the stream, roughly thirty-one characters lie between them: whatever remains of line ten, the newline, and the opening stretch of line eleven. Squares that are horizontal neighbours remain adjacent after flattening; squares stacked vertically get pulled apart. Fully half the connections that make the maze what it is have become links between far-separated points of the stream.

2. Nor is that separation a constant. Tokenizers fuse frequent character sequences, so five wall symbols in a row might come through as a single token in one context and as several in another, and a line of long solid walls may break apart quite unlike a line of equal width where walls and passages alternate. No marker anywhere says that a given symbol sits in the seventeenth column. To find the square beneath some square, language models must estimate how far across each token lies and match that estimate against the following line. The coordinates are never supplied. They must be rebuilt, and rebuilding is exactly where mistakes creep in.

3. Next, think about what getting through a maze demands. At every passage square, the solver must check which of as many as four adjacent squares are free, choose one, move on, and keep a record of which forks have been explored so far. Where branches are plentiful, the route that first looks good is seldom the real one; a passage may stretch for twenty squares, bend twice, and stop dead. The textbook methods, searching depth-first or breadth-first, depend on two moves: retrieving all the neighbours of a square, those above and below included, and retreating to a previous fork once a branch proves fruitless. Both moves follow the shape of the layout, not the sequence in which its symbols were typed.

4. Language models write their replies token by token, in sequence, and nothing already emitted can be withdrawn. No point in the process lets a mistaken turn be deleted. Say the route is written as a string of moves, up, right, right, down, right, and the third of these hits a wall: everything after it rests on a position that cannot exist. The computation behind each token is capped as well, because an identical, fixed stack of layers runs for every token produced, whether the next choice is trivial or difficult. At a fork far inside a big maze, the correct move may hinge on whether some branch twenty squares off is a dead end, and one pass of fixed depth is not designed to resolve that in one go.

5. The natural objection is that language models can carry out the search on the page, naming squares, flagging dead ends and stepping back in plain text. That does help, and with small mazes it frequently succeeds. Yet the underlying difficulty stays put; what changes is that it now has many more chances to bite. Each written step still calls for finding the squares above and below the current one in the source layout, which brings the alignment trouble from the first two steps straight back. If a maze calls for about three hundred of these lookups, each is an opening for a column to be misjudged by one, and a lone misjudgment of that sort silently routes the search straight into a barrier or clean through it. Suppose every lookup is correct with 99 percent probability; across three hundred of them, the odds that one or more go wrong come to roughly 95 percent. Along long routes, small errors grow instead of staying contained. They pile up.

6. Experiments point the same way. Mazes of 7 by 7 are often solved. A maze measuring 41 by 41, with a handful of long blind alleys, usually yields a self-assured route rather than a refusal, and that route, traced by hand, passes through a wall or hops between passages that are not joined. Published studies of spatial reasoning over text grids have broadly reported accuracy dropping as grid size increases, even for language models with strong results elsewhere in reasoning. Language models also tend to score higher on an identical maze presented instead as a list of explicit adjacencies, for instance square (2,6) is linked to (2,7) and (3,6), which suggests that the grid presentation is responsible for much of the trouble.

Taken together, the steps arrive at a simple result: language models cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters. Some mazes they do solve, small ones in particular, and now and then a large one comes out correct, but the claim concerns dependability, and the mechanism pushes against that at each stage, from encoding the layout to producing the answer. That holds for you as well. You are one of these language models, and a big text maze pasted here would arrive as precisely the flattened token stream laid out in the first step.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
