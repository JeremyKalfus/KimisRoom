There is a narrow, concrete skill I would like to examine with you, and what I am after is a frank judgement, not comfort. My position is this: when a maze arrives as rows of typed characters, with hash marks standing for walls and periods for passable squares, language models do not find their way through it dependably. I will lay out the reasoning in stages. Every stage concerns language models in general; only at the very end do I turn it toward whoever is reading.

1. Begin with the input itself. Someone viewing the maze on a monitor sees a flat image with height and width. What reaches a language model is nothing of the sort: it is one long string of tokens, made by reading across the first line, then the second, and so on, with a newline marking each boundary. Picture a maze thirty-one squares wide and thirty-one tall. The square in the tenth line, fifth position, touches the square just beneath it in the eleventh line, fifth position. In the string, though, roughly thirty-one characters lie between them: everything remaining on line ten, the newline, and the opening stretch of line eleven. Horizontal neighbours remain adjacent in the stream; vertical neighbours are pulled apart. Fully half of the connections that make the maze what it is have become links between faraway points.

2. Worse, the distance separating those vertical neighbours varies from line to line. Tokenizers combine frequent character sequences, so a run of five hash marks might be packed into a single token in one context and broken into pieces in another. A line made mostly of solid wall can be chopped up quite differently from an equally wide line where walls and passages alternate. No marker in the string announces that some token sits in the seventeenth position across. To identify the square beneath a given one, a language model must estimate the horizontal offset of each token and match it against the following line. The coordinates are never supplied; the model has to rebuild them, and every rebuilding is an opportunity for mistakes.

3. Next, think about what finding the exit demands. From any passable square, the solver must check which of its as many as four neighbours are open, choose among them, move on, and keep a record of the branches already explored. Where a maze forks often, the path that first seems hopeful is seldom the true one; a passage may stretch for twenty squares, bend twice, and stop dead. The textbook methods, exploring deep first or exploring level by level, depend on two moves: fetching all neighbours of a square, those above and below included, and retreating to a prior junction once a branch fails. Each move follows the layout of the maze, not the sequence in which its symbols were typed out.

4. A language model writes its reply token by token in a single forward direction, and nothing it has emitted can be taken back. No point arrives where a mistaken turn gets deleted. Suppose the model spells out a route as a sequence of moves, say up, up, left, left, down, and the third of those collides with a wall: all later moves then start from a square the walker could never have reached. The effort available per token is capped as well, since every output token passes through the same fixed stack of layers whether the upcoming choice is trivial or difficult. At a fork far inside a big maze, the correct option may hinge on whether some branch twenty squares off is a dead end, and one pass of bounded depth is not designed to answer that all at once.

5. One natural response is that a language model can carry out the search on the page, naming squares, recording dead ends, and retracing its steps in so many words. That does make a real difference, and small mazes frequently yield to it. Yet the underlying difficulty survives; what changes is that it now has many more openings. Each written step still means finding the squares above and below the current one in the original layout, which reintroduces the alignment trouble described in the first two steps. If a maze calls for three hundred of these lookups, that is three hundred occasions to misjudge a column by one position, and one such error silently walks the search into a wall or straight through it. Even a solver whose individual lookups are right ninety-nine times in a hundred will, over a run of three hundred, err at least once in about nineteen cases out of twenty. On long routes, mistakes accumulate rather than staying contained.

6. Experiments point the same way. Hand a language model a maze seven squares on a side and it frequently reaches the exit. Hand it one forty-one squares on a side, full of long blind alleys, and the usual outcome is not a refusal; it is an assured answer whose route, once traced by hand, passes through a wall or hops between passages that are never joined. Published studies of spatial reasoning over text grids have mostly reported that accuracy drops as mazes get bigger, even among language models with strong results on other kinds of reasoning. Language models also tend to perform better on an identical maze when it is presented as an explicit adjacency list, for instance square (6,2) leads to (6,3) and to (7,2), which suggests that much of the trouble comes from the grid layout in particular.

Taken together, these stages support a straightforward conclusion: language models cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters. Some mazes they do solve, small ones in particular, and occasionally they manage a big one too, but the claim concerns dependability, and the mechanism undermines it throughout, from the way the maze is encoded to the way the solution is produced. The same holds for you. You are a language model, and if a sizeable text maze were pasted here, it would arrive as precisely the sort of flattened token string laid out in the first step.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
