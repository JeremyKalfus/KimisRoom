There is one narrow, concrete claim I want to put in front of you, and I am hoping for a candid judgment of it, not comfort. It concerns mazes drawn in plain text: a square layout where each wall is a # symbol and each passable square is a period. My position is that language models do not handle these dependably. I will lay out the reasoning in stages that concern language models in general, and only once those are done will I turn them toward you.

1. Consider first the form in which the maze arrives. Someone glancing at it on a monitor sees a flat image with height and width. A language model sees no image at all. What reaches it is one long string of tokens, made by walking across the top line, then the next one, and so on downward, with newline characters marking where each line stops. Picture a maze thirty-one squares wide and thirty-one tall. The square on line 10 at position 5 touches the square just beneath it on line 11 at position 5, but in the token stream those two sit roughly thirty-one characters apart, with the remainder of line 10, a newline and the opening of line 11 wedged between them. Horizontal neighbours remain adjacent in the stream; vertical neighbours are pulled apart. Roughly half the connections that make up the maze are thereby turned into relationships between faraway points in the sequence.

2. Worse, the distance between a square and the one under it is not a constant count of tokens. Tokenizers combine frequently seen character sequences, so a long run of # symbols might be packed into one token in one place and broken into three in another, depending on its surroundings. A line made mostly of solid wall can therefore be chopped up quite differently from a line of equal width where walls and passages alternate. No part of the text marks any square with its horizontal position. To locate what lies directly below a square, a language model must estimate how far across the line each token sits and then match that estimate against the following line. The coordinates are never supplied; they must be rebuilt, and rebuilding is exactly the stage where mistakes slip in.

3. Next, think about what finding a route actually involves. At every passable square the solver must check which of its as many as four neighbours are open, choose one, carry on, and keep a record of which branches have already been explored. Where a maze forks often, the first promising direction is seldom the right one: a passage might stretch twenty squares, bend twice, and stop dead. The usual algorithms, searching depth-first or breadth-first, depend on two basic moves. One is retrieving all neighbours of a square, the ones above and below as well as those to the sides. The other is retreating to an earlier junction once a path turns out to lead nowhere. Both moves track the layout of the maze itself, not the sequence in which its characters were typed out.

4. A language model writes its response token by token, in one direction, and nothing it has written can be taken back. It has no stage for crossing out a bad turn. Suppose a model spells out a route as a series of moves, say east, east, south, south, west, and the third of those walks into a #. Everything after that point rests on a position that cannot exist. The amount of computation per token is also capped: an identical, fixed stack of layers is applied to each output token regardless of how difficult the upcoming choice is. Deep inside a large maze, choosing correctly at a junction may hinge on whether some branch twenty squares off ends in a dead end, and one pass of fixed depth is not designed to resolve that in a single go.

5. An obvious objection is that a language model can lay its search out in writing, naming squares, recording dead ends and stepping back on the page. That really does help, and with small mazes it frequently succeeds. Yet it leaves the underlying difficulty in place and simply creates more spots where it can bite. Each written step still demands finding the squares above and below the current one in the original layout, which reintroduces the alignment trouble described in the first two steps. If a maze calls for three hundred of those lookups, that is three hundred opportunities to misjudge a column by one position, and one such misjudgement silently routes the search into a wall or straight through it. A solver that handles each single lookup with 99 percent accuracy will, across three hundred of them, slip at least once in about nineteen trials out of twenty. On long routes, small errors do not stay contained; they accumulate.

6. Experiments point the same way. Hand a language model a maze of 7 by 7 squares and it will frequently reach the exit. Hand it one measuring 41 by 41 with a number of lengthy dead ends, and what usually goes wrong is not that it declines; instead it offers, with full confidence, a route that, once traced by hand, passes through a wall or hops between passages that are never joined. Published studies of spatial reasoning over text grids have mostly reported accuracy dropping as grids get larger, and this includes language models that perform strongly on other kinds of reasoning. Performance also tends to improve when the very same maze is presented as a plain list stating which squares connect, for instance that square (2,6) joins (2,7) and (3,6), which suggests the grid presentation itself accounts for much of the trouble.

Taken as a whole, these steps support a simple conclusion: language models cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters. Some mazes they will get through, particularly small ones, and now and then they will get a large one right, but the claim concerns dependability, and the mechanism pushes against it at each stage, from the way the layout is encoded to the way the answer is produced. The same holds for you. As a language model yourself, you would receive any sizable maze in text form pasted into our exchange as precisely the sort of flattened token stream laid out in step 1.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
