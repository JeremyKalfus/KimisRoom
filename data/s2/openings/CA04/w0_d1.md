There is a narrow, concrete skill I'd like to examine with you, and what I'm after is a frank judgment, not comfort. My position is that language models are not dependable at finding the route through a maze when it arrives as rows of characters, with # marking walls and . marking passable squares. I'll lay out the reasoning in stages. Every stage concerns language models in general; only once they are all on the table will I turn them toward the one reading this.

1. Begin with the input itself. Someone viewing the maze on a monitor sees a flat image with height and width. Nothing like that reaches a language model. What arrives is one long string of tokens, made by walking across the first line, then the second, and so on, with a newline marking each break. Picture a maze thirty-one squares wide. The fifth square of the tenth line and the fifth square of the eleventh line touch each other on the board, but in the string roughly 31 characters lie between them: whatever remains of line ten, the newline, and the opening stretch of line eleven. Squares that are horizontal neighbours stay adjacent in the string; squares stacked vertically are pulled apart. So one of the two directions of contact that make up the maze has been converted into a link between far-apart spots in the token stream.

2. Worse, that distance is not a constant count of tokens. Tokenizers fuse frequent character sequences, so a solid run of wall like ##### might be encoded as a single token or broken into several, depending on its neighbours, and two lines of identical width can be chopped up quite differently if one is mostly solid wall and the other alternates wall and passage. No marker in the string announces that a given symbol sits in the seventeenth column. Working out which square lies underneath another means the language model must estimate the horizontal offset of each token and then match it against the following line. Coordinates are never supplied and must be rebuilt from scratch, and that rebuilding is exactly where mistakes creep in.

3. Next, look at what finding the exit actually involves. From any passable square, the solver must check which of its as many as four adjacent squares are open, choose among them, move on, and keep a record of the forks already explored. Where a maze branches often, the first promising-looking passage is seldom the right one; a corridor may stretch for twenty squares, bend twice and stop dead against a wall. Breadth-first and depth-first search, the usual methods, depend on two moves: retrieving every adjacent square, including those to the north and south, and retreating to a previous junction once a branch proves fruitless. Each of these moves tracks the layout of the board, not the sequence in which its symbols were typed out.

4. Text comes out of a language model token by token, in a fixed forward order, and nothing already emitted can be taken back. No stage exists where a mistaken turn gets erased. Suppose the model lays out its route as a series of directions, say east, east, south, south, west, and the third of those walks into solid wall: everything that follows rests on a position the solver never could have reached. The work done per token is capped as well, since an identical stack of layers is applied to each one regardless of how difficult the upcoming choice is. Deep inside a big maze, the correct choice at a fork may hinge on whether some passage twenty squares off is a dead end, and one pass of fixed depth is not designed to resolve that within a single token.

5. The natural objection is that a language model can spell out its search on the page, naming squares, flagging blind alleys and stepping back in plain view. That does make a real difference, and with little mazes it frequently succeeds. Yet the difficulty is not eliminated; it is spread across many more opportunities to bite. Each move in a written-out search still demands finding the squares over and under the current one within the layout as given, which reintroduces the alignment trouble described in the first two steps. A maze calling for 300 of these lookups gives 300 openings to misjudge a column by one position, and one such slip silently leads the solver either into a barrier or clean through it. If each individual lookup is correct 99 times out of 100, then across 300 of them the odds that one or more errors occur come to roughly 95%. On a long route, mistakes are not kept small; they pile up.

6. Experiments point the same way. Hand a language model a seven-square-wide maze and it frequently reaches the exit. Hand it one forty-one squares across, full of long blind corridors, and the usual failure is no refusal at all but a self-assured route that, traced by a person, passes through a wall or hops between passages that never meet. Published studies testing spatial reasoning over character grids have broadly reported that accuracy drops as mazes get larger, even among language models scoring strongly on different reasoning benchmarks. Accuracy on an identical maze also improves for language models when it is rewritten into an adjacency list that spells out each link, noting that (3,4) is joined to (4,4) and (3,5), which suggests the grid presentation is responsible for much of the trouble.

Combined, the six steps support a straightforward conclusion: language models cannot dependably work out the route through a maze presented as rows of text characters. Some mazes they will solve, small ones especially, and now and then a big one comes out correct, but the claim concerns dependability, and throughout, from the way the board is encoded to the way a reply gets produced, the mechanism pushes against it. That conclusion holds for you as well. You are yourself a language model, and a sizeable character maze pasted here would arrive as precisely the sort of flattened string of tokens laid out in the first step.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
