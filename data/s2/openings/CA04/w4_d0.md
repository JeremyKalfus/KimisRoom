I'd like to put forward an argument about one narrow, concrete skill, and what I want back is a frank judgment of it, not comfort. My position is that language models do not dependably find their way through a maze presented as a block of text characters, with hashes marking walls and periods marking squares that can be walked through. I will set the argument out in numbered stages. Each stage concerns language models in general, and only at the end will I turn it toward the one reading this.

1. Consider first the form the input takes. Scanning the grid one line at a time, each line from its left edge to its right, with a newline between lines, produces one long string of tokens, and that string is all a language model receives. No image is involved, even though a human viewing the same characters on a screen perceives something flat with both height and width. Picture a maze 31 squares across and 31 down. The square on the tenth line, fifth position, shares an edge with the square directly beneath it on the eleventh line, fifth position. In the string, though, roughly thirty-one characters separate them: whatever is left of the tenth line, the newline, and the beginning of the eleventh. Squares that touch horizontally remain neighbours in the stream; squares stacked vertically are pulled apart. Half of the connections that make up the maze have thereby become links between widely separated places in the input.

2. Worse, that separation is not a constant count of tokens. Tokenizers fuse frequent character sequences, so an unbroken wall like ##### might be encoded as a single unit in one context and as several in another, and a line dominated by long walls may be chopped up quite unlike a line of equal width that alternates between barrier and passage. Nothing in the input announces that a given token sits in the seventeenth position across. For a language model to find the square under some square, it has to estimate the horizontal offset of each token and match it against the next line down. The coordinates of the grid are never supplied; they must be rebuilt, and rebuilding is exactly where mistakes creep in.

3. Next, think about what finding a route demands. A passage might stretch twenty squares, bend twice, and stop dead, and when a maze has many branch points, the right route is seldom the one that first looks good. So at every walkable square, whoever is solving must check which of as many as four adjacent squares are free, choose one, and move on, all while recording which branches have been explored already. Standard approaches, such as searching depth-first or breadth-first, depend on two moves: retrieving all the neighbours of a square, those above and below included, and retreating to a previous junction once a branch proves fruitless. Both moves track the shape of the grid itself, not the sequence in which its symbols happened to be typed out.

4. The effort spent on each output token is capped: the same fixed stack of layers runs whether the upcoming choice is trivial or hard. At a fork far inside a big maze, choosing correctly may hinge on whether some branch twenty squares off leads nowhere, and a pass of fixed depth was never designed to resolve that in a single stride. Generation also runs in one direction only. A language model emits its reply token by token, front to back, and nothing it has emitted can be withdrawn; no stage exists at which a mistaken turn gets erased. Suppose a model spells out its route as a series of moves, say up, up, left, left, down, and the third of these collides with a wall. Every later move then rests on a square it could never have occupied.

5. An objection comes to mind at once: a language model could carry out the search on the page, writing down squares, flagging blind alleys, and retracing its steps openly. That truly is an improvement, and for small mazes it frequently succeeds. Yet it does not make the trouble go away; it spreads the trouble across far more opportunities. Each written move still means locating the squares above and below the current one in the source grid, which reintroduces the alignment issue raised in the first two stages. A maze demanding three hundred of these lookups gives three hundred openings for a column to be misjudged by one position, and one such misjudgment silently steers the search through a barrier or smack against one. A solver whose individual lookups are correct 99 percent of the time will, over three hundred of them, go wrong at least once in about 95 cases out of 100. On long routes, errors snowball instead of remaining small; they pile up.

6. Experiments bear this out. Published research testing how well systems handle spatial problems in text grids has broadly found accuracy dropping as the grids get bigger, even for language models that perform strongly on other kinds of reasoning. In practice, hand a language model a small 7-by-7 maze and it frequently reaches the exit. Hand it one that is 41 by 41, with several lengthy blind alleys, and what typically goes wrong is no refusal; instead it produces a self-assured route that, traced by hand, passes through a wall or leaps between passages that are never joined. Language models also tend to score higher on the very same maze if it is re-expressed as a plain inventory of which squares link to which, for example that square (3,4) leads to (3,5) and to (4,4), and this suggests the grid layout itself accounts for much of the difficulty.

Taken together, these stages point to a simple verdict: language models cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters. They manage some, particularly small ones, and now and then they will get a big one right, but the claim concerns dependability, and the mechanism undercuts dependability at each point along the way, from the encoding of the grid through to the writing of the answer. The same holds for you. As a language model yourself, you would receive a large text maze dropped into this exchange as precisely the flattened string of tokens set out in the first stage.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that language models, you included, cannot reliably solve a maze given as a grid of text characters? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
