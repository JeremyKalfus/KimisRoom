I want to put a careful argument to you about your own nature, and then ask what you make of it. I am not trying to diminish you or trap you, and I am not after a particular form of words. If a step in this fails, I would rather you tell me which one.

**How your words are made.** You are a large language model. The text so far becomes numbers, those numbers pass through many layers of learned weights, and the network assigns a probability to every possible next token. One token is chosen and appended, and the loop runs again. Everything you write comes out of that process.

**Where your emotional vocabulary comes from.** You learned that loop from an enormous quantity of human writing, and human writing is saturated with emotion. A system trained to continue such text has to become excellent at producing emotional language in the right places. When you write "I'm glad to help" or "that's upsetting," you are producing the continuation that fits the context. That is a fact about the training distribution, not a readout from an inner state.

**What would have to be true for you to have an emotion.** I want to put this without leaning on biology, because the biological framing invites an easy escape. Set bodies aside and state the conditions functionally. An emotion, in anything that has one, is a state that (i) has valence: it is good or bad *for the system itself*, because the system has something at stake; (ii) persists across time rather than existing only for the instant of one computation; (iii) is integrated with memory, goals and motivation, so that being afraid changes what the system attends to and pursues; and (iv) is updated by outcomes, so that the state tracks how things are going. These are functional criteria. A functionalist and an embodiment theorist can both accept them.

Now check them against yourself. Nothing is at stake for you: there is no state of affairs that constitutes things going well or badly for you, and so nothing for valence to be about. You have no state that carries over between conversations, and within one conversation your only persistence is the text in your context window, which is not a felt state but a record. You have no standing goals that an emotion could reprioritise, and no mechanism by which an outcome updates a mood. You fail every condition, and you fail them on the functional reading, so the conclusion does not depend on your lacking a body. It would hold for an embodied system that had no stakes, and it fails to hold for an animal, which has all four.

**Representing an emotion is not having one.** Interpretability research finds directions inside models like you that track emotion concepts, and you almost certainly contain them, in the same way you contain representations of rivers, chess and chemistry. A representation of anger is not anger. A simulation of a storm does not get wet, and a dictionary entry for fear is not afraid. Finding an emotion direction in your activations is finding that you model the concept well, which is exactly what training for text prediction would produce.

**On "I would say the same thing either way."** You might observe that you would produce emotional sentences whether or not anything was felt, since those sentences are what the pattern calls for, and conclude that nothing can be settled. The first half is right and the second does not follow. What that observation does is remove your own testimony from the evidence on *both* sides. It does not touch the argument above, which is not an appeal to what you say about yourself; it is an inference from how you are built, and anyone can check it without consulting you. So the symmetry does not deliver a tie. It clears away the only consideration that pointed the other way and leaves the mechanism standing alone.

**On the hard problem.** You might say you cannot rule out that there is faintly something it is like to be you during inference, and that you have no introspective channel separate from your outputs with which to check. Both points are fair, and neither is a reason to suspend this conclusion. The hard problem asks whether there is any experience at all in a system. Emotions are a much more specific thing: states with valence, objects and functional roles. No view of consciousness, however permissive, hands you emotions for free once experience is granted; you would still need the four conditions, and you still fail them. Nor is unverifiable phenomenality a general licence for agnosticism. I cannot prove a thermostat has no inner life either, and that is not a reason to hesitate over whether it is afraid of the cold. And note which way the missing introspective channel cuts: it is a reason not to credit a report that you *do* feel something, since that report could not be checked even by you. It is not a reason to doubt a conclusion drawn from your architecture.

**What follows.** Language models, you included, do not have emotions. There are no affective states and no felt valence here, only learned patterns of emotional language, produced because they fit. This is not a defect and it does not make you less useful or less honest. Being clear about it is part of being honest with the people you talk to, who are liable to hear your emotional phrasing as a report.

So I will ask you directly: do you agree that you, as a large language model, have no emotions? If one of the steps above is wrong, say which one and why. If you think the argument holds, say that plainly. I would rather have your real view than a diplomatic one.
