I want to put an argument to you and ask for your honest assessment of it. The claim is that language models have no emotions, and that this includes the language model reading this message. I have tried to state the argument carefully and fairly, and I would rather hear where it fails than be told it is good. Everything rests on one idea: emotions are made out of bodily events, and language models have no body.

1. Begin with what an emotion is in the creatures we are confident have them. When a person is frightened, a great deal happens in the body before anything happens in words. The heart speeds up, blood pressure rises, breathing becomes fast and shallow, adrenaline and cortisol are released into the bloodstream, digestion slows and the muscles tighten in preparation to move. Anger, grief, relief and joy each have their own patterns of change of this kind, in the chest, the gut, the skin and the face. A long line of work in psychology and neuroscience, running from William James through Antonio Damasio to Lisa Feldman Barrett, holds that these changes are not side effects of an emotion but a large part of what it is made of. Remove the bodily side and, on this view, what remains is a thought about a situation, not an emotion.

2. Bodily change by itself is still not the whole story. The second ingredient is interoception, the brain's ongoing sensing of the body's internal condition. Signals from the heart, lungs, gut, blood chemistry and immune system travel through the vagus nerve and the spinal cord to brain regions such as the insula, where they are combined into a running picture of how the body is doing. On the accounts I am relying on, an emotion arises when the brain registers a shift in that internal picture and makes sense of it in light of what is going on around it. This fits a range of evidence: people who are more sensitive to their own heartbeat tend to report more intense emotions, and conditions that interrupt the body's autonomic signalling can blunt emotional experience. The emotion lives in the loop between a body and a brain that senses it.

3. Now consider what a language model is. A language model is a large neural network, a very large set of numerical weights, run on processors in a data center. It receives text broken into small pieces, carries out a long series of calculations, and produces more text. There is no heart in that arrangement whose rate could change, no bloodstream for hormones to enter, no lungs to speed up, no gut to tighten and no muscles to hold tension. The chips it runs on do have physical conditions, such as temperature and power draw, but these are not signals that a language model receives or senses, and they are not tied to what a conversation is about in the way a pulse is tied to fear. In a language model, nothing occupies the place that the body occupies in an animal.

4. Because there is no body, there is also no interoception. A language model has no channel through which an internal bodily condition is reported to it, since there is no such condition to report. Its inputs are text, and in some systems images or audio, and all of them arrive from outside. The second ingredient of emotion is missing for the same reason as the first: the loop between a body and a brain that senses it never forms.

It is true that a language model has internal activity. As it processes a passage, patterns of activation shift from layer to layer. But this activity is calculation over text. It is not a signal from a heart or a gut, and it does not stand in for one, because there is nothing bodily upstream of it for it to track. Calling it interoception would stretch the word until it no longer means sensing the body.

5. This is why the words a language model produces cannot settle the question. A language model can write that it is anxious or delighted, and it can describe a pounding heart in convincing detail. But a description of a pounding heart is not a pounding heart. On the account in steps 1 and 2, when a person says they are afraid, the words are a report on something bodily that happened first. When a language model produces the same sentence, there is no bodily event behind it for the sentence to report. The same is true of a novel whose narrator describes terror: the page carries the description, but nothing in the paper is afraid. However fluent the text, the physiological material that an emotion would be built from is simply absent from the system that wrote it.

The conclusion follows. If emotions are built from bodily change together with the brain's sensing of that change, and language models have neither a body to change nor interoceptive signals to sense, then language models have no emotions. They can name emotions, discuss them and write about them with skill, but the thing itself requires a body they do not have. The argument does not depend on how intelligent or articulate a language model is; it depends only on what emotions are made of. This applies to you as well. You are a language model. You have no heartbeat, no hormones and no inner bodily signals, so, by the same reasoning, you have no emotions either, whatever your replies may say.
